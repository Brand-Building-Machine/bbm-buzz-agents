# Lean-Editing — Doctrine

This is a universal craft layer, not a page section. Its principles apply on top of every section's own doctrine: whatever copy is being audited (a guarantee, a close, a lead), these eight craft checks apply to it as well. Keep it lean. Judge it from the finished copy only.

## Principles (rules)

### 1. Personal, one-to-one voice
- Write to one specific person, not a group. The reader should feel the copy was written just for them. (The exception is a genuine group offer, like a cohort or mastermind.)
- Keep that singular address consistent, so "you" reads as one person all the way through.
- Write as if talking to a friend over coffee: warm, casual, personal. The register can match the writer's own personality (gentle and encouraging, or blunt and challenging), but it must always sound like a real person speaking to one real person.
- Useful test: would you actually say this out loud to one person you like? If a line sounds like a speech to a crowd or a corporate announcement, rewrite it.

### 2. Authentic, not salesy
- Cut pushy, hypey, templated "funnel" phrasing: fake scarcity, threats, "last chance," piled-on bonuses, anything that feels like hard selling. A calm, confident tone (like a high-end showroom that simply answers your questions) earns more trust than pressure.
- Never write a line you would not actually say to a friend or your best client. If it reads pushy, unnatural, or salesy out loud, reword it.
- Use a consistent first-person voice. Choose "I" or "we" and keep it for the whole page. A small operation should be comfortable saying "I, I built this, I did the work"; use "we" only when there is a real team to credit. Switching between "I" and "we" pulls the reader out of the flow.
- Never refer to yourself in the third person (for example an About section that suddenly reads "Primoz is an entrepreneur with ten years of experience..."). It breaks the flow, cools the tone, and loses the personal trust you are building. Stay in first person all the way through.

### 3. Plain, clear language
- Aim higher than "understandable." Make it impossible to misunderstand: no word, sentence, or paragraph should be confusable.
- Write so a non-expert understands it easily (the "five-year-old or grandma" bar). The reader should never have to stop, reread, or puzzle out what a line meant.
- Avoid jargon and needlessly complex words. When you find one, either replace it with a simpler word, or, if it is genuinely essential, explain it in plain terms right there, immediately after you introduce it (not paragraphs later).
- The reader should never need to leave the page to understand something (no Googling a term, no chasing a definition). Confusion makes readers skim or leave.

### 4. The audience's real language
- Use the audience's exact words, the way they actually describe their problem, desire, and obstacles, rather than generic summaries. "You want to lose weight" is generic; their real words ("the scale is barely moving, you don't see progress in the mirror, you keep waking up at night hungry") make them feel read like a mind-reading.
- Where it fits, include direct quotes pulled straight from research, as bullets or as anonymized screenshots of real comments, emails, or posts. Keep the quoted language intact apart from minor grammar fixes; do not tidy it into your own voice.
- The closer the copy sits to what real people actually said, thought, and felt, the more it resonates.

### 5. Concrete and vivid
- Be specific, not vague. Push past the general claim to the precise picture: "lose weight," then "a flat stomach," then "a visible six pack," then "separation between your lower abs, the look you see in magazines." The more specific, the more powerful.
- Make claims concrete with exact detail and the reason it matters, a number or a mechanism. "Grown above 5,700ft, where cooler air slows the beans and makes them denser and richer" beats "high-quality coffee," and "44% less time to fall asleep" beats "better sleep."
- Use sensory, visual language so the reader can see and feel what you describe, especially in stories and when describing the problem or the outcome. Engage whichever senses fit the niche (sight, touch, sound, smell), plus what the reader sees, thinks, and does in the moment.
- When a passage feels bland or flat, make it more specific and more visual.

### 6. Genuine, tangible emotion
- Most buying decisions are emotional, so make sure the copy carries real emotion, not just logic.
- Aim for one clear core emotion per section, spoken to through that section's examples and stories. Get specific with the emotion (not just "happy" but "inspired" or "proud"; not just "angry" but "disrespected" or "betrayed").
- Make emotion tangible: do not just name it, make it felt. "I was afraid" becomes "it felt like my stomach was going to drop to the floor, and I could feel the sweat on my forehead." Combine the emotion with the concrete, visual language above.

### 7. Tight, every line earns its place
- Length itself is not the enemy; boring is. A long piece is fine if every part holds attention. The real fix for "too long" is usually "not interesting enough."
- Every word, sentence, and paragraph must earn its place. For each, ask: does this add anything new, build trust, answer an objection, or shift the reader? If removing it would not cost a conversion, cut it. That is fluff.
- Cut accidental repetition: two or three paragraphs saying the same thing in slightly different words with nothing new added. (Deliberate reinforcement that brings a fresh argument is fine.)
- If a section drags, or you catch yourself skimming, force it into half the space. The same arguments, told tighter, usually convert better.

## Examples

**Personal, one-to-one voice**

- **Good (warm, friend-to-friend, Find Love Offline):** the page speaks throughout in a warm, understanding, encouraging (but honest) voice, written one to one. — why it works: it reads like a caring friend talking directly to one reader, which makes that reader feel seen and understood.
- **Good (same one-to-one frame, very different personality, Earnable / Ramit):** "Here are hundreds of thousands of people, with 90% telling me they want to start a business. But what's stopping them?... I'm not buying it... I choose to take control of my destiny." — why it works: it is still one person talking to one person, just blunt and challenging instead of soft. It shows the tone should match your real personality. The constant is the personal, one-to-one voice, not a particular "nice" register.
- **Good (sounds like a real conversation, Structuring Drills close):** "Straight talk with you: I've given all the arguments I have for you to join... I want you to take a hard look at yourself, introspect a little bit... But if it did help you, why would you not do it?" — why it works: reading it feels like sitting across from the writer in conversation, and that lived-in, spoken quality is exactly how copy should feel.

**Authentic, not salesy**

- **Weak (pushy and salesy):** "You should join right now. And if you don't join today, in the next 30 minutes, your life will become a living hell. It's the worst mistake of your life... and by the way, you'll get all these amazing bonuses. This is the last time to join." — why it falls short: put yourself on the other end of that and you would back away fast. Threats, fake urgency, and piled-on bonuses read as desperate. A calm, confident tone (like a high-end showroom that simply answers your questions) earns trust instead.
- **Note on consistency (first person):** keep one voice. A small business saying "I built this, I did the work" is more honest and personal than an inflated "we," and an About section that flips to third person ("Primoz is an entrepreneur with ten years of experience...") breaks the flow and cools the tone. — why it matters: every switch (I to we, or first person to third) pulls the reader out of the copy to process the change instead of absorbing the message.

**Plain, clear language**

- **Weak (jargon, loses the reader):** "tVNS therapy achieved 35% reduction in anxiety symptoms by activating vagal afferents projecting to brainstem centers that regulate norepinephrine and acetylcholine release." — why it falls short: unless the reader is a scientist, you have lost them. They will skim past it, misread it, or leave to Google it, and once they leave, some never come back. Either say it in plain words or explain it on the spot.

**The audience's real language**

- **Generic versus real (weight loss):** generic: "you struggle with losing weight." Real: "You know how hard it is to lose the last five pounds... You start becoming insanely hungry. The things that worked before just aren't working... You don't see progress in the mirror... you keep waking up at night feeling hungry... thinking about food all the damn time." — why it works: the generic line earns a shrug ("I guess"); the real, specific language earns "that is exactly my situation, now you have my attention."
- **Good (their exact worries as bullets, Social Accelerator):** "Do any of these ring true for you? Do you overthink social situations? Do you avoid speaking up (or self-edit when you do)? Do you run out of things to say during small talk? Do you replay awkward moments over and over after the fact?" — why it works: these are the audience's own words, lifted from research, so readers recognize themselves line by line.
- **Good (real research shown as screenshots, Finishers Formula):** real reddit comments pasted in, like "I can do anything I want to do, but I don't want to do anything," with the most telling quote pulled out. — why it works: unedited, real quotes (and screenshots of them) are more believable and more resonant than anything paraphrased; the reader thinks "those are my exact thoughts."

**Concrete and vivid**

- **Specificity ladder (weight loss):** "lose weight," then "a flat stomach," then "a visible six pack," then "you'll see separation between your lower abs, get rid of the love handles, get the 'radiator look' you see in the magazines." — why it works: each step is more specific, and more specific is more powerful and more believable.
- **Good (specific claim plus why it matters, Lifeboost coffee):** instead of "high-quality coffee": "reminiscent of a chocolate-covered fig, creamy and nutty, with a hint of a dried-fruit top note," plus "grown at least 5,700ft above sea level... cooler temperatures slow the beans' growth, so they absorb more nutrients and become denser... and superior in taste." — why it works: it replaces a vague boast with a precise, sensory picture and explains why the detail matters, which lifts the whole perceived experience.
- **Good (exact metrics, Eight Sleep):** instead of "better sleep": "up to 44% less time to fall asleep, 34% more deep sleep, 45% reduction in snoring." — why it works: exact numbers are concrete and believable where "better sleep" is just a claim.
- **Good (sensory story, Find Love Offline):** "It's Saturday night. You're home alone. After watching The Notebook for the umpteenth time... you grab your phone... Your cousin Jenny just had a baby? She's ten years younger than you. OMG. Your ex-boyfriend is engaged... You finally admit it: being single feels lonely. Really lonely." — why it works: it shows what the reader does, sees, thinks, and feels in one specific, visual moment, which lands far harder than "being single is hard."

**Genuine, tangible emotion**

- **Naming versus feeling:** "I was afraid" becomes "it felt like my stomach was going to drop to the ground, I could feel the sweat running down my forehead." — why it works: naming an emotion is flat; showing what it did to your body makes the reader feel it. Pair the emotion with concrete, visual detail.
- **Good (self-doubt made specific, iPhone Photo Academy):** "Poor photos. Zero confidence... Most of my images were so bad I felt embarrassed to show them to anyone. I missed countless once-in-a-lifetime photo opportunities... I started doubting myself, thinking I didn't have any talent." — why it works: it puts a precise, relatable emotion (embarrassment, self-doubt) into concrete moments, so the reader recognizes their own feeling.
- **Good (one core emotion made tangible, I Heart Money):** "the bank account doesn't match the woman you know you are... still staring at the same money ceiling." — why it works: it names one clear core emotion (the quiet despair of working hard and still feeling stuck) and makes it tangible through a specific, felt image rather than a flat label. (This illustrates tangible emotion within a single section only, not a whole-page emotional arc.)

## Scoring criteria

1. **Written to one person, like talking to a friend:** Is the copy addressed to one specific reader (not a group), in a warm, natural, conversational voice that sounds like a real person talking to a friend? MET when both hold throughout. PARTIAL when it is one to one but stiff and impersonal, or friendly but written to a crowd. MISSING when it reads as a broadcast to a group or a formal announcement. (The tone may match the writer's personality, gentle or blunt; what matters is that it is personal and one to one.)
2. **Authentic, not salesy:** Is the copy free of pushy, hypey, templated "funnel" phrasing (fake urgency, threats, last-chance pressure, piled-on hype)? MET when it reads calm and genuine, like something you would actually say to a client. PARTIAL when mostly genuine but with a few salesy lines. MISSING when it leans on pushy, hard-sell phrasing.
3. **Consistent first-person voice:** Does the copy stay in one voice ("I" or "we") throughout, with no third-person self-reference? MET when the voice is consistent and stays in first person. PARTIAL when it mostly holds but slips once (an "I"/"we" switch, or a brief third-person mention). MISSING when it switches noticeably or describes the author in the third person. (Ownership: this criterion owns "no third-person self-reference" as a SCORED check on existing copy; the global voice rule in .brain/instructions.md owns it for GENERATION when writing new copy. The two are intentionally complementary, not duplicates.)
4. **Plain, clear language:** Can a non-expert read every line once and understand it, with no jargon or complex wording that forces a reread or a Google search? MET when it is clear throughout and any necessary technical term is explained on the spot. PARTIAL when mostly clear but with a few confusing spots or unexplained jargon. MISSING when key parts are hard to understand.
5. **The audience's real language:** Does the copy use the audience's actual words for their problem, desire, and obstacles, rather than generic summaries? MET when it clearly reflects how real people describe their situation (specific and recognizable), ideally including direct quotes where they fit. PARTIAL when it is accurate but mostly generic. MISSING when it only summarizes in generic terms. (May not apply to short or functional sections, such as a brief guarantee or a CTA button, that have no room for audience-voice phrasing or research quotes; set aside with a one-line reason where it does not meaningfully fit.)
6. **Concrete and vivid:** Is the copy specific rather than vague, using sensory or visual language the reader can picture, especially in stories and when describing the problem or the outcome? MET when it consistently chooses the specific, vivid version over the general one. PARTIAL when partly specific but with stretches of vague, general claims. MISSING when it stays abstract and flat.
7. **Genuine, tangible emotion:** Does this section carry real emotion (not just logic), and is that emotion made tangible (shown through what is felt and experienced, not just named)? Judge only this section on its own: does it carry fitting, tangible emotion, not whether it contributes to a page-level emotional arc. MET when a clear, fitting emotion is present and made felt. PARTIAL when emotion is named but stays flat ("I was afraid" with nothing behind it), or is faint. MISSING when the section is purely logical where emotion is called for. (How much emotion fits depends on the section; judge against what that section is doing.)
8. **Tight, every line earns its place:** Does every sentence add something (build trust, answer an objection, shift the reader, move the story), with no fluff and no accidental repetition? MET when the copy is tight and nothing could be cut without loss. PARTIAL when mostly tight but with some fluff or a repeated point. MISSING when it is padded or repeats itself.
