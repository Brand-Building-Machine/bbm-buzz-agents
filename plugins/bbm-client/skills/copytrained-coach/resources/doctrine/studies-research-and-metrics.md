# Studies, Research and Metrics — Doctrine

**Scope:** Proof element, page-level and section-level. Run it on the whole page (does the page back claims with data), or on a specific claim or section to check whether a research-backable claim is actually backed by a study or metric. May need page context to know which claims should be backed.

## Principles (rules)

### Purpose
- Stack different proof types. Some readers respond to testimonials, some to a personal story, and some care most about hard data before buying. The more varied your proof, the more engaging the copy and the higher the conversions.
- Data lets you make the argument "don't take my word for it, don't even take my clients' word for it, look at what the cold, hard data says," which is convincing to the data-minded reader.

### What counts as studies, research, and metrics
- **Real scientific studies.** If you are in supplements or health, lean heavily on studies to back your claims. Even outside health, you can usually find relevant studies (breathwork studies for a breathwork workshop, long-form-content studies for a content course).
- **Client success metrics.** Survey your clients or analyze backend data and cite the rate ("91% of users said Loop improved their sleep quality," "78% report speaking more confidently in the first 3 weeks"). A specific metric beats a vague claim ("helps you sleep better").
- **Product or business metrics.** Impressive counts that build trust (users, downloads, YouTube views, star ratings, students, alumni).
- **Your own surveys.** You can run your own (even unscientific) survey of your audience and cite the result as proof of a claim you make (for example, a survey showing your audience's #1 struggle is the one your product solves).

### How to use it well
- Place a study or metric right after any claim on the page that can be backed by research. "Here is the claim, and here is the research behind it" is a flow that works.
- Link to the specific studies when you reference them, so a skeptical reader can verify (and so it does not read as invented).
- Make the data visual. A chart or graph gets more attention than text, is more believable, and acts as an extra hook point on the page.
- Use specific numbers. "94% of users" is stronger and more believable than "most users."

### How to collect it
- Set up automated check-in surveys sent 30, 60, and 90 days after purchase to gather metrics (and testimonials and case studies at the same time), or send a one-time check-in to all existing clients now. Periodically pull the metrics that prove your claims and add them to the page.

## Examples

- **Real studies with linked references (Momentous creatine):** benefit claims tied to specific percentages ("increase strength and power by up to 14%," "up to 21% faster recovery"), plus a list of the 8 credible studies and reviews behind the product. — why it works: it ties each claim to a specific researched figure and links the actual studies, which makes the claims verifiable and trustworthy.

- **Clinical results shown as charts (NeuroSym):** "35% reduction in anxious states" and "48% reduced fatigue" presented as clinical-study charts with a "see scientific evidence" link. — why it works: the findings are specific, visual, and backed by a path to the evidence.

- **A visual placebo-vs-product comparison (AG1):** two body illustrations, "diet + placebo, 71% of nutrient requirements met" vs "diet + AG1, 94% met," plus a 3-month self-perceived-efficacy study ("97% felt more energy at 1 month"). — why it works: the visual contrast makes the benefit instant and concrete, and the study adds credibility.

- **A relevant study outside health (serpIQ, for a content course):** a study of the top 10 search results showing the top posts average over 2,000 words, shown as a bar chart. — why it works: even a non-health product can find and cite a relevant study, and the chart makes the data land and doubles as a hook.

- **Client success metrics (Loop Dream Earplugs):** "91% of users said Loop Dream improved their sleep quality," "87% reported feeling focused during the day," paired with product benefits. — why it works: a specific survey metric next to each benefit is far stronger than the benefit stated alone.

- **Your own survey as proof of a claim (Body Mechanics Mastery):** "Since 2016 I've been asking animators what they need the most help with," shown as a bar chart with body mechanics at the top. — why it works: it uses the author's own survey data to prove the claim (earlier on the page) that body mechanics is the audience's biggest struggle.

- **Business and product metrics (GOWOD, Comme une Française, Brite):** "1.6M+ users, 4.9/5 rating, 300+ exercises"; "19.6M+ YouTube views, 32,000+ students"; "91% felt more focused, 85% no jitters or crash." — why it works: impressive counts and benefit percentages build trust and prove the product at scale.

- **Research plus a competitive comparison (Jumpspeak):** a retention-rate bar chart (immersion far above lectures, reading, and traditional apps) with "research proves immersion is 3.5x more effective than passive methods," plus a "78% report speaking more confidently in the first 3 weeks" metric. — why it works: it uses third-party research to prove the method is superior and adds a client metric on top.

## Scoring criteria

1. **Backs research-backable claims with studies or data:** Where the page makes claims that could be supported by research or data, are they backed (studies, client metrics, or business metrics), rather than left as bare assertions? MET when the key backable claims carry data. PARTIAL when only some do. MISSING when claims that clearly need proof have none. (product-dependent — health and supplement products especially must lean on studies; for others, judge whether relevant data is available and used.)
2. **References specific studies where relevant:** Does it reference specific, real studies where the claims call for them? MET when specific studies are cited. PARTIAL when studies are alluded to vaguely ("science shows") without specifics. Set aside where studies genuinely do not apply to the product. (product-dependent) Never invent a study.
3. **Links to the referenced studies:** Where studies are referenced, are there links (or clear references) to the actual studies on the page? MET when the studies are linked or clearly referenced. PARTIAL when mentioned with no way to verify. Set aside where no studies are referenced.
4. **Includes specific client success metrics:** Does the page include specific client success metrics (for example "94% of users experience X")? MET when specific, concrete client metrics are present. PARTIAL when results are stated only vaguely. MISSING when there are none where they could exist. (product-dependent — never invent a metric.)
5. **Includes impressive product or business metrics:** Does the page include impressive product or business metrics (users, downloads, views, ratings, students)? MET when present and concrete. PARTIAL when vague ("thousands"). MISSING when none. (product-dependent — a newer business may have few; note that this is a real proof gap to fill over time, not a copy flaw.)
6. **Presents the data visually:** Are the studies, findings, or metrics shown visually (charts, graphs, screenshots) rather than only as text? (visual — audit must ask the user)
