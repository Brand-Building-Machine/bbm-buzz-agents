# Emotion — Doctrine

**Scope:** Editing/craft, section and page. Run it on a single section (does this section carry the right core emotion, made tangible) or across the whole page (the emotional roller coaster and the ending). The per-section emotion checks are section-level; the roller coaster and the ending are page-level. A deep section audit applies the section-level checks; the page-level checks run on the whole page.

## Principles (rules)

### Why emotion
- Most buying decisions are emotional, not logical. In editing, deliberately pump up the emotion. The more your copy carries real emotion, and the more precisely it hits the emotions your audience is already feeling, the higher it converts.

### One core emotion per section
- For each section, identify the one core emotion the reader is experiencing (for a problem) or wants to experience (for the dream or the solution), and speak to it through the examples and stories you share.
- Get the emotions from research (if you asked people how something makes them feel, the underlying emotions are there) or from your own transformation (how you felt facing the problem, finding the solution, and finally solving it).
- A problem-solution lead opens on one emotion; a promise lead opens on a different one.

### Get specific with the emotion
- Do not settle for "happy," "sad," or "angry." Get specific: inspired, proud, hopeful; ashamed, lonely, betrayed, disrespected, jealous. A tool like a Feelings Wheel helps you find the precise emotion. Specific emotions are far easier to write great examples for.

### Make emotion tangible
- Do not just name the emotion, make it felt, by combining it with the visual, sensory storytelling from Powerful Language and Show Don't Tell. "I was afraid" is flat. "It felt like my stomach was going to drop to the ground, I could feel the sweat running down my forehead" makes the reader feel it.

### The emotional roller coaster (page-level)
- Across the page, alternate between positive and negative emotions. A problem brings shame; the promise of a solution brings pride; obstacles bring dread or fear; then hope that they can be overcome, and so on. As the reader rides these, they stay far more engaged than with purely logical copy.
- End on a positive note. The page usually closes on hope, reassurance, or relief, that after weeks, months, or years, the problem can finally be solved.

## Examples

- **Leading on the pain (Master Market Sizing):** "Back in 2012... I made a frustrating, costly mistake... I failed estimations in THREE DIFFERENT FIRMS that year," with the rejected cases shown. — why it works: the lead opens on a clear core emotion (regret, humiliation, frustration), which pulls the reader in emotionally rather than logically.

- **A precise negative emotion (I Heart Money):** "you've got the ambition, the work ethic, the vision, but the bank account doesn't match the woman you know you are... you're still staring at the same money ceiling." — why it works: it names one clear core emotion (the frustration and quiet despair of working hard and staying stuck) and makes it tangible through a specific, felt image.

- **Self-doubt made specific (iPhone Photo Academy):** "Poor photos. Zero confidence... most of my images were so bad I felt embarrassed to show them to anyone... I started doubting myself, thinking I didn't have any talent." — why it works: it puts a precise, relatable emotion (embarrassment, self-doubt) into concrete moments, so the reader recognizes their own feeling.

- **A full emotional roller coaster (I Heart Money):** despair ("the same money ceiling") into shame ("brilliant women over-delivering and under-earning") into hope and not-your-fault ("no one ever taught them"), into empowerment ("this is your Rich Life Era"), ending on a triumphant close ("step into the identity of the wealthy woman now"). — why it works: the page moves the reader through alternating emotions and ends on a high, which is far more engaging than a logical march.

- **A section-by-section roller coaster (iPhone Photo Academy):** injustice ("Apple won't tell you this") into pride (the disbelieving compliments) into self-doubt (the instructor's bumpy start) into calm mastery (250,000 photos, 4 million followers) into frustration (11 years of trial and error) into hope (an easier blueprint). — why it works: nearly every section switches between a positive and a negative emotion, which keeps the reader hooked throughout.

## Scoring criteria

1. **Each section carries one clear core emotion:** Does each section carry one clear, fitting core emotion, spoken to through its examples and stories, rather than reading as purely logical? MET when a clear core emotion is present and fitting for the section. PARTIAL when emotion is present but muddled or faint. MISSING when the section is purely logical where emotion is called for.
2. **The emotions are specific:** Are the emotions specific (inspired, ashamed, betrayed, hopeful) rather than generic (happy, sad, angry)? MET when the emotional language is specific. PARTIAL when it stays somewhat generic. MISSING when it names no real emotion.
3. **Emotion is made tangible:** Is the emotion made felt through visual, sensory detail (what it feels like in the body, the specific moment), rather than just named? MET when the emotion is made tangible. PARTIAL when named with only light detail behind it. MISSING when it is a flat label ("I was afraid") with nothing behind it.
4. **The page rides an emotional roller coaster:** Across the page, does the copy alternate between positive and negative emotions to keep the reader engaged, rather than holding one flat tone? MET when there is a clear alternation across the page. PARTIAL when there is some variation but it is mostly one-note. MISSING when the page is emotionally flat or purely logical throughout. (page-level)
5. **The page ends on a positive note:** Does the page end on hope, reassurance, or relief, rather than leaving the reader in the pain? MET when it closes on a clear positive note. PARTIAL when the ending is faintly positive. MISSING when it ends flat or on the low point. (page-level)
