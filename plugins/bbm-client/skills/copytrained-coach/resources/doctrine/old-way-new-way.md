# Old Way, New Way — Doctrine

## Principles (rules)

### Purpose
- Like The Reframe, Old Way / New Way promises a better solution that lines up with yours. It contrasts the old way (how the reader has been doing things) with the new way (your approach), usually presented side-by-side and visually. The old way ties to your Counter-Mechanism, the new way to your Unique Mechanism.
- It is a strong, attention-grabbing section that summarizes both the problems and the desires while tying different parts of the page together mid-way through.

### What each side covers
- The old way covers the problems, the obstacles, and the negative consequences.
- The new way covers the better outcomes, the desires, and the positive consequences.
- Pull the bullets from research, in the audience's exact language.
- Name both sides (ideally with your Counter-Mechanism and Unique Mechanism names) so the contrast is clearer.

### Match the framing to the audience
- Match the framing to where your audience actually is. For advanced buyers, "good vs great" resonates better than "beginner vs expert," which can make them feel the product is beneath them.
- You can break the contrast into multiple sub-sections if you want to expand on each difference.

### Repetition carve-out
- This is a summarizing, contrasting section. Reusing or repeating bullets from other sections is fine and expected here, because you are presenting the same ideas in a very different, side-by-side format. Do not treat that cross-section repetition as a flaw, including under the usual tightness and no-repetition checks.

### Visual
- Present it with a strong visual (Show Don't Tell): a side-by-side table, a line chart of different customer journeys, or a simple-versus-complex diagram.

### Audit dependency
- When auditing, ask whether the page has a Counter-Mechanism and a Unique Mechanism, and their names, so you can check that the old and new sides line up with them. If the user does not have them, judge the contrast on its own and say so.

## Examples

- **Old ways vs new standard (Entrepedia):** "Old ways keep you stuck" (no time or skills, hiring freelancers is slow and expensive, publishers take your profits, 6-12 month production) versus "New production standard" (done for you, full ownership and no royalties, launch in 7 days). — why it works: the old side stacks problems, obstacles, and consequences, and the new side contrasts each one directly.

- **Past failure vs the dream (Speak Business English):** "What happened in the past" (you made notes, you practiced, but the words don't come, you feel limited) versus "Now, imagine instead" (natural, spontaneous, professional, confident), transitioning to the program. — why it works: it contrasts the old failure with the dream outcome and hands the reader to the solution.

- **A reframe-style contrast (Recursion):** "What they teach you" (recursive functions call themselves, factorial, Fibonacci) versus "What you need to know" (how to find the subproblems, the base case, how to succeed under pressure in your interview). — why it works: it lists what others teach as "important" against what actually matters, positioning the product as the one that teaches the latter.

- **Named old vs named new (Time Genius):** "Traditional Productivity" (surface tactics that fade, rigid systems, doing more) versus "Time Genius Approach" (deep habit change, adapts to your rhythms, doing what matters). — why it works: it contrasts the named old way with the named new way (the Unique Mechanism), and it is fine that some bullets repeat ideas from elsewhere on the page, because the format is new.

- **Named customer journeys on a chart (Strict Pull Up):** three named trajectories on a line chart, "The Hacker," "The Obsessive," and "The WODprep Way." — why it works: naming three real ineffective approaches (and the effective one) works when research shows the audience uses several different failing paths, and the line chart shows them visually.

- **Show, don't tell (Loop School):** "What people think you need to create music" (a complex tangle of gear) versus "What you actually need" (a guitar, an amp, and one pedal). — why it works: the visual lands the contrast instantly, with almost no words.

- **Good vs great for advanced buyers (Electric Elevation):** "Novice vs Pro Guitarists," contrasting "Good" habits with "Great" ones. — why it works: for experienced buyers, contrasting good with great positions the product as the path to excellence without making them feel like beginners.

- **Expanded across sub-sections (Mental Mastery):** "5 differences between Mental Mastery and an ordinary life," each a "most people" versus "people with Mental Mastery" contrast pulled from research. — why it works: it expands the old/new contrast across multiple sub-sections, using research quotes on each.

## Scoring criteria

1. **Contrasts the old way and the new way, side-by-side:** Is there a clear side-by-side contrast between the old way and the new way? MET when the contrast is clear and balanced. PARTIAL when it is muddy or one-sided. MISSING when there is no real old-vs-new contrast.
2. **The old way covers the problems, obstacles, and negative consequences:** Does the old side capture the problems, obstacles, and negative consequences the reader knows? MET when it clearly does. PARTIAL when thin. MISSING when the old side is empty or generic.
3. **The new way covers the better outcomes, desires, and positive consequences:** Does the new side capture the better outcomes, desires, and positive consequences? MET when it clearly does. PARTIAL when thin. MISSING when the new side is empty or generic.
4. **Bullets pulled from research, in the audience's exact language:** Do both sides use the audience's real words? MET when specific and recognizable. PARTIAL when accurate but generic. MISSING when it only summarizes in your own words.
5. **Names the old way and the new way:** Does it name both sides, ideally with your Counter-Mechanism and Unique Mechanism names, so the contrast is clearer? MET when both sides are clearly named. PARTIAL when only one is, or the naming is vague. MISSING when neither side is named.
6. **Matches the framing to the audience:** Does the framing fit where the audience actually is (for example, "good vs great" for advanced buyers rather than "beginner vs expert")? MET when the framing fits the audience's level. PARTIAL when it is slightly off. MISSING when it talks down to, or over the head of, the audience.
7. **Presented with a strong visual (Show Don't Tell):** Is the contrast presented with a strong visual (a side-by-side table, a chart, a simple-versus-complex diagram)? (visual — audit must ask the user)
