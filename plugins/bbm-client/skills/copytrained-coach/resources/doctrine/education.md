# Education — Doctrine

## Principles (rules)

### Purpose
- Before you sell, educate. A strong education section builds trust (especially with cold readers), positions you as the expert, and moves the reader closer to buying by showing them why they have not solved the problem on their own. Done well, it does a lot of the selling for you.

### Pick ONE format
- There are five common formats: common mistakes, myths, limiting beliefs, obstacles, and secrets. Use ONE (in most cases, one is enough). Pick the format that best fits your product, your narrative, and how you see the world.
- To find your angle, ask why your readers are not getting results, and dig (in your mind or your research): is it something they do not know (secrets), something they were taught wrong (myths), a belief that holds them back (limiting beliefs), something they get stuck on (obstacles), or a mistake they repeat (mistakes)?
- When auditing, stay aware of all five formats. Score the format the copy actually uses, and if that format seems poorly matched to the product or to the audience's real blocker (for example, framing something as a "secret" when the real blocker is a limiting belief, or as a "mistake" that blames the reader when it is really an industry myth), gently point that out and suggest the better-fitting format and why.

### The four things every format covers
- The what: name exactly what is holding them back.
- The why: explain why it is happening (the background and context).
- The explanation: break it down as an expert, so they see what you see and know what you know.
- The proof: do not just say it, prove it (personal stories, examples, screenshots). The stronger the proof, the better this section converts.

### Craft
- Each point gets its own smaller headline, often a quote pulled straight from research (a myth or belief in the audience's own words: "I guess I'm not motivated enough").
- Use soft framing ("you may have tried this") rather than accusing framing ("you're making these mistakes"). You do not want to assume the reader has made every mistake.
- Pull everything from research, in the audience's exact language.
- Transition smoothly toward the solution. If you name obstacles you do not resolve here, the product resolves them later (that later payoff lives in another section and is not scored here).

## Examples

**Common mistakes**
- **Named mistake (Social Accelerator):** the "DIY Paradox," the mistake smart people make thinking they can figure social confidence out alone, pouring over random articles and videos that feel productive but get them nowhere ("still stuck, wondering what's wrong with you"). — why it works: it names the mistake as a memorable counter-mechanism, explains what it is, why they do it (they're smart), and the negative consequence.
- **Soft framing (Fear of Falling):** "Three fear solutions that further ingrain your fear... you may have tried one (or all) of these." — why it works: it reframes the reader's coping habits as "solutions" that backfire, uses soft framing so it does not accuse, and breaks down the what, the why, and the consequence.

**Myths**
- **Myths that hold you back (Analytics Academy):** "The 3 Big Myths of case math," each with its own sub-headline, laying out what candidates think versus what is actually true. — why it works: a clean "3 myths holding you back" frame that meets readers where they are, then corrects each with what they actually need.
- **Proof that disarms the objection (In-Person Styling):** "Looking good is a skill" and "your body type doesn't matter as much as you think," with before/after photos of the same body type. — why it works: the before/afters of the same body type prove the point rather than just asserting it.
- **Proving the myth is real, then resolving it (Level Up Your Ear):** "You either have a 'good' ear or you don't," leading with a real New Scientist headline as proof the myth exists, then a "FACT" resolution (a good ear is learned, not born). — why it works: it proves the myth is real with a credible screenshot, teases hard ("this is poison"), then resolves it with a reframe.

**Limiting beliefs**
- **Belief / invisible script / truth (How To Talk To Anybody):** "3 WTF beliefs that were holding me back," each broken into what they believed, the invisible script running underneath, and the truth. — why it works: it reframes the real blocker from tactics to beliefs, and the three-part structure exposes what is really going on behind each one.
- **Proving the belief false with testimonials (No Stress Negotiation):** "Invisible script: I can't negotiate," backed by real testimonials of people who held that belief and still negotiated big raises. — why it works: instead of just saying the belief is false, it proves it with people who broke through it.

**Obstacles**
- **Named, then solved next (The Decision Maker):** "Which mental hurdles are holding you back," with client quotes, resolved in the following section. — why it works: it names the specific obstacles in the audience's own words, then follows the arc of "these are the obstacles, here's how I solved them, here's how you can too."
- **Obstacles as bad habits (Ship 30):** "3 killers of great writing," bad habits called out on the left and explained on the right. — why it works: it frames obstacles as "bad habits," acknowledges them as common, then positions the product to fix them.

**Secrets**
- **The relief of "just two things" (Fluent in 3 Months):** "The secret of why the method works," revealing just two things (speak with natives, and stick with it). — why it works: the "secret" headline plus "you only need two things" gives relief, frames the two as genuinely hard, and positions the product as the way to actually do them.
- **Secrets as "keys" (Climbing Technique):** "The three keys to unlocking harder grades." — why it works: it frames the secrets as keys to the desired outcome and explains what each requires and how the product delivers it.

## Scoring criteria

*Numbering is stable across revisions: a missing number is a criterion deliberately removed, not an error. Judge only the criteria listed.*

2. **Shows why they have not succeeded on their own:** Does it make clear why the reader has not solved the problem by themselves so far? MET when the reason is clear. PARTIAL when only hinted at. MISSING when it never addresses it.
3. **Commits to one clear, well-fitting format:** Does it commit to one clear education format (mistakes, myths, beliefs, obstacles, or secrets), rather than blurring several? MET when it clearly uses one format well. PARTIAL when the format is muddled, or when the chosen format is a poor fit for the product or the audience's real blocker. Judge the section on the format it uses; do not penalize it for not using the others. If the format seems ill-suited, gently name the better-fitting format and why (this is guidance, not a heavy penalty).
4. **Covers the what:** Does it clearly name what is holding the reader back? MET when named clearly. PARTIAL when vague. MISSING when never named.
5. **Covers the why:** Does it explain why this is happening (the background and context)? MET when the why is clear. PARTIAL when thin. MISSING when absent.
6. **Explains it like an expert:** Does it break the point down as an expert would, so the reader sees what you see? MET when the explanation is clear and expert. PARTIAL when surface-level. MISSING when it just states the point with no explanation.
7. **Backs it with proof:** Does it back the point with proof (personal stories, examples, screenshots), rather than just asserting it? MET when there is real, relevant proof. PARTIAL when proof is thin. Set aside only if the point genuinely needs no proof. (product-dependent — and where the proof is a screenshot, confirm it with the user, since it is visual.)
8. **Pulled from research, in the audience's exact language:** Does it use the audience's real words for what holds them back? MET when specific and recognizable. PARTIAL when accurate but generic. MISSING when it only summarizes in your own words.
9. **Uses soft framing:** (violation check) Does it use soft framing ("you may have tried this") rather than accusing framing ("you're making these mistakes")? MET when the framing is soft and does not assume. PARTIAL when it slips into accusation once or twice. MISSING when it accuses the reader throughout.
10. **Each point gets its own smaller headline:** Does each point get its own smaller headline, ideally a research quote in the audience's own words? MET when each point is clearly headlined. PARTIAL when only some are. MISSING when it is one undifferentiated block.
11. **Transitions smoothly toward the solution:** Does it end by moving the reader toward the solution? MET when the transition is smooth. PARTIAL when abrupt. MISSING when it ends flat. (Set aside only if this section is genuinely the end of the page.)
