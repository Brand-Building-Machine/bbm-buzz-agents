# Hook Points — Doctrine

**Scope:** Editing/craft, primarily page-level (hook points are spread across the whole page, especially the first part before the product introduction). Run it across the whole page. A deep section audit can check whether a given section carries or sets up a hook, but the count and the building-interest arc are judged at the page level.

## Principles (rules)

### The whole page is a chain of hooks
- The first part of a page is the hook: the headline exists to get the subheadline read, the subheadline to get the first sentence read, the first sentence to get the second, and so on. It is all about getting people to start reading and keep reading.
- The mistake many make is thinking the hooks end there. They do not. Think of every new section as a separate hook. A strong opening does not mean the reader's attention is yours for the next fifty pages. You have to keep earning it. If you confuse them, you lose them; if you bore them, you lose them.
- Without hook points throughout, interest keeps fading. The goal is to reverse that, so interest keeps rising until the reader is so engaged they just want to buy.

### You already have some
- If you followed the section guide, you already have hook points sprinkled in: fascination bullets in the product section, and strong subheadlines throughout. The job in editing is to go through the first part of the page, especially before the product introduction, and add more where they are missing.

### How to add hook points: pieces of the puzzle
- Give the reader pieces of the puzzle. Hint at what is coming later without giving it away: "there's a solution to that which I discovered, and I'll share it in a bit," or "that ended up not being the core issue at all, there was a bigger underlying problem, and I'll tell you all about it."
- Lines like these generate fresh interest ("I want to find out what that is") and keep the reader going. Slowly build the puzzle in front of them, so they keep getting new revelations while seeing that more pieces are still missing. You can close an open loop in the very next section or much further down.
- Generally, the longer the page, the more hook points it needs.

### Do not overdo it
- Once you add hook points, the page must still flow smoothly, so do not overdo it. If you have none, add just one, two, or three strong ones, one at a time, checking the flow still works and the transitions stay smooth.
- Aim for five to ten hook points across the page, each building on the last, so interest keeps climbing. Done well, by the time the reader reaches the product section they are already thinking "I don't care what the price is, I just want to buy this."

## Examples

- **Hinting at a coming revelation:** "I'll tell you about those a little bit further down this page," and "Now before we share the 'secret' that makes this method so effective..." — why they work: each opens a loop the reader wants closed, so they keep reading to find the answer.

- **A story hook that opens a loop:** "I wish I could tell you it was just bad luck... That the interviewers had a bad day... But I can't. It wasn't bad luck." — why it works: it sets up a mystery (then what was it?) that pulls the reader forward.

- **A curiosity hook mid-page:** "There is something sinister at play...," "It wasn't until I was 19 or so that I saw what was possible with a really good ear," "What's a reverse makeover?" — why they work: each hints at something intriguing coming, without revealing it, which re-grabs attention deeper into the page.

- **A reframe hook:** "But I've got news for you: that struggle you're stuck in? It's not your fault." — why it works: it promises a surprising reframe, so the reader keeps going to find out why.

## Scoring criteria

1. **Hook points throughout, not just the opening:** Does the page carry hook points beyond the opening, especially through the first part before the product introduction, so attention keeps getting re-earned? MET when hook points are spread through the page. PARTIAL when there are only a couple, or they cluster at the start. MISSING when there are none past the opening.
2. **Uses open loops (pieces of the puzzle):** Do the hook points open loops that hint at what is coming without giving it away, rather than flatly stating everything? MET when it clearly uses open loops. PARTIAL when hints are present but weak or fully revealed. MISSING when nothing is teased forward.
3. **Hook points keep the flow smooth:** Do the hook points read naturally and keep the transitions smooth, rather than feeling forced or breaking the flow? MET when they integrate smoothly. PARTIAL when one or two feel forced. MISSING when they disrupt the flow.
4. **Enough hook points, building on each other:** Are there roughly five to ten hook points across the page, each building on the last so interest keeps rising? MET when there is a healthy number that build. PARTIAL when there are only a few. MISSING when there are effectively none. (Scale to page length: a longer page needs more.)
