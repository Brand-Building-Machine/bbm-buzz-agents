# Show, Don't Tell — Doctrine

**Scope:** Editing/craft, section and page. Run it on a single section (does this section show rather than tell) or across the whole page (are visuals used throughout). It is one of the editing elements a deep section audit should apply, and it also runs at the page level. Because it is entirely about visuals, its criteria are visual and need the user to confirm what is actually on the page.

## Principles (rules)

### The principle
- A picture is worth a thousand words, and the same is true in copy. Whenever you can show something instead of just telling it, do it. Visuals throughout the page are one of the most powerful ways to raise conversions.
- On short pages (especially e-commerce and physical products), visuals let you keep the copy lean while still communicating clearly. On long pages, visuals keep it engaging instead of a wall of text.

### Why visuals matter (the jobs they do)
- **Hook points.** Visuals grab and hold attention. A well-chosen image re-grabs a skimmer and keeps an engaged reader immersed.
- **Data made clear.** A study, statistic, or research finding is far easier to grasp as a graph or infographic than as text.
- **Frameworks made simple.** A framework or concept that is hard to describe in words is easy to grasp as a diagram (a flywheel, an acronym graphic, a model diagram).
- **Proof.** An image or screenshot makes people believe what you describe actually happened (email screenshots, revenue dashboards, overnight-sales notifications).
- **Stronger storytelling.** A real photo from the story (what you looked like when you were overweight, or awkward) immerses the reader far deeper than description alone.
- **Humor.** Funny images, memes, or gifs build the story and add likability.

### Editing method (not scored)
- This is the stage where you resolve every "TODO: image" and "TODO: photo" placeholder from the first draft, adding all the visuals in one pass.
- While you do, look for more places to add visuals: to explain frameworks, to show data, to strengthen storytelling, or to add proof. The end result should be a highly visual, engaging page.

## Examples

- **Data visualized (Mitopure, NuroSym):** a clinical result shown as a bar or line chart ("mitochondrial renewal +39% over placebo"; "18% increase in HRV vs placebo"). — why it works: the finding lands instantly as a chart, and the reader stops to take it in.

- **Research data visualized (Earnable):** the audience's own survey data shown as a pie chart ("Do you want to start a business? Yes 90%") and a bar chart of their reasons. — why it works: it turns research into an engaging, credible visual that also acts as a hook point.

- **A framework shown as a diagram (Product Flywheel, BASB "CODE," ACT Immersion):** a flywheel, an acronym graphic (Capture, Organize, Distill, Express), or a hexagon model. — why it works: a simple diagram captures attention and explains a system far better than a paragraph, and readers stop to read it.

- **Proof through screenshots (Level Up Your Ear, Master Market Sizing):** screenshots of real emails ("I get emails like these all the time"), or an anonymized email proving a claim. — why it works: the screenshot makes the claim believable and makes the page more engaging.

- **Proof through a photo (Earnable):** a phone showing a list of overnight order notifications, captioned "these are actual sales from my business that came in while I was sleeping." — why it works: it shows the result instead of asserting it.

- **Storytelling through a photo (How to Talk to Anybody, iPhone Photo Academy):** a self-deprecating photo ("Yes, this guy has a show on Netflix"), or a before/after ("from regular shots like this, to incredible images like this"). — why it works: the photo immerses the reader in the story and proves the transformation visually.

- **Humor through images (Level Up Your Ear, Body Mechanics Mastery):** a stern drummer photo ("You don't want this look from your drummer"), or a person tossed in a dumpster next to "I felt like an imposter... they were going to toss me in the dumpster." — why it works: a playful, on-point image adds humor and likability while carrying the story.

## Scoring criteria

1. **Visuals throughout as hook points:** Are visuals sprinkled throughout the page to grab and hold attention and keep readers engaged (rather than long walls of text)? (visual — audit must ask the user)
2. **Visuals strengthen storytelling:** Are photos or images used to immerse the reader in the stories (the before state, the moment being described)? (visual — audit must ask the user)
3. **Images or screenshots used as proof:** Are images or screenshots used to back up key claims (emails, dashboards, results, sales notifications)? (visual — audit must ask the user)
4. **Data and research visualized:** Are studies, statistics, or research findings shown as graphs or infographics rather than only text? (visual — audit must ask the user) (product-dependent — set aside where the page cites no data)
5. **Frameworks explained with a diagram:** Are frameworks or hard-to-describe concepts shown as a diagram or graphic? (visual — audit must ask the user) (product-dependent — set aside where the product has no framework to visualize)
6. **Humor through images where it fits:** Does the page use any funny images, memes, or gifs to add humor and likability? (visual — audit must ask the user) (bonus — if met it can raise the score; being missing does NOT count against the score)
