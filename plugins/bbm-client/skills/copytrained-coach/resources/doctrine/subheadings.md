# Subheadings — Doctrine

**Scope:** Editing/craft, primarily page-level (subheadings work as a set across the whole page), and section-level (each section's own subheadings). Run it across the whole page. A deep section audit can check a section's subheading, but the skim test and the direct/indirect mix are judged across the whole page.

## Principles (rules)

### What subheadings do
- Subheadings are the mini-headlines at the start of each section and inside longer sections. Do one dedicated editing pass on them. They play three roles:
  - **They define the overall flow.** The set of subheadings is the skeleton of the page, and a great tool for reverse-engineering the flow (of your page, or of pages you study).
  - **They guide and reel in skimmers.** Some readers read word for word, others skim. Strong subheadings grab the skimmers and pull them into the copy.
  - **They keep readers engaged.** Like hook points, they break up long text and introduce new, intriguing ideas. Make them research-based where relevant and curiosity-inducing.

### The three types
- **Direct subheadings:** clear, descriptive, and direct. Use these from the product introduction onward. By then the reader is engaged and wants a clear idea of the product, so an indirect subheading here would only reduce clarity ("How X works," "Here's what you'll learn," "Frequently Asked Questions").
- **Indirect (transitional) subheadings:** curiosity-inducing and not immediately clear. Read in a vacuum, you could not tell what the page is even about. Use them to open or break up longer Body sections, and in the Close. Their job is to keep engagement and curiosity high ("And this didn't just happen to me...," "Could it get any worse?," "The good news: it doesn't have to be this way").
- **Semi-direct subheadings:** the in-between, and the workhorse for most of the Body. They include the audience's exact problems, desires, thoughts, emotions, or quotes, or name a Unique Mechanism or Counter-Mechanism, or open a loop. Specific enough that the topic is clear, but not as plainly descriptive as the product-introduction ones ("No one teaches you how to answer estimation questions properly").

### How to strengthen each subheading
- **Make them benefit-driven** where relevant: answer the silent "so what," explaining why something matters in the subheading itself.
- **Open loops:** name a specific Unique Mechanism or Counter-Mechanism to spark curiosity.
- **Use the audience's exact language:** pull it straight from research and paste it in.
- **Infuse emotion:** speak to the audience's specific emotions where relevant.

### Technical rules
- Start every section with a subheading.
- Add a mid-section subheading whenever a block of text runs long (roughly every 200 to 300 words), so a skimmer does not lose the thread.
- Run the skim test: if you read only the subheadings, you should still get a clear idea of the page's whole story and narrative.
- Prioritize clarity over cleverness. Indirect subheadings can be clever, but never confusing. If a reader might have to reread a subheading, rewrite it.
- Keep most Body subheadings curiosity-inducing rather than revealing: pull the reader in with the subheading, and reveal the answer in the copy itself.

## Examples

- **Direct subheadings (Master Market Sizing, product introduction onward):** "How Master Market Sizing works," "Here's what you'll learn as soon as you join," "Try Master Market Sizing for 30 days, 100% risk-free!," "Frequently Asked Questions." — why they work: they are simple and clear, so an engaged reader instantly knows where to look for how the product works, the guarantee, and the answers.

- **Indirect / transitional subheadings (Master Market Sizing, opening and Body):** "And this didn't just happen to me...," "Could it get any worse?," "The good news: it doesn't have to be this way," "I had an unfair advantage...," "Beware: Hidden risks don't hide forever." — why they work: read alone you cannot tell what the page is about, which is the point; they open loops and keep curiosity high to pull the reader through.

- **Semi-direct subheadings (Master Market Sizing, Body):** "I underestimated estimation and market sizing questions" (the audience's own pattern), "Why do candidates get rejected in estimations so often?" (an intriguing question), "No one teaches you how to answer estimation questions properly" (the responsibility shift). — why they work: they are specific enough that the topic is clear and tie to research, while still carrying curiosity, which is the right mix for the Body.

- **The skim test in action (Master Market Sizing):** reading only the subheadings, from the headline through the lead, the fatal problems, the reframe, the development story, the unique mechanism, the product, and the close, still tells the whole story. — why it works: it proves the page is skimmer-friendly and the flow is sound.

## Scoring criteria

1. **Every section starts with a subheading, and long blocks are broken up:** Does every section start with a subheading, with a mid-section subheading added wherever a block runs long (~200 to 300 words)? MET when sections are consistently headlined and long blocks are broken up. PARTIAL when some sections or long blocks lack one. MISSING when the page is largely undifferentiated text.
2. **Passes the skim test:** Reading only the subheadings, do they still tell the page's whole story and narrative? MET when the subheadings alone convey the story. PARTIAL when they give a rough sense but miss key parts. MISSING when the subheadings alone do not tell the story.
3. **Body subheadings are mostly semi-direct:** Are most Body subheadings semi-direct (research-based, speaking to the reader, with some curiosity), rather than fully generic or fully cryptic? MET when the Body uses this mix well. PARTIAL when they lean too generic or too vague. MISSING when they are bland labels with no curiosity or audience voice.
4. **Body subheadings pull the reader in (curiosity over revealing):** Are most Body subheadings curiosity-inducing rather than revealing the answer, so they pull the reader into the copy? MET when they mostly tease and let the copy deliver. PARTIAL when many give away the point. MISSING when they are flat and reveal everything.
5. **Direct subheadings from the product introduction onward:** From the product introduction on, are the subheadings direct and clearly descriptive (so an engaged reader gets clarity)? MET when the second half uses clear, direct subheadings. PARTIAL when some stay needlessly indirect there. MISSING when the product half is confusingly cryptic.
6. **Benefit-driven where relevant:** Do the subheadings answer the silent "so what" where relevant, rather than only labeling? MET when key subheadings carry a benefit or reason to care. PARTIAL when only some do. MISSING when they are bare labels.
7. **Open loops naming the mechanism:** Do some subheadings open loops by naming the Unique Mechanism or Counter-Mechanism? MET when the mechanism names are used to spark curiosity. PARTIAL when hinted at. Set aside where the page has no named mechanism.
8. **Use the audience's exact language:** Do the subheadings pull the audience's exact language (problems, desires, thoughts, quotes) from research where relevant? MET when they clearly use the audience's real words. PARTIAL when accurate but generic. MISSING when they never reflect the audience's voice.
9. **Infuse emotion where relevant:** Do the subheadings carry the audience's specific emotions where relevant? MET when emotion is woven in where it fits. PARTIAL when faint. (A soft, upside-leaning criterion.)
10. **Clear over clever:** Are the subheadings clear rather than confusing, with nothing a reader would have to reread (even the indirect, clever ones)? MET when all are clear on one read. PARTIAL when one or two make the reader pause. MISSING when several are cryptic or confusing.

## Reporting a subheadings audit (attach this on top of the report)

Before the prose, pull the raw material so the reader can see it:

1. **List every subheading on the page, in order,** exactly as written. This doubles as the skim test: read the list alone and check whether it tells the whole story.
2. **Color-code each subheading:** 🟢 strong (clear and pulls the reader, or the right kind for its place on the page), 🟡 weak (a bland label, gives the point away where it should tease, generic, or carries hype), 🔴 poor (confusing, or a section that is missing one where it needs it).
3. **Give specific, per-subheading feedback on the 🟡 and 🔴 ones:** name the subheading, say what is weak, and show a better direction (a short "closer to X" pointer, not finished copy). Leave the 🟢 ones unremarked or with a word.

Then give the normal report and the band and Copy Score (out of 100).
