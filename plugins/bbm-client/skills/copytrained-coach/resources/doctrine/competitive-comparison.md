# Competitive Comparison — Doctrine

## Principles (rules)

### Purpose
- When you are in a highly competitive space, believe your product is clearly superior, and are comfortable positioning against competitors, a competitive comparison can help. It compares your product's features or metrics directly against other products.
- This is an optional, situational section, and it is especially strong for e-commerce and physical products.

### Use it with care
- Do not use it if you do not want to burn bridges in your industry. If you do use it, consider not naming competitors directly (unnamed competitor columns or icons work fine).
- Be accurate and fair. A false or unfair comparison that a reader can disprove will backfire and cost you trust. Never fabricate competitor data.

### How to build it
- Compare your product's features (checkmarks) or metrics against competitors or common alternatives, in a clear side-by-side.
- Make your advantage obvious on the dimensions that matter to the buyer.
- Where it helps, add supporting text explaining why each difference matters (stronger than checkmarks alone), specific metrics or prices, and even your company mission or values.
- Use an attention-grabbing graphic; this section lives or dies on a clear, scannable visual.

## Examples

- **A one-stop-solution table (Fit Mom For Life):** a table comparing the program against Weight Watchers, Nutrisystem, personal training, and more, across features and price. — why it works: a simple table positions the product as the one-stop solution on the dimensions that matter.

- **Metric-based, doubles as price justification (AppointWise):** versus human setters, with metrics (conversion 10-20% versus 30-40%, $2,000/month versus $297/month). — why it works: a metric-based comparison proves the advantage with numbers, and doubles as price justification.

- **Comparing without naming names (Holo):** versus unnamed competitor columns, "save $400/month," with a checkmark table. — why it works: it compares clearly without naming competitors, which avoids burning bridges.

- **Specific numbers in a crowded market (OliRoli):** price per 100 sheets and features versus named brands, plus a photo. — why it works: in a crowded market, specific numbers and a visual make the comparison land, and running several comparisons reinforces it.

- **Comparison plus the "why" and the mission (Truvani, Laird Superfood):** a checkmark table plus supporting text explaining why each difference matters, the company mission, and an ingredient-label photo. — why it works: the supporting text and mission build trust alongside the comparison, which is stronger than checkmarks alone.

## Scoring criteria

*Numbering is stable across revisions: a missing number is a criterion deliberately removed, not an error. Judge only the criteria listed.*

1. **The comparison is framed by an attention-grabbing headline:** Does the headline give the reader a reason to read the comparison rather than merely labelling it? MET when it frames the comparison around what the reader is weighing. PARTIAL when it is a plain label. MISSING when the table arrives with no framing at all.
2. **Compares features or metrics side-by-side:** Does it compare your product's features or metrics against competitors or alternatives in a clear side-by-side? MET when the side-by-side is clear. PARTIAL when it is muddled. MISSING when there is no real comparison.
3. **Makes your advantage clear:** Does your product clearly win on the dimensions that matter to the buyer? MET when the advantage is obvious. PARTIAL when it is mixed or unclear. MISSING when the comparison does not favor you.
4. **Explains why you're superior, not just checkmarks:** Does it add supporting text explaining why the differences matter? MET when there is supporting text. PARTIAL when it is a checkmark table with no explanation (which can still work, but is weaker). MISSING only if the comparison is unreadable.
5. **Uses accurate, fair, specific data:** (violation check) Is the comparison specific and fair, with real metrics or prices and accurate competitor claims? MET when it is specific and fair. PARTIAL when it is vague. MISSING when it makes false or unfair claims a reader could disprove (which backfires). Never fabricate competitor data.
6. **Includes an attention-grabbing comparison graphic:** Is the comparison presented as an attention-grabbing, scannable graphic? (visual — audit must ask the user)
7. **Includes the company mission or values:** Does it include your company mission or values alongside the comparison? (bonus — a nice trust-building touch, as in Truvani; absence does NOT count against it)
