# Qualifying — Doctrine

## Principles (rules)

### Purpose
- By this point the reader knows the product, and they are wondering "is this right for me?" The Qualifying section answers that. It does two jobs: it calls out your ideal client groups and confirms the product is perfect for them, and it turns away the wrong-fit clients to avoid bad reviews, refunds, and difficult clients. This is a must-have section.

### Who it's for (the positive part)
- Use a simple headline ("Who is [product] for?", "Is [product] right for you?").
- Call out the major audiences (or audience sub-groups), and speak to the specific desires and challenges of each. A welcome survey to new clients is the best way to learn what these groups are and what they want.
- There are a few shapes: several distinct audiences, one audience split into sub-groups by where they are on the journey, or (when there are no distinct groups) the strongest challenges and desires from your research.

### Who it's not for (the conditional part)
- If you have refunds, bad-fit clients, or difficult clients, add a "who it's not for" part. Turn away the audiences the product does not work for, the client types that consistently refund, and the client types who make difficult clients. Briefly say the product is not for everyone, and why.
- Turning the wrong people away also reassures the right people that the program is serious and not full of dabblers. Common patterns to filter out: people not willing to do the work, people chasing a "magic pill" or quick fix, and (for advanced products) beginners.
- Use a positive-only section if you genuinely have near-zero refunds and bad-fit clients.

### Craft
- Do not overthink this section. It matters more that it is on the page and hits the right audiences than that the wording is perfect.

## Examples

- **Several distinct audiences (Digital Launchpad):** "Who is Digital Launchpad for?", then beginners, professionals, entrepreneurs, and business owners, each with a benefit tailored to them. — why it works: a reliable headline, and each audience gets a benefit that fits where they are.

- **Sub-groups of one audience (Ship 30):** "Quiz: Is Ship 30 for you?", speaking to three sub-groups by journey stage (overwhelmed non-starters, stuck writers publishing into the void, idea-rich people who never hit publish). — why it works: even one audience can be split into sub-groups with different challenges, so more readers see themselves.

- **Challenges and desires, no distinct groups (Unignorable):** "Is UNIGNORABLE right for you?", one audience with a list of challenges and desires, ending on "if you relate to any of these, it's for you." — why it works: when there are no distinct groups, speak to the strongest challenges and desires from research, and reassure that relating to a few is enough.

- **Turning away wrong-fit beginners, with a next step (Clients Come to Me):** "not a good fit if you don't have any clients yet, we recommend you wait until you've figured out your offer." — why it works: it turns away the wrong fit and tells them what to do first, so they can come back later.

- **Filtering out quick-fix seekers (Social Accelerator):** "you should skip if you aren't ready to do the work, want a magic pill, or are under 21 or unemployed." — why it works: it filters out work-averse and quick-fix clients, which protects results and reduces refunds.

- **Turning the wrong fit away reassures the right fit (Podcast Marketing Academy):** "Who's not a fit?", beginners, dabblers, quick-fixers, outsourcers. — why it works: turning the wrong people away also tells serious clients the program is not full of dabblers.

## Scoring criteria

*Numbering is stable across revisions: a missing number is a criterion deliberately removed, not an error. Judge only the criteria listed.*

2. **Calls out the major audiences:** Does it call out the major audiences (or audience sub-groups) the product is right for? MET when the key audiences are clearly called out. PARTIAL when only one broad audience is named where there are really several. MISSING when no audience is called out.
3. **Speaks to each audience's real desires and challenges:** Does it speak to the specific desires and challenges of each audience, pulled from real research? MET when each audience gets specific, recognizable desires or challenges. PARTIAL when it is generic. MISSING when it just names audiences with no substance.
4. **Turns away the wrong-fit clients where needed:** Does it include a "who it's not for" part that filters out the audiences the product does not work for, refund-prone clients, and difficult clients (with a brief "it's not for everyone, and here's why")? MET when it turns the right people away clearly. PARTIAL when it gestures at it weakly. Set aside when the seller genuinely has near-zero refunds and bad-fit clients and chooses a positive-only section. (product-dependent)
