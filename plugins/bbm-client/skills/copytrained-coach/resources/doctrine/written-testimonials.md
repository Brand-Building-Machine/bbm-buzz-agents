# Written Testimonials — Doctrine

## Principles (rules)

### Purpose
- Testimonials are the simplest proof element. Even if you have no product testimonials yet, use testimonials from past clients about working with you (they prove you are trustworthy and can help, even before they prove the product). These can speak to your personal qualities (committed, responsive, great feedback). This is a must-have section.

### Strength
- Quality beats quantity: three strong testimonials beat twenty weak ones. Curate the best up front, and add a "testimonial flood" later for the rest (even the weaker ones).
- Avoid short, generic testimonials ("Primoz is great, I recommend him!"). A generic testimonial only earns its place when it comes from a major authority figure in your field, whose name alone builds trust.
- Make each testimonial stronger with: a name (named beats anonymous), a full name (beats a first name), a photo (beats no photo), and specific details (a company name, a role, or a telling detail like "mother of two").
- Give your strongest testimonials more space and a bigger font.
- Raw screenshots (from texts, emails, or your community) are engaging and trustworthy.

### Integrity (the hard rule)
- Raw beats over-polished. Real testimonials with imperfect spelling or a little rambling read as more trustworthy than polished, uniform ones. It is fine to trim a section; never reword what a client said, and never fabricate a testimonial. When writing copy, always pull from your real testimonials.
- The audit assesses the strength, specificity, and presentation of the testimonials shown. It cannot verify authenticity, so it does not score that. It also does not rewrite testimonials.

## Examples

- **Named, specific, results-carrying (Super Spicy Media):** three testimonials with company names, roles, and specific results ("helped us boost website visits from social media by 230%"). — why it works: named, specific, and result-carrying testimonials from real businesses read as credible.

- **Generic, but from an authority (Selena Soo, via Marie Forleo):** "a powerhouse of publicity and marketing genius... I cannot recommend her highly enough." — why it works: the testimonial itself is not specific, but it comes from a major authority figure whose name builds instant trust. This is the one case where a generic testimonial earns its place.

- **One strong extended story (Earnable):** "My side hustle idea went from paying my rent to supporting a team," then the full arc (no experience, a subscription product, a full-time business, employees, a sold company). — why it works: one strong, detailed testimonial beats twenty weak ones, and giving it space lets the story land.

- **Telling details (Astra AI):** "a student," "a high-school student," "mother of Igor and Viktor." — why it works: small, specific details make testimonials more believable than a bare first name.

- **A punchy one-liner, given space (Breathwrk):** "One minute of this is like 20 minutes of meditation," Eben Britton, former NFL linebacker, in a big font. — why it works: a strong short testimonial deserves emphasis and room to breathe.

- **Raw screenshots (Baby Sleep Code):** unedited text-message screenshots from parents. — why it works: raw, unedited screenshots read as more trustworthy than polished, designed testimonials.

## Scoring criteria

1. **Has enough written testimonials:** Are there enough written testimonials (aim for at least 5)? MET when there are several. PARTIAL when only one or two. MISSING when there are none.
2. **Testimonials are strong and specific:** Are the testimonials strong and specific, rather than short and generic? MET when they are specific and substantive. PARTIAL when it is a mix. MISSING when they are mostly generic. (A generic testimonial only earns its place when it comes from a major authority figure.)
3. **Covers each core target audience:** Is there at least one testimonial for each core target audience? MET when each audience is represented. PARTIAL when only the main one is. Set aside for a single-audience product; if audiences exist but are not all covered, flag it as a gap. (product-dependent)
4. **Carries specific details:** Do the testimonials carry specific details (ideally full names, plus a company, role, or telling detail)? MET when the details are specific. PARTIAL when it is mostly first names or thin. MISSING when they are anonymous and detail-free.
5. **Best testimonials curated up front:** Are the strongest testimonials curated up front and given emphasis, with a testimonial flood later for the rest? MET when the best are led with and emphasized. PARTIAL when they are an undifferentiated wall. MISSING when only weak ones are shown.
6. **Reads raw and authentic:** Do the testimonials read raw and authentic, rather than reworded or over-polished? MET when they read genuine and un-massaged. PARTIAL when over-designed but still credible. (When writing copy, never reword or fabricate a testimonial; trimming is fine, rewording is not.)
7. **Includes photos of the clients:** Do the testimonials include photos of the clients? (visual — audit must ask the user)
