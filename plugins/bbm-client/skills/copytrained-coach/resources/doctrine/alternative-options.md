# Alternative Options — Doctrine

## Principles (rules)

### Purpose
- By this point the reader is sold that they must solve the problem, and they may still think "I could just do this myself, or use a cheaper route, and save the money." The Alternative Options section pre-addresses that before the reader ever reaches your product section.
- It covers the typical alternatives your audience could use to get the same result, and honestly analyzes each one's drawbacks. This is a must-have section.

### How to do it well
- Cover the real alternatives your audience actually considers (do it yourself, YouTube, books, hiring someone, other methods).
- For each, genuinely acknowledge its appeal before you reveal the drawbacks. If you skip the appeal and only attack, you lose credibility.
- Explain the specific drawbacks of each (why it falls short), and back it with proof where you can (explanations, examples, screenshots).
- Address the "can't I just do this myself?" objection head-on.
- Position your product as the best option without a hard "you must buy or else" push.

### Be honest, or it backfires
- Be critical, honest, and realistic. Do not dismiss alternatives your readers know actually work. If you claim something is useless and the reader knows better, you lose them. Acknowledging that a good coach (or another route) genuinely can work actually makes the section more trustworthy.

### Placement
- Place it before the Product Introduction, or in the Close (as the final "can't I just do this myself?" objection before the invitation).

## Examples

- **Appeal, then drawback, with proof (Level Up Your Ear, "The shortcuts that actually kill your ability to play by ear"):** tab sites (appeal: so much easier than by ear; drawback: many are inaccurate, shown with a bad-tab screenshot), YouTube tutorials (appeal: you see and hear it; drawback: the same accuracy issues, hands cover the fretboard, and the song might not exist), and asking others (appeal: a good teacher helps; drawback: they cannot teach you every song forever, shown with real reader emails). — why it works: it names each real alternative, genuinely acknowledges its appeal, then explains the specific drawback with proof, and frames the shortcuts as making the problem worse.

- **Short and efficient (Dynamic Programming):** Google or YouTube (the same basic problems, Fibonacci again), more practice problems (a lot of time for little transfer), and books (4,500+ results, graduate-level theory). — why it works: a quick, efficient version that still names each option and its specific drawback.

- **Each tactic acknowledged, then undercut (Ultimate Course Launch):** "just do a webinar" (it is a skill of its own, it took me 20 tries to make my first sale), "just send emails" (copywriters get paid 5-6 figures for this), "just run ads" (most people lose money), "just build a funnel" (it needs all of the above). — why it works: it acknowledges each tactic's appeal, then names the specific reason it falls short, before introducing the product.

- **Honest, soft close (Master Market Sizing, in the Close):** keep traditional materials (they feel safe, but most who use them get no offers), hire a coach (a genuinely good option, but risky and very expensive), ignore it (the worst option), or join us (risk-free). — why it works: it is honest (it admits hiring a good coach genuinely works), it does not push a hard "you must buy or else," and it softly positions the product as the best option, which makes it more trustworthy.

## Scoring criteria

1. **Covers the typical alternatives:** Does it cover the real alternatives the audience actually considers (do it yourself, YouTube, books, hiring someone, other methods)? MET when it names the real alternatives. PARTIAL when it covers only one, or misses obvious ones. MISSING when it names no real alternatives.
2. **Genuinely acknowledges each alternative's appeal first:** Does it honestly grant each alternative's appeal before revealing the drawbacks? MET when the appeal is granted first. PARTIAL when some are attacked without acknowledging appeal. MISSING when it only attacks.
3. **Explains the specific drawbacks of each:** Does it explain the specific reason each alternative falls short? MET when the drawbacks are specific and clear. PARTIAL when vague. MISSING when it asserts they are bad without saying why.
4. **Backs it with proof:** Does it back the drawbacks with proof (explanations, examples, screenshots)? MET when there is real, relevant proof. PARTIAL when thin. Set aside where the drawback is self-evident. (product-dependent — where the proof is a screenshot, confirm it with the user.)
5. **Addresses "can't I just do this myself?" head-on:** Does it address the do-it-yourself objection directly? MET when it takes it on head-on. PARTIAL when it only brushes past it. MISSING when it never addresses it.
6. **Positions the product as the best option without a hard push:** (violation check) Does it position your product as the best option without a hard "you must buy or else" push? MET when it lands as a confident, soft invitation. PARTIAL when it leans pushy. MISSING when it is a hard "buy or else," or never positions the product at all.
7. **Honest and realistic:** (violation check) Is it honest and realistic, without dismissing alternatives the audience knows actually work? MET when it stays credible and honest. PARTIAL when it overstates a drawback. MISSING when it dismisses an alternative the audience knows works (which backfires).
