# Expert Endorsements — Doctrine

**Scope:** Proof element, page-level and section-level. Run it on the whole page (does the page use expert endorsements) or on a specific endorsement block. Optional and product-dependent: most products have no expert endorsements, and that is not a flaw, so treat absence as an upside opportunity to suggest, not a gap to penalize.

**Overlap note:** This overlaps Client Logos, Names, and Photos. The line: this element is a formal endorsement quote (or partnership) from a well-known expert who vouches for your product; the client element is logos, names, and photos of clients who use it. A well-known expert's photo plus quote can serve both, so credit it under each and do not double-penalize.

## Principles (rules)

### Purpose
- A natural step beyond having well-known client names and testimonials is to create win-win partnerships with recognized experts, where they endorse (and often regularly promote) your product. Use those endorsements across the page.
- An endorsement from a credible expert borrows their authority to your product, which is especially powerful for science-based or health products.

### What a strong endorsement includes
- The expert's quote, their name, and a clear persona description (their credentials, titles, or role), so the endorsement carries weight.
- A professional photo and clean design.
- For a less-known expert, describe who they are and their achievements, so an unfamiliar reader still trusts the endorsement.
- A frame that presents the endorsements well: a headline ("Trusted by Dr. X," "Recommended by experts around the world") and, where you have several, persona descriptions under each. A spread of experts across different categories can make the proof feel more achievable and relatable.

### How to get them (guidance, not scored)
- If you hear that someone well-known uses and loves your product, ask them for a quick quote for your website. Even a short endorsement adds real credibility.

## Examples

- **A named expert endorsement with credentials (AG1, Dr. Andrew Huberman):** a quote about the product, attributed to "Dr. Andrew Huberman, Neuroscientist, Host of the Huberman Lab Podcast, AG1 Partner," with a photo. — why it works: the quote is specific and the persona description makes the endorsement authoritative and trustworthy.

- **An endorsement used as a headline (AG1):** "Trusted by Dr. Andrew Huberman," used as a homepage headline over a Huberman quote and his credentials. — why it works: when the endorser is credible enough, the endorsement itself becomes the headline and hook.

- **Multiple experts across categories (AG1):** a headline ("Trusted by the world's top athletes and experts"), then testimonials grouped by category (endurance, alpine, rugby, parenting), each with the person's role. — why it works: the spread across categories, plus persona descriptions and design, gives it credibility even when the reader has not heard of every expert, and it feels achievable for an everyday business.

## Scoring criteria

1. **Uses expert endorsements where available:** Does the page use endorsements from recognized experts? MET when a real expert endorsement is present. PARTIAL when an endorsement is present but weak or unattributed. Set aside when the product genuinely has no expert endorsements (not a flaw; suggest reaching out to any well-known users). Never fabricate one. (product-dependent)
2. **Describes who lesser-known experts are:** For any expert the reader may not recognize, does the copy describe who they are (credentials, titles, achievements)? MET when unfamiliar experts carry a clear persona description. PARTIAL when only some do. MISSING when experts are named with no context. (product-dependent — judged only where endorsements exist)
3. **Each endorsement has a quote, name, credentials, and photo:** Does each endorsement carry the expert's quote, name, persona description, and a photo? MET when these are present. PARTIAL when the photo or credentials are missing. (visual — audit must ask the user, for the photo)
