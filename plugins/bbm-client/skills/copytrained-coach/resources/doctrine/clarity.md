# Clarity — Doctrine

**Scope:** Editing/craft, section and page. Run it on a single section (is this section clear) or across the whole page. It is one of the editing elements a deep section audit should apply, and it also runs at the page level.

## Principles (rules)

### The bar
- Copy should not just be possible to understand. It should be impossible to misunderstand. That is a much higher bar than "understandable," and it is the one to aim for.
- The opposite of clarity is confusion. Every time a reader gets confused and has to reread a line, you risk losing their attention, which leads to skimming or, worst case, closing the page. Crystal-clear copy keeps the reader engaged, flows better, and converts better.

### The main cause of confusion: complex language and jargon
- The most common cause of confusion is unnecessarily complex language or jargon. The best copy is very easy to read and uses very simple language, the "a five-year-old or your grandma could understand it" bar.
- You are the expert, so words that are natural to you can confuse your audience. When a reader hits a word they do not understand, one of three things happens, and all are bad: they ignore it and miss the meaning, they misinterpret it and get the wrong idea, or they leave the page to Google it.

### Never make the reader leave the page
- Every time a reader leaves the page (to Google a claim, look up a study, or look up a word), you lose some of them. Some open a tab, then another, and never come back. Even losing 5% is a dent in conversions.
- So the rule is: the reader should never have to leave the page. This also applies to links. Do not put external clickable links in the copy that take people away. The one job of the page is that they read it and buy, with no clicking elsewhere.

### The two fixes for a confusing word
- Reword it. Replace the complex or jargon word with a simpler one (a thesaurus helps).
- Or, if the word is genuinely important and cannot be replaced, explain it right there in the copy, immediately after you introduce it (a sentence, a few sentences, or a short paragraph), not ten paragraphs later.

### How to check (editing method, not scored)
- Read the copy yourself. There should never be a point where you stop to think about what you just read or have to reread it. If there is, mark it and rewrite it.
- Have one to three other people read it. Ask them to flag anything they do not understand, and watch whether they stop or reread, which signals confusion. The ideal outcome is that every sentence makes sense and flows, with zero confusion.

## Examples

- **Weak (jargon that loses the reader):** "tVNS therapy achieved 35% reduction in anxiety symptoms by activating vagal afferents projecting to brainstem centers that regulate norepinephrine and acetylcholine release." — why it falls short: unless the reader is a scientist, you have lost them. They will skim past it, misread it, or leave to Google it. Either say it in plain words or explain the term on the spot.

- **The fix (reword or explain on the spot):** replace the jargon with plain language ("calms the part of your nervous system that controls stress, and studies show a 35% drop in anxiety"), or, if a technical term must stay, define it in the next sentence. — why it works: the reader understands it in one pass and never has to leave the page.

## Scoring criteria

1. **Simple, plain language a non-expert gets in one read:** Is the copy written in simple language a non-expert understands on the first read (the five-year-old or grandma bar)? MET when it is consistently plain and easy. PARTIAL when mostly plain with a few dense or complex spots. MISSING when it reads as technical or hard for the intended reader.
2. **Free of unexplained jargon and needlessly complex words:** Is the copy free of jargon or complex words the reader would have to Google, or are such words replaced with simpler ones? MET when there is no unexplained jargon. PARTIAL when there are a few unexplained terms. MISSING when jargon is frequent.
3. **Any unavoidable technical term is explained on the spot:** Where a technical term genuinely must stay, is it explained immediately after it is introduced (not paragraphs later)? MET when every necessary term is explained right there. PARTIAL when some are explained late or thinly. MISSING when necessary terms are dropped with no explanation. Set aside if the copy contains no technical terms.
4. **Impossible to misunderstand, no confusing lines:** Is every line clear enough that the reader never has to stop, think hard, or reread, so the copy is impossible to misunderstand rather than merely possible to understand? MET when there are no confusing or ambiguous lines. PARTIAL when a few lines make the reader pause. MISSING when parts are genuinely hard to follow.
5. **No external links that take the reader off the page:** (violation check) Is the copy free of external clickable links that would take the reader away from the page? MET when there are none. PARTIAL when there is an occasional off-page link. MISSING when the copy routinely sends the reader elsewhere. (visual — audit must ask the user, since links may be design elements not visible in the copy text)
