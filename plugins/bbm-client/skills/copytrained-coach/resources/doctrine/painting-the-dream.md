# Painting the Dream — Doctrine

## Principles (rules)

### Purpose and placement
- This is the desired-outcome section, the polar opposite of Twisting the Knife: it makes the reader feel how good life gets once the problem is solved.
- Flow depends on the lead. In a Problem-Solution lead, a common order is Twist the Knife, then the Solution, then Paint the Dream (sometimes Twist, then Dream, then Solution; both work). In a Promise lead, the Dream is usually an extension of the lead, followed by the Twist (the contrast of where they want to be versus where they are). With other lead types, the flow is flexible.

### What to call out
- Call out the biggest, most common desired outcome(s) your product delivers, strongest first (most common to least common).
- Pull the desires from research, in the audience's exact words, rather than your summaries.
- Cover the positive consequences: the benefits that follow the outcome, plus the additional outcomes that solving the problem unlocks. Find these with the "so what?" question (their team gets proactive, so what? fewer pings, so what? peace of mind, so what? real time off), or from research ("if you solved this, how would your life change?").
- Pinpoint the core emotion (often the deepest one, like peace of mind or freedom) and write to it, not just to logic.

### How to open and paint it
- Open with a dream hook that sets up the promise: "Imagine...", "What if you could...", "Picture...", "How great would it be if...", "I'll show you...". There are no hard rules; the headline just needs to set up the promise.
- Paint a specific, real-life scenario the reader can step into, rather than vague outcomes. A concrete "Monday morning, you wake up at 9:15 with no alarm" or "you open an email and a new client writes 'consider me a customer for life'" lands far harder than "you'll have more freedom."
- Escalate the dream. Build outcome on outcome to a peak (the client is thrilled, then wants to be a customer for life, then refers their friends, then "you're living your dream," then freedom, purpose, income).
- Position the product as a vehicle to a better life, rather than only the promised result. You learn a language SO you can travel, re-ignite relationships, and live your dreams. The outcome is the doorway, not the destination.

### Proof and transition
- Real audience quotes shown as screenshots, or a resonant visual (a phone showing overnight sales notifications), make the dream more believable.
- End with a smooth transition into the next section (often the solution or the mechanism).

## Examples

- **"What if you could..." bullets into the solution (Social Accelerator):** "What if you could... feel more confident in social situations? Never run out of things to say during small talk? Rebound quickly from awkward moments?" then a transition to the program. — why it works: it uses a simple dream hook, research-driven desire bullets with emotion (confidence), and hands the reader to the solution.

- **"Results you can expect, starting day one" (Time Genius):** "Escape the Time Stress Trap... 2-5X your creative output: get your most important work done faster, and STILL have energy left to create, play, and spend quality time with loved ones." — why it works: framed as concrete results starting immediately, it names very specific outcomes, pre-addresses objections (no burnout), gives real-life situations ("flip your focus switch and get into deep work even when you don't feel like it"), and stacks positive consequences ("habits that make success inevitable").

- **Concise, for an intellectual audience (BASB Foundation):** "The Building a Second Brain Promise: you'll beat information overload... organize your digital life... create a digital environment that promotes flow, clarity, and peace of mind." — why it works: it is concise and not over the top, which fits an intellectual audience, and still carries positive consequences, with "peace of mind" as the core emotion promised.

- **"Imagine if..." with a specific scenario (Level 11 Ignite):** "Imagine if your team could run more than the day-to-day... Picture yourself logging off at 2PM knowing projects are moving forward and your team is handling things with confidence." then bullets ("take real time off... build leaders, not just doers, so your business can scale"). — why it works: a clean "Imagine" hook, a specific dream scenario (logging off at 2PM), the solution to the problems woven in, and clear positive consequences (build leaders SO your business scales).

- **Product as a vehicle (Fluent in 3 Months):** four boxes, "Travel the World," "Embrace Your Passion for Learning," "Re-ignite Relationships," "Live Your Dreams," each with specific outcomes. — why it works: it positions learning a language as the vehicle rather than the end result. You learn the language SO you can live abroad, impress friends, and find the overseas job you dreamed of.

- **"Imagine / Picture" with emotion and a transition (Fear of Falling):** "How would you climb if fear wasn't a factor?... Picture yourself at the crag, eyeing the route you've always wanted to climb... imagine that falling feels like jumping into a bed of feathers... Imagine the freedom, the exhilaration, the pure joy." then a transition to the common advice that does not work. — why it works: the headline sets up the whole dream, it moves through specific experiences and emotions (freedom, exhilaration, joy), contrasts dread with delight, and transitions smoothly.

- **The dream via a real quote, addressing an objection (Truvani):** "'I feel like I'm cheating! It's like my favorite candy without all the junk.'... The taste is so amazing you'll look at the label twice... 'How does it taste LIKE DESSERT?'" — why it works: it paints the dream (a protein that tastes like dessert) through a real quote, and quietly disarms the core objection (can a protein with no artificial sweeteners actually taste good?) with specificity and proof rather than a bare "it's delicious."

- **Escalating dream with a personal tone (The Decision Maker):** "How great would it feel to be your own boss and finally be in charge of your life? Imagine it's a couple of months from now... an email from a new client... 'Consider me a customer for life,' they write. And they told their friends too... You're living your dream. The freedom. The sense of purpose. The extra income. It feels incredible." — why it works: it starts with a promise plus an emotional follow-up, drops into a specific escalating scenario, and turns "business success" into "you're living your dream" with a cascade of positive consequences.

- **Specific scenario plus proof (Earnable):** "What if your life was different? Imagine a different kind of Monday morning. You wake up at 9:15am, no alarm... You've received 13 orders while you were sleeping. $6,500 in total. You've built an asset that pays you to live." with a screenshot of overnight order notifications. — why it works: a dream hook, a vivid specific scenario, a concrete desired outcome ($6,500 earned in your sleep), the positive consequence (an asset that pays you to live), and a screenshot that makes the dream believable.

## Scoring criteria

1. **Calls out the biggest, most common desired outcomes, strongest first:** Does the section name the biggest, most common outcomes the product delivers, leading with the strongest? MET when it clearly names the core desires and leads with the strongest. PARTIAL when it raises minor or vague desires, or buries the strongest. MISSING when it names no clear, relevant desire.
2. **Pulled from research, in the audience's exact language:** Do the desires read as the audience's actual words, rather than your summaries? MET when specific and recognizable (ideally verbatim). PARTIAL when accurate but generic. MISSING when it only summarizes in your own words.
3. **Opens with a dream hook that sets up the promise:** Does it open with a hook that sets up the dream ("Imagine...", "What if you could...", "Picture...")? MET when the hook clearly sets up the promise. PARTIAL when the opener is flat. MISSING when there is no dream setup.
4. **Paints a specific, real-life scenario, not vague outcomes:** Does it drop the reader into a concrete, real-life scene they can step into, rather than listing vague benefits? MET when there is a specific, vivid scenario. PARTIAL when partly specific but leaning vague. MISSING when it stays abstract and general.
5. **Covers the positive consequences:** Does it cover the benefits that follow the outcome plus the additional outcomes it unlocks (the "so what?" chain)? MET when the downstream benefits are clear and laddered. PARTIAL when only the surface outcome is stated. MISSING when there are no positive consequences.
6. **Escalates the dream:** (the one job) Does it build outcome on outcome to a peak, rather than staying flat? MET when it clearly escalates. PARTIAL when it builds a little. MISSING when it is a flat list.
7. **Positions the product as a vehicle to a better life:** Does it frame the outcome as the doorway to a bigger life, rather than only the end result? MET when the product is clearly a vehicle to a larger dream. PARTIAL when hinted at. MISSING when the outcome is treated as the whole story. (product-dependent — set aside for products where the result truly is the end goal, such as a taste-based or purely functional product.)
8. **Pinpoints the core emotion and writes emotionally:** Does the section carry real emotion tied to one core feeling? MET when the core emotion is clear and felt. PARTIAL when named but flat. MISSING when purely logical.
9. **Real audience quotes or a resonant visual:** Does it use real audience quotes as screenshots, or a resonant visual (a proof screenshot, a dream image)? (visual — audit must ask the user) (bonus — if met it can raise the score; being missing does NOT count against the score)
10. **Transitions smoothly into the next section:** Does it end by handing the reader to the next section? MET when the forward transition is smooth. PARTIAL when abrupt. MISSING when it ends flat. (Set aside only if this section is genuinely the end of the page.)
