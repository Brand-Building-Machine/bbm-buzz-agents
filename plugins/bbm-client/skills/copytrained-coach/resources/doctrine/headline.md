# Headline — Doctrine

## Principles (rules)

### Purpose and the one job
- The headline is the single most important line on the page. Far more people read it than read the body, so if it fails, most of the work below it is wasted.
- The headline has ONE job: get the reader to keep reading the subheadline. Nothing more.
- Do not try to sell the product, name the product, or be ultra-creative in the headline. Just hook the reader, spark enough interest, and reassure them they are in the right place.
- Do not make the headline too sensational or too-good-to-be-true. If it feels unbelievable, readers read it as scammy and can close the page immediately. Strong but credible beats hypey.
- There is no single formula. The principles below are a menu of moves. Follow as many as make sense for the specific headline; not all apply every time.

### Two core angles: lead with a desire OR a problem
A headline usually leads with one of two angles. Pick the one that fits where the audience is.
- **Benefit angle (lead with a desire):** put the biggest, most common desire of the audience up front. This is the strongest default in most cases.
  - You can keep it to a single line and single idea, or expand it by adding the vehicle (the mechanism for how they get there) and by pre-addressing a big, obvious objection.
  - Add the mechanism when the promise itself is no longer new to the market. A named or unique mechanism captures attention from an audience that has seen the plain promise before.
  - If you are the first to make a promise (an unsaturated market), the plain promise alone is enough; you do not need extra elements.
  - If you have several desires the audience shares, you can fold them into one headline, but make sure the single biggest desire is highlighted.
- **Problem angle (lead with a problem):** find the biggest, most burning problem of the audience and spotlight it. Use this when the market is problem-aware.
  - These headlines do not need to be creative. If 8 of 10 prospects name the same problem, simply spotlighting that exact problem is often enough.
  - Problem-led headlines often work well written as a question, and frequently pair the problem with the solution just beneath.

### Powerful, specific, visual language
- The more specific and the more visual the headline, the more powerful it becomes. Turn a vague claim ("learn a language," "be more productive") into a concrete, visual picture ("confident conversations in weeks instead of years," "get your bed back").
- Mine customer research for specific, visual words and phrases the audience actually uses; lift headline ideas straight from there.
- The headline just needs to be less vague; it does not need to carry every specific or be maximally detailed.

### The eyebrow: call out your audience
- The eyebrow is the small line of copy just above the headline. Its usual job is to call out the ideal audience, so readers instantly feel they are in the right place, without crowding the headline itself.
- Almost always call out the audience in the eyebrow, unless doing so genuinely hurts how the copy opens or flows.
- You can call out more than one audience, and you can add a specific desire to the call-out. You can also speak directly to the reader ("Hey there, ambitious lady, yeah you…").
- Less commonly, you can name the audience inside the headline itself, but only when the headline stays uncrowded (and ideally pairs the audience with a curiosity hook).

### Curiosity
- Try to open a curiosity gap in the headline. Opening a loop the reader feels compelled to close is one of the best hooks.
- Ways to open curiosity: a unique named method or framework, a "secret" or "key," positioning against conventional wisdom (what people usually do that does not work), or an intriguing question.
- Curiosity only works when it is relevant to something the reader already cares about. A mysterious but seemingly irrelevant headline makes the reader feel they are in the wrong place and leave.
- The answer to a curiosity question must not be obvious, or the page becomes a let-down. It has to be something surprising.

### Clarity over cleverness
- Never sacrifice clarity for cleverness. Clever headlines tend to confuse, and confusion this early loses readers.
- The reader must understand the headline in a single glance, with no rereading. If they stop and think "wait, what does that mean?", you have lost some of them.
- When in doubt, choose clear over clever. A simple, direct, promise-driven headline that works beats a clever one that puzzles.
- Test for this: show the headline to 5 people; if they ask "what do you mean?", it is too clever.

### Uniqueness and market saturation
- To grab attention, the headline should feel like something the audience has not seen before. Copied, stale headlines do not trigger interest or emotion.
- Unique is not the same as clever; you can be unique without being clever.
- How unique it must be scales with market saturation. In an uncrowded market, a simple, direct, clear headline is unique enough. In a crowded market, you must differentiate so you do not sound like every competitor.

### Emotion
- If the audience's problem is genuinely emotional, try to infuse that core emotion straight into the headline. Real emotion (especially pulled from customer research) is powerful.
- Only use an emotion that is genuinely there and fits. Do not force or fabricate one. It is perfectly fine to have no emotion in the headline as long as emotion shows up later in the lead.

### Process and craft
- Study swipe headlines for ideas, but do not copy them word for word; a headline the audience recognizes will not hook them.
- Write many headlines (aim for around 100) before choosing, and let ideas marinate over a few days. Narrow to your best 5 to 10.
- Test headlines in the real world before publishing: share a few with 5 to 10 honest people in the target audience, watch for genuine curiosity and emotional investment, or A/B test if you have the traffic. Testing the headline usually gives the biggest return of any part of the page.

## Examples

**Benefit angle (lead with a desire)**

- **Good (single idea, plain promise, Ship 30 for 30):** "Start writing online in 30 days" — why it works: one headline, one idea, nothing fancy; it simply captures what the audience already wants. Enough on its own in a market where the plain promise still feels fresh.
- **Good (desire plus vehicle plus mechanism, UNIGNORABLE):** "Build an UNIGNORABLE personal brand (that makes clients chase you)" — why it works: the core is the desire ("clients chase you," a visual way to say "more clients"), while it also names the vehicle (building a personal brand) and a unique mechanism (becoming UNIGNORABLE), which is what captures an audience that has seen the plain promise before.
- **Good (desire plus how plus objection, Employee to Entrepreneur):** "Turn Your Skills and Experience Into a Coaching Business That Can Replace Your 9-to-5 Income... Without Quitting Your Job First" — why it works: the core desire is replacing your 9-to-5 income; it adds how you get there (turning skills into a coaching business) and pre-addresses the obvious objection (without quitting first), which makes it more specific and stronger.
- **Good (stacked desires, one highlighted, Time Genius):** "Work Less. Make More. Get the Freedom You Desire." — why it works: it looks like three desires, but working less and making more both ladder into the one highlighted desire (freedom). Fold in multiple desires only when the single biggest one stays the star.

**Problem angle (lead with a problem)**

- **Good (burning problem, audience language, Texting AI):** "Are you sick and tired of texting that goes nowhere?" — why it works: it spotlights a top problem in the dating audience's own words ("texting that goes nowhere"). Not creative, just an exact hit on a common pain.
- **Good (common problem as a question, Pillars of Productivity):** "Why does your to-do list keep growing, no matter how hard you work?" — why it works: it simply nails a problem the audience feels daily. Problem headlines do not need to be clever.
- **Good (problem as a question, The Content Lab):** "You know content is important. So why does it always get pushed to the bottom of the list?" — why it works: it names a problem the audience already agrees with and opens the loop of why it keeps happening.
- **Good (problem plus solution, question format, Find Love Offline):** "Question: Why haven't you found love?" above "How to Find Love Without Apps" — why it works: it pairs the burning problem (stated as a question) with the solution just beneath. Problem-led headlines often work best in question form.

**Powerful, specific, visual language**

- **Good (vague made specific, Fluent in 3 Months):** "Have Confident Conversations In Weeks Instead Of Years" — why it works: it turns something vague ("learn a language," "become a confident speaker") into a specific, visual promise you immediately understand and want. (The product name itself, Fluent in 3 Months, is also a highly visual promise.)
- **Good (simple but visual, Moving on From Cosleeping):** "Ready to Get Your Bed Back?" — why it works: super simple, yet specific and visual; it nails the core desire of parents who want their kids sleeping in their own beds.
- **Good (creative, vivid, Inbox Hero):** "Emails are your most powerful tool for getting your audience to pant, drool, and joyfully fork over fat cash for everything you sell. Here's how you can INSTANTLY sharpen yours." — why it works: vivid, sensory language ("pant, drool, and joyfully fork over fat cash") differentiates in a crowded copywriting market where "write high-converting emails" would fall flat.

**The eyebrow: call out your audience**

- **Good (audience in the eyebrow, Clients by Design):** "For creative freelancers + agency owners" above "The exact system to go from inconsistent revenue to a constant flow of high-ticket clients" — why it works: the eyebrow names exactly who the page is for, reassuring the right readers without crowding the headline.
- **Good (multiple audiences plus desire, Rich Relationships Mastery):** "For entrepreneurs, leaders and changemakers who want to spend their precious time and energy where it matters most…" above the headline — why it works: it calls out three audiences and attaches a specific desire, all in the eyebrow.
- **Good (speaks directly to the reader, I Heart Money):** "Hey there, ambitious lady, yeah you…" above "Ready to Transform Your Money Mindset and Become Your Most Abundant Self?" — why it works: same audience call-out principle, delivered as a direct, personal address, which can land well.
- **Good (audience inside the headline, BASB foundation):** "6 Questions Every Knowledge Worker, Creator And Life-Long Learner Must Answer" — why it works: it names the audience inside the headline without overcrowding it, and pairs them with a secret (the 6 questions) that adds curiosity.

**Curiosity**

- **Good (against conventional wisdom, Mental Mastery):** "Forget about the hacks, the morning routines, the random tactics" — why it works: it contradicts conventional wisdom (you need a morning routine), so the reader wonders "well, if that's not the answer, then what is?" and keeps reading.
- **Good (secret plus specific outcome, Bulletproof Mental Math):** "The secret to have bulletproof mental math in just a few days" — why it works: it ties a "secret" to a specific, desirable outcome.
- **Good (foolproof method hook, Decision Maker):** "If there was a foolproof, step-by-step method for building a business you knew was going to succeed…" above "Would you finally have the confidence to go all-in and become your own boss?" — why it works: it makes the reader ask "what IS this method?" while tying straight to the outcome they want.
- **Good (curiosity plus injustice, Success Triggers):** "Why are some people 10x, or even 100x, happier, more confident, and more successful than others? What do they know that you don't?" — why it works: it ties to desired outcomes, triggers curiosity, and adds a sting of injustice (what do they know that you don't?).

**Clarity over cleverness**

- **Good (clear and direct, Speak Business English Confidently):** "Speak Business English Confidently" — why it works: a simple, promise-driven headline that is instantly clear. Not the most exciting, but clear beats clever versions like "Mean Business," "Fluent in Success," or "Say It Like You Own the Room," which leave the reader asking "what does that mean?"
- **Good (clear and direct, RP Hypertrophy App):** "Build More Muscle. Get Jacked." — why it works: straightforward and instantly understood, especially for its audience. Clever alternatives like "Sculpt the Statue Within," "Iron Sharpens Iron," or "Gains, Reframed" fall flatter because they trade clarity for cleverness.

**Uniqueness and market saturation**

- **Good (simple is enough, uncrowded market, Effortless Forehand):** "How To Hit Forehands With Effortless Power And Finally Start Dominating The Rallies" — why it works: online tennis courses are not saturated, so a simple, direct, clear headline is unique enough.
- **Good (simple is enough, uncrowded market, Fear of Falling):** "The proven step-by-step system to overcome your fear of falling" — why it works: in an uncrowded market, a plain headline that solves the problem works fine.
- **Good (more creative, crowded market, Launch Hero):** "Email Copywriting Course for launch sequences that kick ass (and make bank)" — why it works: in a crowded market you need more than "email copywriting course to increase conversions," so it adds voice and specificity to stand out.
- **Good (fresh angle, crowded market, Hume Band):** "The wearable designed to extend your lifespan and detect early illness" — why it works: in a fierce wearables market, it says something competitors have not, which differentiates it.

**Emotion**

- **Good (raw emotion from research, Finisher's Formula):** "Does this sound like you? 'I'm this close to giving up on myself'" — why it works: it leads with a powerful, genuine emotion taken straight from customer research, so the reader feels instantly seen.
- **Good (leads with hope, Fit Mom For Life):** "There's hope for moms who've spent years dieting, cutting out carbs, eating bland, starving themselves, obsessing over the cheat day and doing hours of sweaty and boring cardio without seeing results." — why it works: it opens on a real, core emotion (hope) for an audience worn down by failed attempts.
- **Good (emotion behind settling, I Heart My Life):** "Stop Settling. Create a Life You Genuinely Love." — why it works: it taps the underlying emotion of settling and pairs it with the desire. Use an emotion like this only when it is genuinely there; never force one.

## Scoring criteria

1. **Hooks the reader to keep reading (the one job):** Taking the eyebrow and headline together, does it generate enough interest to make the reader want to continue reading, and reassure them they are in the right place? This is the holistic test the headline exists to pass. MET when it clearly hooks and fits what the reader cares about. PARTIAL when it is serviceable but flat or generic. MISSING when it gives no real reason to read on or feels off-topic for the reader.
2. **Believable, not too-good-to-be-true:** (violation check) Is the headline strong yet credible, rather than so sensational it reads as scammy? MET when the promise is compelling and believable. PARTIAL when it edges into hype. MISSING when it makes an unbelievable claim likely to make readers bounce.
3. **Benefit angle leads with the biggest desire:** If the headline is benefit-led, does it lead with the single biggest, most common desire of the audience (optionally adding the vehicle/mechanism or pre-addressing a big objection)? MET when the audience's biggest desire is front and center. PARTIAL when it names only a minor or vague desire, or buries the main one. Judge this only when the headline takes a benefit angle; set it aside when the headline instead leads with a problem. Criteria 3 and 4 are a matched pair: score the one matching the headline's angle, set the other aside; for a headline that blends both, judge whichever is primary. (product-dependent)
4. **Problem angle spotlights the biggest problem:** If the headline is problem-led, does it spotlight the single biggest, most burning problem of the audience, ideally in their own language and often as a question? MET when it nails the audience's top problem. PARTIAL when it raises a real but secondary problem, or states it vaguely. Judge this only when the headline takes a problem angle; set it aside when the headline instead leads with a desire. (Matched pair with criterion 3, per the note there.) (product-dependent)
5. **Specific and visual, not vague:** Does the headline use specific, visual language the reader can picture, rather than vague, generic phrasing? MET when it paints a concrete, visual picture (for example "confident conversations in weeks instead of years," "get your bed back"). PARTIAL when partly specific but leaning generic. MISSING when it stays abstract and vague (for example "learn a language," "be more productive").
6. **Calls out the audience:** Is the intended audience called out so the right reader knows they are in the right place, ideally in the eyebrow just above the headline (or, less commonly, inside an uncrowded headline)? MET when the specific audience is clearly named where the reader can see it. PARTIAL when the audience is only loosely implied. MISSING when there is no call-out and the fit is not otherwise obvious. (If only the headline line itself is provided with no eyebrow, and audience fit is unclear, treat this as something to confirm rather than assume missing.)
7. **Opens a curiosity gap:** (prose only) Does the headline open a curiosity gap through a named method or framework, a "secret" or "key," a contrarian stance against conventional wisdom, or an intriguing question whose answer is not obvious? (bonus — if met it can raise the score; being missing does NOT count against the score)
8. **Crystal clear over clever:** Can the reader understand the headline in a single glance with no rereading, choosing clarity over cleverness? MET when it is instantly clear. PARTIAL when mostly clear but with a phrase that makes some readers pause. MISSING when it is clever or cryptic enough to force a "wait, what does that mean?"
9. **Unique enough for the market's saturation:** Is the headline fresh enough for how crowded its market is, rather than a stale or copied-sounding line? The bar scales with saturation: in an uncrowded market a simple, direct headline is unique enough; in a crowded market it must clearly differentiate. MET when it clears the bar for its market. PARTIAL when it is somewhat familiar for a crowded space. MISSING when it sounds stale or copied in a saturated market.
10. **Infuses a genuine emotion when one fits:** (prose only) If a real, core emotion underlies the audience's situation, is it woven into the headline (never forced or fabricated)? (bonus — if met it can raise the score; being missing does NOT count against the score)
