# Powerful Language — Doctrine

**Scope:** Editing/craft, section and page. Run it on a single section (is this section specific and vivid) or across the whole page. It is one of the editing elements a deep section audit should apply, and it also runs at the page level. It works closely with Emotion and Show Don't Tell.

## Principles (rules)

### Be specific, not vague
- The more specific your language, the more powerful the copy. Push past the general claim to the precise picture.
- Use a specificity ladder. "You want to lose weight" is vague. "You want a flat stomach" is better. "You want a visible six-pack" is better still. "You'll see separation between your lower abs, get rid of the love handles, get the radiator look you see in the magazines" is stronger again. Each step is more specific, and more specific is more powerful and more believable.

### Make claims concrete, and explain why they matter
- Take a plain claim and get ultra-specific with it, then explain why that detail matters (a number, a mechanism, the effect on the reader). "High quality coffee" becomes "grown at least 5,700ft above sea level, where cooler air slows the beans' growth so they absorb more nutrients and become denser, which makes them more nutrient-rich and superior in taste." "Better sleep" becomes exact metrics ("up to 44% less time to fall asleep, 34% more deep sleep").
- The specific detail plus the reason it matters elevates the whole perceived experience and makes the copy far stronger.

### Use visual and sensory language
- Make the reader visualize what you describe, especially in stories and when describing the problem or the outcome. Engage the senses: what does the reader see, hear, smell, feel, think, or touch in that moment?
- For six-pack abs, engage touch ("you touch your abs and they feel rock hard, not fluffy anymore"), sight ("you look in the mirror, take off your shirt, and you look absolutely shredded"), and emotion through a real-life situation ("you're on the beach, your friends take their shirts off, and you keep yours on"). These specific, situational, visual moments land far harder than "lose weight."
- Match the senses to the niche. A cooking product leans on smell (fresh-baked cookies filling the room); a weight-loss product leans on sight and touch. Think about which senses your audience relates to most.
- Mine your research for the visual and sensory language your audience already uses, and paste the best of it straight into the copy.

## Examples

- **Specific claim plus why it matters (Lifeboost Coffee):** instead of "high quality coffee": "reminiscent of a chocolate-covered fig, creamy and nutty, with a hint of a dried-fruit top note," plus the altitude explanation (grown at least 5,700ft up, cooler air slows growth, denser and more nutrient-rich beans, superior taste). — why it works: it replaces a vague boast with a precise, sensory picture and explains why the detail matters, which lifts the perceived experience.

- **Exact metrics instead of a vague claim (Eight Sleep):** instead of "better sleep": "up to 44% less time to fall asleep, up to 34% more deep sleep, up to 45% reduction in snoring." — why it works: exact numbers are concrete and believable where "better sleep" is just a claim (and they double as proof).

- **Specificity woven through a story (Level Up Your Ear):** the jam-session scene, told with specific, visual moments (the songs flowing, locking in with the drummer, the guitarist nodding and tuning up for the count-off). — why it works: the specificity is woven throughout, so the reader can see and feel the scene.

- **Visual, situational language for the problem (Fit Moms for Life):** "I didn't like what I saw in the mirror, I didn't feel comfortable naked in front of my husband," and later "what would it feel like if hubby couldn't keep his hands off?" — why it works: these specific, visual, emotionally charged situations resonate far more than "lose weight."

- **What you say, think, see, and do (Finishers Formula):** "we tell our friends, 'Yeah, we should get together!'" (what you say), "but as we say it, we know we're never going to do it" (what you think), "there's the briefest roll of their eyes" (what you see), "we actually become lazy" (what you do). — why it works: the precise, visual detail of each moment is what makes the section land.

- **A specific, sensory moment (Find Love Offline):** "It's Saturday night. You're home alone. After watching The Notebook for the umpteenth time... your ex-boyfriend is engaged... being single feels lonely. Really lonely." — why it works: it shows what the reader does, sees, thinks, and feels in one specific, visual moment, which lands far harder than "being single is hard."

## Scoring criteria

1. **Specific, not vague:** Does the copy push past general claims to the precise picture (the specificity ladder), rather than staying general? MET when it consistently chooses the specific version. PARTIAL when partly specific with stretches of vague, general claims. MISSING when it stays abstract and general.
2. **Claims made concrete, with the reason they matter:** Are key claims made concrete with exact detail (a number, a mechanism, a precise picture) and, where it helps, the reason that detail matters? MET when claims are concrete and, where relevant, explained. PARTIAL when concrete but unexplained, or explained but vague. MISSING when claims stay as bare, general assertions.
3. **Visual and sensory language:** Does the copy use visual, sensory language the reader can see and feel, engaging the senses that fit the niche, especially in stories and when describing the problem or outcome? MET when it consistently helps the reader picture and feel the moment. PARTIAL when partly vivid but flat in places. MISSING when it stays abstract with no sensory picture.
4. **Pulls sensory language from research:** Where possible, does it use the visual and sensory language the audience already uses (pulled from research), rather than invented phrasing? MET when it clearly reflects the audience's own vivid language. PARTIAL when it is vivid but generic. (A soft, upside-leaning criterion; depends on the research available.)
