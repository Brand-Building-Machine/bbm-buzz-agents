# Development Story — Doctrine

## Principles (rules)

### Purpose and placement
- The Development Story sits just before the Product Introduction and bridges the body into the offer. By this point the body has made the reader care about the product before revealing it; the Development Story shows how you solved your own or your clients' problems and turned that into a proven, tested product.
- It raises the perceived value of the product. A product that took six months and three iterations to get right is far more compelling than one thrown together over a weekend and never tested.

### What a strong story includes (only what genuinely happened)
- Why you created it (solving your own or your clients' problems).
- The research behind it (studying the audience's real challenges, and how you did it).
- Self-testing (testing it on yourself, with your own results).
- Client or beta testing (a beta cohort, students, or early users, and how it went).
- Iteration (how it evolved over versions until it was the best it could be).
- Early testimonials or preliminary results, woven in as proof.
- Only include what actually happened. Never invent a beta test, an iteration, a research phase, or a testimonial. If a step did not happen for this product, leave it out.

### Craft
- Tell the whole story in depth, rather than a vague summary. Depth is what makes this section convincing; a thin summary works far less well.
- Do not worry about length here. By this point the reader is engaged and will not bounce; take your time and weave in proof.
- Close with a quick summary of the finished product and a smooth transition into the offer.

## Examples

- **Textbook, proof-rich (Analytics Academy):** "We've looked into 193 real case math questions looking for the perfect system..." then the setup (they collected, analyzed, and categorized 193 questions, with a database screenshot), the hard work (the three things they were hunting for, a tease of the "5 archetypes," and pre-addressing "we didn't want to give you 900 questions"), the beta testing (Zoom calls with candidates, some awake at 3am), early testimonials (McKinsey offers), and a summary of the finished method with a smooth transition to the offer. — why it works: it is specific and proof-rich at every step (a real number in the headline, a database screenshot, real beta calls, real early results), which makes the product feel hard-won and tested.

- **Beta cohort as proof (Level Up Your Ear):** "It took me 15 years to figure this out. But you don't need 15 years... I took everything I learned and created a 6-week class and offered it to a small number of students," then who the students were and what they struggled with, then specific results (Aielo joined a cover band, Brian cut transcription time by 75%), then back to the reader (the dream, objections) and the offer. — why it works: it uses a real beta cohort as proof, shares specific student results, and turns back to the reader before inviting them in.

- **Weaker, a caution (Valued):** it tells the origin (a two-day event in Atlanta with a photo), teases the audiences it helped, and explains how the event became the online course, but stays vague and skips concrete proof and the in-depth work. — why it works, and the caution: it hits the origin, a visual, and the transition, and it is much weaker than a full story because it summarizes instead of telling the whole story in depth. Tell the whole story.

## Scoring criteria

For criteria 3-7 (research, self-testing, beta testing, iteration, early results): score what genuinely happened. If the copy omits one, note that adding it would strengthen the section IF it really happened, and set it aside if it did not. Never fabricate any of them.

1. **Bridges the body into the offer:** Does it sit as a bridge just before the product introduction, carrying the reader from the body into the offer? MET when it clearly bridges toward the offer. PARTIAL when the bridge is weak. MISSING when it does not lead into the offer.
2. **Explains why you created it:** Does it explain why you created the product (solving your own or your clients' problems)? MET when the reason is clear. PARTIAL when hinted at. MISSING when absent.
3. **Shows the research behind it:** Does it show the research that went into it (studying the audience's real challenges, and how)? MET when the research is concrete. PARTIAL when vague. (product-dependent — set aside if there was genuinely no research phase; never invent one.)
4. **Explains self-testing:** Does it explain how you tested it on yourself, with your own results? MET when self-testing and results are clear. PARTIAL when vague. (product-dependent — set aside if the product was not self-tested, for example one built for clients; never invent it.)
5. **Explains client or beta testing:** Does it explain how a beta cohort, students, or early users tested it, and how it went? MET when the beta testing is concrete. PARTIAL when vague. (product-dependent — set aside if there was no beta or client testing; never invent it.)
6. **Explains iteration:** Does it explain how the product evolved over versions until it was the best it could be? MET when the iteration is clear. PARTIAL when vague. (product-dependent — set aside if there was no real iteration; never invent it.)
7. **Weaves in early testimonials or preliminary results:** Does it weave in early testimonials or preliminary results as proof? MET when real early proof is present. PARTIAL when thin. (product-dependent — set aside if none exist; never fabricate; where the proof is a screenshot, confirm it with the user.)
8. **Tells the whole story in depth:** Does it tell the whole story in depth, rather than a vague summary? MET when it goes deep and concrete. PARTIAL when it summarizes some of it. MISSING when it is a thin, vague summary throughout.
9. **Closes with a product summary and a transition into the offer:** Does it close with a quick summary of the finished product and a smooth transition into the offer? MET when it clearly does both. PARTIAL when one is weak. MISSING when it ends flat with no bridge to the offer.
