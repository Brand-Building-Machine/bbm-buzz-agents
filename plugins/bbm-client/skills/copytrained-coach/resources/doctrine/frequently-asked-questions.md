# Frequently Asked Questions — Doctrine

## Principles (rules)

### Purpose
- The FAQ is one of the most important sections on the page: your chance to address any remaining questions or objections before the reader buys. Position it right after the first Buy Buttons section. This is a must-have section.
- Cover a mix of universal questions (that apply to almost any product) and product-specific questions (unique to your offer). Always include the relevant universal questions, and continuously update the FAQ with the real questions you get from potential clients (usually by email).

### The universal questions to cover
- Is this right for me? (and "will it work for me if [objection]", including any minority segment of your audience)
- How does it work / what happens when I join?
- Required time and effort
- Length of access (lifetime or limited; and, for limited access or a membership, why)
- The guarantee
- How is this different (from your other products or tiers, from competitors, or from common alternatives like books or hiring a coach)
- What results can I expect?
- What if I fall behind, or don't have enough time?
- Payment options
- Feature-specific questions (for features people ask about)
- Tools or software needed
- Have a different question? (a contact prompt at the end)

### How to write the answers
- Keep a personal, conversational tone.
- Directly acknowledge the objection, rather than just providing information.
- Educate the reader on why the objection should not stop them.
- Point to the specific product features or bonuses that address it.
- Back up your answer with proof where possible (a testimonial, a specific result). Never fabricate proof.
- On "expected results": promise a specific result only if you can genuinely deliver it (and back it with a result-based guarantee if you have one); if results are intangible or vary, be honest, and frame real student results as proof rather than promises. On "what if I fall behind": reassure via lifetime access or your support system if you have one, though for some programs you may instead choose to turn these clients away here.

### Design and order
- Make it expandable and clickable (an accordion) if you can; otherwise, separate each question and answer clearly.
- Write the questions as real audience questions, in their own words, so they grab attention. Order them from most common to least common.

## Examples

- **A minority segment called out (Find Love Offline):** "Is the course only for women seeking men?", answered and backed by a detailed male testimonial. — why it works: directly calling out a minority segment with a relevant testimonial converts readers who would otherwise assume the product is not for them.

- **Differentiation, done softly (Behind The Sales Email):** "There are lots of books about sales. What's different?", which acknowledges that books are valuable, then names the specific things you can only learn by watching ("the difference between reading a recipe book and watching a chef cook"). — why it works: acknowledging the alternative's value first, then the specific difference, is more persuasive than dismissing it.

- **Differentiation, honestly (Bulletproof Mental Math):** "Can't I just get a mental math book?", which names the two real drawbacks of books, says when a book does make sense, and lets the reader decide. — why it works: honesty about when the alternative works actually builds trust.

- **Length of access plus the follow-on objections (Notion Mastery):** "How long will I have access?" (12 months), then "Why is there a membership fee after 12 months?" and "How often is the course updated?". — why it works: it pre-empts the objections a limited-access model naturally raises.

- **Expected results as proof, not promises (Find Love Offline):** "What results can I expect?", answered with a list of specific, named student results. — why it works: framing results as "here's what my students achieved" is stronger and safer than "you'll get X."

- **A guarantee question framed as the objection (Fear of Falling):** "What happens if I try the course but don't end up liking it?", answered with the guarantee. — why it works: it puts the objection in the headline and answers it plainly.

- **"No time" answered head-on and backed by proof (Master Market Sizing):** "What if I don't have enough time?", reframed ("that's the wrong question"), then a specific feature that helps, backed by a testimonial. — why it works: it answers directly, points to a feature, and proves it with a client story.

- **An answer carried by a testimonial (Level Up Your Ear):** "Will this work for the kind of music I play?", answered briefly, then a full email testimonial from a student. — why it works: the testimonial does the convincing, not just the claim.

## Scoring criteria

1. **Covers the real questions a buyer still has:** Judged against the standard coverage set (what it is and who it is for, what is included and how it works, the time and effort it takes, results and what is realistic, price and payment, access and refunds, support, and the objections this audience actually raises). MET when the questions a real buyer would still be holding are all answered. PARTIAL when the obvious ones are covered and one or two live objections are left standing. MISSING when it answers questions nobody was asking while the real ones go unanswered. **Name what is absent rather than counting what is present: coverage alone is not quality.**
13. **Covers the product-specific questions:** Does it cover the real questions this product's audience actually asks, beyond the universal ones? MET when the real audience questions are present. PARTIAL when only universal questions appear. MISSING when it is thin and generic.
14. **Answer craft:** Do the answers directly acknowledge the objection (not just give information), stay personal and conversational, educate on why it should not stop the reader, point to specific features or bonuses, and back claims with proof where possible? MET when the answers consistently do this. PARTIAL when some do and others are dry info. MISSING when answers are flat and information-only. (Read every answer for missed opportunities to acknowledge, prove, or point to a feature.)
15. **Ordering and language:** Are the questions written as real audience questions in their own words, and ordered from most common to least common? MET when both hold. PARTIAL when one does. MISSING when the questions are generic and unordered.
16. **Design (expandable and separated):** Is the FAQ expandable/clickable, or at least clearly separated question-by-question? (visual — audit must ask the user)
