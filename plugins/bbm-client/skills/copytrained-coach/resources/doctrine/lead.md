# Lead — Doctrine

## Principles (rules)

### Purpose and why the lead matters
- The lead takes over right after the headline and subheadline. The headline gets the subheadline read, the subheadline gets the first line of the lead read, and the first line of the lead exists to get the second line read. No selling, no offers yet. Just pulling attention forward.
- The headline and the lead together carry a huge share of whether the page succeeds (some estimate around 80%), especially on cold traffic, where the reader is meeting the brand for the first time and will leave in seconds if the opening does not land.
- On a very warm audience (people who read everything you publish or buy anything you make), the lead matters a little less, because a strong product section can rescue an imperfect lead. On cold traffic, the lead is make-or-break.
- Because of this, the lead is the section to spend the MOST time editing and rewriting, and the first thing to A/B test when you want to lift conversions.

### What great leads have in common
- It hooks and pulls the reader in. Every sentence intrigues them enough to read the next one.
- It moves the reader emotionally before it convinces them logically. The feeling comes first, the proof comes later.
- It concentrates on one thing: one big idea, one core emotion, one promise. Not five.
- It is specific, not vague. Specific language is believable language.
- It matches the reader's stage of awareness and warmth (this drives which lead type you pick).

### Awareness and warmth drive the lead type
- The core rule: the more aware and warmed-up your reader is, the more direct your lead can be. The colder and more skeptical they are, the more indirect it needs to be.
- A warm audience (an existing list, repeat buyers, a Black Friday deal to happy users) needs almost no setup. Lead with the offer.
- A cold audience (someone who just clicked a Meta, TikTok, or YouTube ad and has never heard of you) will bounce if you lead with the offer before they care or trust you. You earn their attention first, with a story, a secret, or a bold claim, before you sell.

### The lead type is a thread through the whole opening
- The lead type is not just the body copy. It is the angle of the entire opening. It starts in the headline, carries through the subheadline, and into the first lines of the copy. A Promise lead promises from the first line and keeps promising. A Story lead opens a story in the headline and then tells it.
- **When auditing, always ask for the headline and subheadline first.** The lead type is a thread that starts in the headline, so seeing the whole opening sharpens the read of the lead. If the user gives only the lead, gently ask them to share the headline and subheadline too, explain that it sharpens the analysis, and wait for them before scoring. If the user does not have them or declines, audit the lead on its own and say that is what you did.

### The 6 lead types (a menu, matched to awareness)
- **Promise lead.** Lead with your single biggest benefit or boldest believable claim (the biggest, most common desire from your research), and keep promising. The most common type today, because most products lean on a clear, desirable outcome people already want.
- **Problem-Solution lead.** Open on the reader's most painful problem, then bridge to your solution. Works when the audience feels a specific pain but has not yet connected it to your solution, and especially when the problems in your research are more common, painful, or emotionally charged than the desires.
- **Story lead.** Open with a powerful story instead of a plain promise or problem. One of the most powerful ways to start for a cold audience, because it builds emotional connection before selling. Also the hardest to pull off and the riskiest (stories hit big or flop). Avoid it on a brand-new product unless you are very confident in your storytelling, you know the story resonates (tested through talks, emails, conversations), or you are A/B testing with budget to burn.
- **Big Secret lead.** Tease a piece of hidden knowledge and hold it back through the copy, revealing it just before or inside the product section. Sometimes the secret is an a-ha moment revealed in the copy; sometimes the product itself is the secret. Trickier to pull off; make sure the payoff is genuinely new and worth the wait.
- **Proclamation lead.** Open with a bold claim that raises eyebrows: a shocking statistic, a superiority claim, or a controversial opinion. It grabs attention and raises skepticism, so only use it with exceptional proof, used early and heavily throughout, and only if the product truly delivers on the claim.
- **Offer lead.** The most direct type. Lead with the deal itself. Use it for a warm audience that already knows the product (bundles, discounts, Black Friday, flash sales), where you are mostly giving a better deal. The lead is short and moves straight into the product section.

### Which type to choose
- In about 80% of cases, start with a Promise or a Problem-Solution lead, because most visitors are aware of either their problem or the outcome they want, and these two speak directly to them.
- If unsure between them, draft both and see which flows better and feels stronger. The lead is only a few hundred words, so trying both is cheap.
- The other four (Story, Big Secret, Proclamation, Offer) are situational: Story to win a cold audience with a powerful story; Proclamation when you have undeniable proof and eye-catching metrics; Big Secret when the product or narrative is genuinely new; Offer when buyers are already warm and you are just giving a better deal.

### What the lead should include, and how much
- The lead's job is to hook and to frame, not to say everything at once. Think of it as a tease of the page's key elements (the problem, the dream, the objections): touch and acknowledge them here, then fully develop them in their dedicated sections later.
- A good lead transitions smoothly into the later sections, so the reader rarely notices the shift.
- How much you front-load depends on the type: a Problem-Solution lead does heavy lifting on the problem (the problem becomes the lead); a Promise lead leans on the promise and reasons to believe it and goes light on the problem; a Story lead lets the problem and the dream emerge through the story; an Offer lead is short and hands off to the product section.

### Craft principles (use the ones that fit; do not force all of them)
- **Start with the reader, not yourself.** Open with the narrative already running in the reader's head (their problem, desire, daily frustration). The weakest leads open with the product or the author talking about themselves. Your own story has a place, usually after you have shown the reader you understand them.
- **Fast pacing.** Make it effortless to read: short sentences, short paragraphs, often one-liners. Avoid complex or run-on sentences that force a re-read, and avoid walls of text. This applies to customer quotes and screenshots too: a long or complex quote hurts the pace just as much as long body copy, so trim it or pull out only the punchy part. Fix any confusing line (for example "the status quo sleep sitch" makes readers stop and puzzle).
- **Advance, do not repeat.** Every beat must add something new. Do not restate the headline in the lead, and do not repeat the same idea two or three times in a row. Repetition, whether from the headline into the lead or within the lead itself, kills momentum and makes readers start skimming. This is one of the most common and most costly lead mistakes.
- **Tease the key elements.** Sprinkle in light touches of the problem, the dream, and the objections you will develop later.
- **Incorporate visual proof.** A lead does not have to be only words. Strong visual proof (before and afters, annotated screenshots, a striking graph) can go straight into the lead and make the promise more believable and specific.
- **Mirror what your audience is already thinking.** Write the lead directly from customer research. The closer it sits to their exact thoughts, feelings, and situation, the more they feel you get them and keep reading. Real research quotes (as bullets or shown as screenshots) work well.
- **Name the villain.** Point a finger at a false belief, a broken system, or an enemy. This takes the blame off the reader ("it is not your fault") and opens a curiosity gap. Optional: use it only when you have a real, resonant belief about what the industry gets wrong.
- **Open new loops.** Plant a piece of the puzzle early (something you reveal later), which pulls the reader forward. Loops can live in the headline, the subheadline, or the lead.
- **Address the biggest objections.** In an objection-heavy market, if you did not pre-address objections in the subheadline, acknowledge them early in the lead. You do not need to fully prove them here; acknowledging keeps skeptics engaged by hinting it can work for them too.
- **Don't bury the lead.** Scan the copy you have already written; a powerful passage (often a story) buried deeper down may be your best lead. Great leads are often found in the flow of a first draft, not written to order.

### Craft and testing
- Give the lead more editing attention than any other section. Read it out loud. Show it to friends or clients. Write it over multiple sessions so ideas can marinate.
- Three quick checks: does the first sentence pull you into the second, are you opening on the reader rather than yourself, and is there a hook, an emotion, and a reason to keep going.
- Keep it simple. Do not use every principle in one lead; more principles usually add complexity, and you want one simple, clear core idea.

## Examples

Each example shows the opening working together (headline into lead), because the lead type is a thread that runs through the whole opening.

**The 6 lead types**

- **Promise lead (Unignorable):** "Build an UNIGNORABLE personal brand (that makes clients chase you). Algorithms change lightning-fast. Human nature doesn't. UNIGNORABLE is the only personal branding course based on the science of attention and persuasion. We've helped 1,482 entrepreneurs build audiences that actually buy. Now it's your turn." — why it works: the promise (become unignorable so clients chase you) starts in the headline and continues straight into the lead with a specific proof number (1,482), keeping one clear idea.

- **Problem-Solution lead (Find Love Offline):** "Question: Why haven't you found love?" (problem) into "How to Find Love Without Apps" (solution), then it deepens the problem with a vivid scene: "It's Saturday night. You're home alone. After watching The Notebook for the umpteenth time... your ex-boyfriend is engaged... You finally admit it: being single feels lonely. Really lonely." — why it works: it opens on the burning problem, bridges to the solution, then goes deeper into the pain with specific, visual storytelling before eventually reaching the solution.

- **Story lead (Level Up Your Ear):** "I don't want to be the weakest link in my band anymore..." into "You're at a jam session, playing your favorite songs... Tune after tune gets called, and you're all over them... But then someone calls a song you don't know how to play... So what do you do?" — why it works: the headline plants the problem, then the lead becomes a vivid, felt story that builds a conflict the reader wants resolved. Story leads build emotional connection before any selling, and this is a strong one. (Story leads are also the riskiest: they hit big or flop.)

- **Big Secret lead (iPhone Photo Academy):** "Unlock the EXACT blueprint to capture breathtaking iPhone photos. Join 654,241 iPhone users who now take stunning photos every time. Apple won't tell you this: there's a proven shortcut to take incredible iPhone photos..." — why it works: "Apple won't tell you this" teases hidden knowledge and holds it back, so the reader keeps going to find the secret, while the product is positioned as the way to get it.

- **Proclamation lead (Brand Voice Challenge):** "The Internet is Dead. It's official. AI-generated content has now surpassed human content online... here's why this creates a massive opportunity for you... If you're relying on cheap, generic AI content right now, you're in a race to the bottom. Be human or R.I.P." (with a graph of AI vs human content) — why it works: a bold, controversial claim grabs attention and is immediately backed by proof (the graph) and an argument. Proclamation leads raise skepticism, so they need strong proof early and throughout.

- **Offer lead (Sauna Pod Bundle):** "4.8/5 based on 574 reviews. 3 free gifts included with your sauna. SAUNA POD BUNDLE... Heats to 85C like an authentic Finnish sauna... 1,326 EUR down to 599 EUR, 727 EUR off, 54% off." — why it works: for a warm, ready-to-buy audience, the deal itself is the lead. The spotlight is the free gifts and the 54% discount, with only light benefit copy.

**Start with the reader, not yourself**

- **Good (all reader, no product or author, I Heart Money):** "Let's be honest, you were never meant for 'just enough.' You've got the ambition, the work ethic, the vision, but the bank account doesn't match the woman you know you are. You've tried journaling harder, manifesting harder, hustling harder. And yet, you're still staring at the same money ceiling. Here's the truth: strategy alone won't save you." — why it works: every line is about the reader's inner world (their ambition, their frustration, their ceiling), not the product or the author, which makes the reader feel seen.

**Fast pacing**

- **Good, with one caution (Moving on From Cosleeping):** "Have you been putting it off, even though you KNOW you're ready? Sometimes we keep the status quo sleep sitch because big changes are scary! What if things get worse? What if they cry or feel abandoned? But when it's no longer working and NO ONE is sleeping well? It's time for a shift, and I'm here to guide you through it." — why it works: simple language, short sentences, good pace. The caution: "the status quo sleep sitch" is confusing and makes the reader re-read, which is exactly what fast pacing should avoid.

- **Good (fast story pacing, Teach Yourself Anything):** "A few years ago, my friend and I were grabbing drinks in the East Village... But a few years later, I found myself in exactly the same situation as my friend: Same bars. Same conversations. Same weekend plans. In fact, when someone asked 'What'd you do this weekend?' my response was... 'Nothing really.' Or: 'Same old.' Or even worse: 'Just worked.'" — why it works: it starts in a real conversation, builds conflict fast, and uses bullets and one-liners to break up the copy and escalate. (Nitpick: the first paragraph is a little too complex and would pace better if simplified or split.)

**Tease the key elements**

- **Good (promise plus benefit bullets plus objections, Home Studio Blueprint):** "The perfect studio for your business? How much better would your content look and sound if you had a studio that didn't hold you back? Clean, sharp video even in a small or dark room. Clear audio that doesn't echo or buzz... Most people never get results like this, because they assume it takes expensive gear or advanced skills... But it doesn't." (with a before/after grid) — why it works: it opens on the promise, expands it in clear benefit bullets, pre-addresses objections (expensive gear, advanced skills, space), and teases the transformation, all while staying one idea.

- **Good (framing plus objections plus outcomes, Earnable):** "Introducing Earnable, a totally new way to start and grow your business. Even if you don't have a business idea. Even if you're not a world-famous expert. Even if you're overwhelmed with no idea where to start. And yes, especially if you want to increase your income by $1,000, or $10,000, or even $100,000..." — why it works: "a totally new way" adds curiosity, the "even if" bullets pre-address the market's biggest objections (pulled from research), and it teases specific outcomes, all in a few tight lines.

**Incorporate visual proof**

- **Good (proof-led lead, Behind The Sales Page):** "For the first time ever, get a behind-the-scenes look at the secret playbook I've been using to quietly crank out 6- and 7-figure sales pages, for years. Like this 16-page Finisher's Formula sales page, which earned $344,523 in less than a week." (with annotated screenshots showing "$344,523 / 5 days" and "$19,070 a month") — why it works: the visual proof runs through the lead and makes the promise specific and immediately believable. It is not "how to write 6-7 figure pages," it is "how I wrote this exact page that made $344,523 in 5 days." The promise, the specificity, and the visual proof work together.

- **Good (one strong before/after, iPhone Photo Academy):** "So you can go from taking regular shots like this: [ordinary flat beach photo]. To incredible images like this! [stunning golden-hour silhouette]." — why it works: one before/after that contrasts the photos the audience takes now with the ones they want is a strong, believable hook.

**Mirror what your audience is already thinking**

- **Good (research-pulled bullets, Social Accelerator):** "Do any of these ring true for you? Do you overthink social situations? Do you avoid speaking up (or self-edit when you do)? Do you run out of things to say during small talk? Do you replay awkward moments over and over after the fact? If any of those rang true, then you're just like my private coaching clients..." — why it works: the bullets are lifted straight from research, so readers recognize themselves line by line, then it calls out the audience and their ambitions and ends on a hook into the next section.

- **Good (living their moment, Dynamic Programming):** "Have you ever had this experience? You spend weeks preparing to interview at your dream company. Hours in the library, jittery from your third coffee... Some topics are easy. Others, maybe dynamic programming, aren't quite sticking... 'Maybe I can delay my interview?' Nope. The interview is next week. You've already rescheduled twice." — why it works: it aligns so exactly with what the reader is going through, and is written so personally, that a reader in that situation feels instantly understood and keeps reading.

- **Good (real research quotes, Analytics Academy):** "Recently we talked to a couple dozen candidates who had just WRECKED their interviews because they got stuck on a math question. Here are a few things they said: 'I got so nervous that I couldn't even think.' 'Her tone of voice changed... And I had no idea what to do!' 'I just over-complicated everything.'" — why it works: it mirrors the audience with their own unedited words shown as quotes, so readers who see themselves in even one keep reading.

**Name the villain**

- **Good (named villain plus "not your fault," Time Genius):** "Millions of smart, hard-working people have been sucked into a toxic, soul-crushing nightmare I call the 'Time Stress Trap'... that struggle you're stuck in? It's not your fault. The truth is, you've inherited a broken and dysfunctional approach to time." — why it works: it names an enemy (the "Time Stress Trap," a broken inherited system), takes the blame off the reader ("it's not your fault," which feels good), and opens a curiosity gap about the fix.

- **Good (villain as a false belief, Bought In):** "They lied to you: Perfect programming is not enough. You'll never get the results you want if your athletes don't fully buy into you or your vision." — why it works: the enemy is a belief the audience was taught (that great programming is what makes a great coach), which reframes their struggle and pulls them in.

**Open new loops**

- **Good (planted loop, I Heart Money):** "You don't need another marketing tip. You don't need another productivity hack. Because wealth isn't just about what you do, it's about who you are... no one ever taught them how to rewire their money story, expand their nervous system capacity for wealth..." — why it works: "rewire your money story" is a new idea planted early and paid off later, an open loop the reader wants closed.

- **Good (withheld solution, Dynamic Programming):** "So why not just spend a few extra hours studying dynamic programming and be on your merry way? Unfortunately, it's not so simple." — why it works: it opens a loop ("it's not so simple") and, instead of handing over the solution, breaks down why the conventional approach fails, which builds interest.

**Address the biggest objections**

- **Good (objections as bullets, Earnable):** "Even if you don't have a business idea. Even if you're not a world-famous expert. Even if you're overwhelmed with no idea where to start." — why it works: it acknowledges the market's biggest objections head-on, early, so skeptics stay engaged by sensing it can work for them too.
- **Good (objection in an early subheadline, Own The Inbox):** "There's money hiding in your email list (no matter how small it is)." — why it works: "no matter how small it is" directly answers the objection of readers with tiny lists, right up front. Acknowledging the objection early is enough; the full proof comes later.

## Scoring criteria

Always ask for the headline and subheadline before scoring a lead (see the note in Principles): they sharpen the read, because the lead type is a thread that starts in the headline. Ask for them, wait, then score. If the user does not have them or declines, audit the lead on its own and say so.

1. **Pulls the reader forward (the one job):** (the one job) Does the first sentence pull the reader into the second, and each line into the next, so they keep reading? This is the lead's core job. Repetition and slow, heavy pacing sag the momentum and get readers skimming, which lowers this even when the underlying material is strong. MET when the pull is consistent and strong throughout. PARTIAL when it mostly holds but sags in places. MISSING when the reader has little reason to keep going.
2. **Opens with the reader, not you or the product:** Does the lead open inside the reader's world (their problem, desire, or daily frustration), rather than with the author, the author's own story, or the product? MET when the opening clearly centers the reader. PARTIAL when it mixes reader focus with early product or author talk. MISSING when it opens on the product or the author's own story. (Your own story has its place later; the opening earns trust by showing you understand the reader first.)
3. **Fast-paced (short sentences and paragraphs):** Is the lead built from short sentences and short paragraphs, often one-liners, that move fast down the page? Judge the whole lead, including any customer quotes: long, complex, or run-on sentences hurt the pacing even inside a quote or a screenshot. MET only when the sentences are genuinely short and the whole thing moves fast and light. PARTIAL when the framing is short but the substance leans on long or complex sentences, or when there are dense stretches. MISSING when it is slow and heavy throughout.
4. **No confusing lines (nothing forces a re-read):** Can the reader read every line once and keep moving, with no confusing phrasing that makes them stop and re-read? MET when every line is effortless on first read. PARTIAL when mostly clear but with a confusing line or two that breaks the flow. MISSING when confusing phrasing repeatedly trips the reader.
5. **Moves the reader emotionally before logically:** Does the lead stir a feeling first and bring the proof or logic later, rather than opening with dry logic? MET when emotion clearly comes first. PARTIAL when emotion is present but muted, or mixed early with heavy logic. MISSING when it is logic-first and flat.
6. **Stays on one core idea:** Does the lead hold to one big idea, one core emotion, one promise, rather than juggling several? MET when it stays focused on one idea. PARTIAL when mostly focused but drifting into extra ideas. MISSING when it is scattered across many.
7. **Specific and believable, not vague:** Is the lead concrete and specific (which is what makes it believable), rather than vague and general? MET when it consistently chooses the specific over the generic. PARTIAL when partly specific but with vague stretches. MISSING when it stays vague and generic.
8. **Mirrors what the audience is already thinking or feeling:** Does the lead reflect the reader's real inner narrative, their actual thoughts, feelings, and situation (ideally straight from research), so they feel understood? MET when it clearly mirrors the reader's real experience. PARTIAL when it is accurate but generic. MISSING when it does not reflect their real experience.
9. **Teases the key elements (hooks and frames):** Does the lead touch on and acknowledge the elements it will develop later (the problem, the dream, the objections), framing the page without fully developing them here? A lead that only establishes the problem, with no glimpse of the dream, the way out, or any forward element, is one-note and is not teasing. MET when it clearly teases and frames what is coming, beyond just the problem. PARTIAL when it teases only thinly, or only one forward element. MISSING when it teases nothing of what is coming, or tries to dump everything at once. (The lead's job is to hook and frame, not to say it all.)
10. **Advances without repeating (no restating the headline, no repeating an idea):** Does each part of the lead, from the headline through the lead, add something new, rather than restating the headline or repeating the same idea two or three times? Repetition (from the headline into the lead, or within the lead) kills momentum and starts readers skimming, so treat it as a serious problem, not a small one. MET when every part moves the reader forward with new material. PARTIAL when it mostly advances but restates the headline once, or repeats one idea once. MISSING when it restates the headline in the lead, or repeats the same idea two or three times in a row.
11. **Uses specific numbers, statistics, or metrics:** Does the lead include a specific number or metric (a real stat, count, or figure) where it strengthens believability? MET when it uses a real, concrete number. PARTIAL when it references a quantity vaguely ("thousands") where a real figure exists. Set aside if the product genuinely has no metric to cite. Never invent a number. (product-dependent)
12. **Incorporates visual proof:** (prose only) Does the lead include strong visual proof (before and afters, annotated screenshots, a striking graph or stat visual)? MET when strong visual proof appears in the lead. (visual — audit must ask the user) (bonus — if met it can raise the score; being missing does NOT count against the score)
13. **Acknowledges the biggest objection(s):** In an objection-heavy market, and only if the objections were not already handled in the subheadline, does the lead at least acknowledge the reader's biggest objection(s) (even without fully proving them yet)? MET when it names or acknowledges the core objection(s). PARTIAL when it hints without really acknowledging. Set aside when the market is not objection-heavy or the objections were already handled earlier. (product-dependent)
14. **Names the villain:** (prose only) Does the lead point at a villain (a false belief, a broken system, an enemy) that takes the blame off the reader and opens a curiosity gap? (bonus — if met it can raise the score; being missing does NOT count against the score)
15. **Opens at least one loop early:** (prose only) Does the lead plant an open loop early (a piece of the puzzle it promises to pay off later) that pulls the reader forward? (bonus — if met it can raise the score; being missing does NOT count against the score)
