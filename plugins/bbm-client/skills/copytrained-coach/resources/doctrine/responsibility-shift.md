# Responsibility Shift — Doctrine

## Principles (rules)

### Purpose
- This section shifts some of the responsibility for the reader's situation from the reader to the outside world. A reader who believes "I am the problem" is paralyzed by shame and less likely to act; lifting some of that blame frees them to move.
- It does four useful things: it externalizes the blame, it opens a curiosity gap ("if they lied to me, then what actually works?"), it builds trust (empathy plus demystifying how things really work), and it restores hope (past attempts failed because of the wrong approach, not a lack of effort).

### Two ways to do it
- Name a common enemy (a broken system, an industry, a category of "experts," a product category). Naming an enemy is optional; do not force one if you do not have a real one.
- Tell the reader the problem "is not their fault." You can do one or both.

### How to do it well
- Make sure the audience actually resonates: pull from research, the things they have tried that did not work.
- Explain why it is not their fault (or why the enemy blocked them), rather than just asserting it.
- Back it with strong proof wherever you make a claim: a personal story, statistics, or screenshots. A strong claim ("apps are designed to keep you single") must be proven, or you lose the reader.
- Open a curiosity gap that points the reader toward your solution or mechanism.

### The sincerity guardrail (handled later, not scored here)
- Do not take FULL responsibility off the reader; that reads as insincere. Later on the page (usually the close), you reframe it as "it is not your fault you are here, but it IS your responsibility to fix it." That later beat lives in another section and is not scored in this one.

## Examples

- **Naming the enemy, backed by proof (Find Love Offline):** "Apps are Designed to Keep You Single," then proof bullets ("the only way dating platforms make money is by keeping you single and swiping," "over 65% of app users were revealed to already be married or in a relationship"). — why it works: a strong, curiosity-inducing headline names the enemy, and because it makes a big claim, the bullets below prove it and pile on reasons to distrust the apps.

- **Enemy plus "it's not your fault" plus the why (Unignorable):** "It's not your fault though. You've been lied to by the audience growth experts. What worked for them won't work for you. Chances are their business looks totally different than yours." — why it works: it names the enemy, says plainly it is not your fault, and explains why (their situation is different from yours), rather than just declaring it.

- **Deep, proof-heavy takedown (Master Market Sizing):** it names multiple enemies (books, websites, YouTube) and goes deep with proof (real screenshots of bad advice, detailed explanations of why it fails), then three "fatal problems." — why it works: it earns trust by proving, in detail, why the advice the reader has been following is bad, so they start trusting the author and wondering what the real solution is.

- **"Not your fault" leading, self-evident claim (Upright Posture):** "Poor posture isn't your fault. But there is something you can do about it. Modern life traps us in positions that destroy our natural alignment." — why it works: it leads with "not your fault," names the enemy (modern life), and expands on it, without needing heavy proof because the claim is self-evident.

## Scoring criteria

1. **Shifts some responsibility off the reader:** Does the section externalize at least some of the blame, through a named enemy and/or a clear "it's not your fault"? MET when it clearly lifts some responsibility off the reader. PARTIAL when it only hints at it. MISSING when the reader is left carrying all the blame.
2. **Names a common enemy:** Does it name a clear, specific common enemy (a broken system, an industry, a category of "experts," a product category)? This is a key part of the section. MET when a specific enemy is clearly named. PARTIAL when the external cause is implied but no enemy is named plainly (leaning only on a soft "it's not your fault"). MISSING when it names no enemy and points to no external cause. If this product genuinely has no real enemy, a sincere "it's not your fault" that points to a clear external cause can still earn credit, but the strongest version names the enemy outright.
3. **Explains why, rather than just declaring it:** Does it explain why the enemy blocked them (or why it is not their fault), rather than only asserting it? MET when the reason is clearly explained. PARTIAL when the explanation is thin. MISSING when it just declares it with no reasoning.
4. **Pulled from research, so it resonates:** Is it built from the things the audience actually tried that did not work, in their real language? MET when it clearly reflects their real experience. PARTIAL when accurate but generic. MISSING when it does not reflect what they actually tried.
5. **Backs a claim with proof:** Where it makes a claim, does it back it with proof (a personal story, statistics, or screenshots)? MET when a real claim is clearly proven. PARTIAL when proof is gestured at but thin. Set aside when the shift is a soft, self-evident "not your fault" that needs no hard proof. (product-dependent — and where the proof is a screenshot, confirm it with the user, since it is visual.)
6. **Opens a curiosity gap toward the solution:** Does it open a curiosity gap that points the reader toward your solution or mechanism ("if that's the enemy, then what works?")? MET when it clearly points forward. PARTIAL when the forward pull is faint. MISSING when it dead-ends.
