# Close — Doctrine

## Principles (rules)

### Purpose and when to use
- The close is one of the last sections on the page. Its job is to help close the sale by nudging an interested reader to make a decision now.
- It speaks to people on the fence: readers who are clearly interested (they've read to the end) but hesitating. Its central objection to answer is "I don't know if now is the right time to join."
- Include a close on mid-ticket ($47–$497) and flagship ($497–$2997) product pages. There it is almost a must.
- Leave the close OFF shorter pages, physical-product pages, and low-ticket pages (for example a $27 e-book). On those it can feel like overselling and work against you.
- There is no single formula for a close. Follow the principles below and write it in your own tone.

### What a close can do (a menu, not a checklist to fill)
A close can use any combination of these moves. Pick the ones that fit your audience; do not force all of them in.
- Create urgency: why to join now, or the cost of not joining (waiting and inaction).
- Address any final objections for people on the fence.
- Communicate your vision, mission, or values that your audience resonates with.
- Reiterate your strongest copy: the guarantee, past student successes and proof, your unique framework or mechanism, and other powerful elements from earlier on the page.
- Take readers on an emotional rollercoaster, switching between the problems they face and the outcomes they want.
- Go deeper into the positive consequences of the outcome, or the negative consequences of doing nothing.
- Include a brief, relatable personal story, or a message written from the heart.
- Inspire, empower, or nudge the reader to take action.
- Use future pacing ("where will you be a year from now?").
- Add a hint of injustice (others doing better than you). Use this only if your readers genuinely feel it.

The overall goal: write it true to your tone and brand, speak to your specific audience's most common objections, and use the strongest marketing materials you have at hand.

### The emotional journey
- You can take the reader on a mini emotional rollercoaster: move between their problems, jarring truths, and their desires.
- Always end the close on a good, uplifting note. This is a firm rule, not a preference.
- Injustice and envy only work if the reader actually experiences them. Do not manufacture an emotion the audience does not feel.

### Match the close to your audience
- If clients buy mainly to escape painful problems, lead with the problems (for example a "No more…" bullet close).
- If clients resonate more with the desire, lead with the dream (for example an "Imagine…" bullet close).
- If the dream is the single biggest reason they buy, a mostly positive, dream-painting close can fit.
- Justify the price where it is a live objection (cost per day, or money already wasted on alternatives).

### Formats to choose from
- Standard paragraph close.
- Short close. Fine even on a moderately long page; just shorter than on a very long page.
- "Still on the fence?" opener. A safe, reliable headline that immediately grabs the hesitating reader.
- From-the-heart personal note ("a final note"). Works well when it genuinely reads as written from the heart.
- Testimonial-led close. Let strong proof do the work. The more strong proof you have, the less copy you need to write.
- Bullet close. Bullets of painful problems for problem-driven buyers, or bullets of the dream for desire-driven buyers.
- New-idea close. Introduce one powerful idea not mentioned elsewhere, woven into the dream. More advanced; use only when you can explain it in a few sentences and know it resonates.
- Cost-of-inaction close. Extend the close and write out the daily negative consequences, especially when those consequences are painful and not covered earlier on the page.
- Long close. Reserve for pages that already run long and have held attention for a while.

### Craft
- Study examples for ideas. Do not copy them word for word. Use the concepts in your own tone and words.
- Reference earlier parts of the page (your named mechanism, a phrase you coined) so the close ties back to the rest of the copy.
- You can test your way into the best close over time (for example as the last email before the close-cart email in a launch), then put the winner on the page.

## Examples

- **Friendly, objection-first (Ship 30 For 30):** "One last thing… If you're down here, you might be thinking… is this really worth it? And sure, you could go and stitch together all of the glorious free content… But I'm guessing that if you're on this page, and you've read this far, you've tried something like this before. And the results… well… they're not what you've been hoping for… wouldn't it be magical to look back at the end of 2026, pointing to this exact moment… So, what do you say?" — why it works: it opens with a compelling headline, names the "is this worth it?" objection, then the "I could do this myself" objection (and gently points out they've likely already tried and it didn't work), taps a little envy at others progressing, and ends on the audience's achievable desires.

- **Rollercoaster with personal and development story (Intentional Life Design):** "Stop Waiting for 'Some Day'… 'Someday, I'll start tapping into my potential'… Here's a cold hard fact of life: One day, you'll be out of 'somedays.'… Two years ago, I left an unfulfilling career… I struggled with self-doubt… But over time, I developed the clarity, strategy, and accountability to make it happen… The systems within Build an Intentional Life have made it possible for me, and they can make it possible for you, too… Are you ready to turn 'someday' into 'today'?" — why it works: it opens on a familiar habit (daydreaming) with specific "somedays," twists the knife ("you'll be out of somedays"), softens with a relatable personal story (struggle then results), moves into the development story of why the program exists, and ends uplifting. A clean model of the rollercoaster that ends on a high.

- **Future pacing and a ladder of desires (A-Z Podcast Launch Plan):** "Still here, wondering whether to make a move? Hey, I get it. All the results I'm talking about probably seem far away or straight-up impossible right now. Your imposter syndrome is probably going into overdrive… So let me break it down for you. Because in reality… it's all just a series of steps… four weeks from now, you launch your podcast… consistent content… consistent traffic… consistent leads… consistent sales… money in the bank… credibility… ALL OF THAT STARTS RIGHT HERE, WITH A CLICK OF THIS PINK BUTTON." — why it works: it addresses the "too good to be true" objection and imposter syndrome, reassures that the result is just a series of steps, future-paces a clear journey, ladders one desire into the next, and ends with a single clear nudge.

- **Short close, cost of inaction plus guarantee (Beat Your Best Ad):** "P.S. Every day that you stick with a 'pretty good' ad is a day that you're literally throwing money away… 'this far' isn't where you want to stay, is it? For $247, you can find out exactly how much money you've been leaving on the table. If I'm wrong? Keep everything, pay nothing. If I'm right? You just bought yourself a direct path to more sales & fatter profit margins. Your call." — why it works: it opens on the negative consequence of staying put, adds curiosity, reiterates the guarantee, shifts to the desire, and nudges a decision. Proof that a short close still works on a moderately long page.

- **Empowering and values-driven (AI Dream Team):** "YOU WERE BORN FOR THIS MOMENT. Dreamer, Visionary, Friend, the choice is yours… you're not just falling behind, you're jeopardizing your future in your industry… But within this challenge lies a tremendous opportunity: To Harness the Power of AI For Good… Now is your moment to make a difference… I wholeheartedly believe you were destined for this moment. AI is reshaping the world. How will it shape yours?" — why it works: an empowering headline and an immediate nudge to choose, then cost of inaction (left behind) contrasted with the opportunity, then addressing fear, then back to "born for this." Note its tone is very different from the others, which is the point: write your close in a tone aligned with your own values.

- **Future pacing for urgency (Employee to Entrepreneur):** "Twelve months from now, you're going to be somewhere. Maybe still… sitting in meetings that make you feel half-alive… Or… you could be here: Waking up when you want… The truth? Every day you wait, you're paying for it. With hours you'll never get back… The next client you could have signed? They're out there right now… hiring someone else… It's this decision. Today." — why it works: it future-paces two paths with vivid before-state details, twists the knife on the daily cost of waiting, adds a sprinkle of urgency, and lands on a decision today. Strong for creating urgency on pages without limited-time enrollment.

- **Strong rollercoaster with vivid specifics (Find Love Offline):** "IF NOT NOW, THEN WHEN? Time Will Keep Passing You By… if you don't start doing something differently in your dating life, you may never find that incredible man… It also means a year from now you will likely be in the same position… Getting food at the grocery store to make a meal for one… Scrolling through your Facebook feed looking at all the pictures of happy couples… But it doesn't have to be that way, if you take action right now." — why it works: a strong twist, dream, twist swing carried by really visual, relatable, specific examples (a meal for one, scrolling happy couples), and it addresses objections toward the end (no matter your age or where you live).

- **Painful headline plus price justification (The Home Studio Blueprint):** "For experts who are tired of being underestimated… your video quality whispers 'amateur'… You've watched competitors with half your talent charge twice your rates… Ring light? Made you look like a ghost… You've spent $1000+ on random gear that's now collecting dust… The Home Studio Blueprint is a proven system… Validated by over 3,000+ entrepreneurs." — why it works: a powerful headline (nobody wants to feel underestimated), strong "already tried" examples, and a punchline ("$1,000 of gear collecting dust") that twists the knife and justifies the price at once, then proof to back it.

- **Short, injustice plus dream (Ambitious Creatives Booked Solid):** "IF YOU'RE STILL HERE I WANT YOU TO KNOW THIS… There are thousands of creatives charging double or triple your rates for poorer work and they are still booked solid… I want to help you have the business and life of your dreams… whether you want ski seasons in the French Alps, time off with your kids… I'm here for all of it!" — why it works: a strong headline that stirs injustice (a real emotion, and we mostly buy on emotion), then it reiterates the dream and ends positive. When the idea is powerful, you do not need to say much.

- **One powerful, relatable idea (Marie Forleo, Time Genius):** "WILL YOU SCROLL THROUGH LIFE? OR LIVE IT? When you look back on your life, will you smile at the time you went after your dreams… Or cringe at the hours you spent chasing 'inbox zero'?… OR YOU CAN TAKE BACK CONTROL… if you don't learn to master your time, it will continue to master you." — why it works: a well-worded, highly relatable idea (stop scrolling your life away), paired with "take back control" and "you have one life, how will you spend it?"

- **Reliable opener, small-investment framing (Second Brain Membership):** "Still on the fence? Think about the time you've spent searching for misplaced information… the frustration, the wasted energy… Now imagine… seamless workflows, clear systems… The Second Brain Membership is a small investment with the potential for an outsized impact… You don't have to figure it all out alone. Let us guide you." — why it works: "Still on the fence?" immediately grabs the hesitating reader, it contrasts current frustration with the imagined smooth state, frames the price as a small investment with outsized impact, and reassures they are not alone.

- **Same opener, simple contrast and a timely window (Annual Review):** "Still on the fence? The coming weeks are the perfect window of time to pause, reflect, and take stock… Once 2026 gets rolling, you know what will happen: your usual routines will return, and the inertia will start building… Don't wait until you feel overwhelmed by the rush of a new year." — why it works: a simple, effective contrast of what usually happens (inertia returns) against what could happen if they join, anchored to a timely window that adds natural urgency.

- **Testimonial-led (Level Up Your Ear):** "Still On The Fence? Have A Look At What Other LUYE Students Say About The Course… [a detailed student email describing exactly what they could not do before and can now do] 'I feel that I learned more from this than from taking ear training lessons… over the course of 4 months.'" — why it works: instead of writing out the close, it lets strong testimonials carry it. The more strong proof you have, the less copy you need.

- **"Don't let it gather dust," shortcut, callback (21-Day Beginner Bass Challenge):** "Don't Let Your Bass Gather DUST. I know you didn't start playing bass just so your instrument could just sit… gathering dust… I want to offer you a shortcut. Rather than trawling through YouTube, gathering 'tip soup' on the Hamster wheel, you can take 20 minutes every day… If you're ready, I'd be honored to have you as a student." — why it works: a great headline (the same "don't let it gather dust" idea as the gear example), it positions the program as a shortcut, and it references earlier parts of the page (the "YouTube Hamster Wheel").

- **Mostly-positive dream close with a personal story (The Decision Maker):** "I'm incredibly excited for you… right now, you have the chance to experience something very few people ever do: Owning your own business… Owning a cleaning company changed everything for me. I went from an unsatisfying office job to owning a thriving company… you owe it to yourself to try The Decision Maker and see what being your own boss feels like… I can't wait to see you inside the course!" — why it works: about 90% of it is positive, painting the dream, carried by the founder's own transformation and a warm invitation. A good fit when the dream is the biggest reason clients buy.

- **New-idea close (Rich Relationships Mastery):** "LET ME LEAVE YOU WITH ONE FINAL THOUGHT: 'Proximity = Power.' Imagine hopping on a plane, and finding yourself in a room of the people you most admire in business?… Proximity to prestige. Proximity to prominence… Proximity = Power. And this is where you'll find it." — why it works: it introduces one fresh, powerful idea woven straight into the dream. More advanced; use it when you have a resonant idea you did not mention elsewhere and can explain in a few short sentences.

- **Bullet close, problem-driven (Fit Mom For Life):** "No More… No more of those 6,8,12 week cookie cutter programs… No more diets and detoxes that are so hard to maintain… No more counting calories and points… It's Time To Step Into The Fit Mom Life We Deserve!" — why it works: it uses bullets of the audience's most annoying problems to grab attention and turns them into a promise (you will no longer experience these). Strong when the problems are especially painful and clients buy mainly to solve them.

- **Bullet close, desire-driven (Product Flywheel Framework):** "Imagine… Having a clear roadmap to not just productize and grow once, but to keep growing… emails coming to your inbox, testimonial after testimonial… notification after notification, about payments clearing, while you're on vacation… it all starts with a decision… then I'm honored to invite you to be a part of our crew." — why it works: it leads with the dream, the first bullets describe it, and the rest nudge the decision and address objections. The mirror image of the problem-driven bullet close, for desire-driven buyers.

- **From-the-heart note with price justification (The Goddess Collective):** "A final note, from yours truly. Congratulations, you've made it to the end of the page!… I don't believe that you are on this page by chance. I believe that you were called here for a reason… The membership works out around $2.20 a day. Meaning for the cost of a gym membership… Above all, you say YES to becoming the woman that you know you've been inside all along." — why it works: it is framed as a heartfelt personal message, includes price justification ($2.20 a day, the cost of a gym membership), and stays in a spiritual tone true to the brand.

- **Course idea plus a personal story from the founder (Altitude, Climbing Technique):** "REACHING YOUR POTENTIAL. While working on the course Dave said: 'without good technique you can't get anywhere near your potential.'… improving your technique can make you a better climber overnight… That's why I was so excited when Dave said yes to creating this course… What I took away from climbing with Dave is just how much fun it can be… I hope to see you on the inside!" — why it works: it references an idea from inside the course and adds a personal story of the platform's founder working with the course author, which builds trust and lifts perceived value.

- **Cost-of-inaction close (Fear of Falling):** "THE HIDDEN COSTS OF FEAR OF FALLING… You Miss Out on Epic Experiences… You Never Know Your True Potential… Ironically, You Increase Your Risk of Injury… Which of these hidden costs are you willing to pay? If you decide not to work on fear of falling now, what will change for you?" — why it works: it is built entirely around the costs of not solving the problem, with relatable specifics and real student quotes, and ends on pointed questions. Extend a close like this when the daily negative consequences are strong and not covered earlier on the page.

- **Long close: development story, rollercoaster, timeframe ladder (Analytics Academy):** "Two weeks from now, how good will your case math be?… if you keep doing what you're doing, it's probably not going to be very good, is it?… you CAN learn this skill… [a meme breaks up the page] Two weeks from now, your case math skills will be drastically better. Two months from now, you might have a new dream job. Two years from now, your career will have skyrocketed… So, what choice will you make today?" — why it works: it combines a development story, twisting the knife, and the dream; a meme grabs attention and breaks up the text; and a specific timeframe ladder (two weeks, two months, two years, twenty years) makes the payoff concrete. A model for a long close on a long page.

- **Understated, missed-opportunity, "what if" (Ramit Sethi, How to Talk to Anybody):** "Why now? It would be easy to put this off… After all, you're doing fine… And that's exactly the point… every day you don't learn how to control the world's perceptions of you, you're losing out… What life-changing opportunities have you already missed…? We all have a 'What if…?' regret in our lives… Which person will you be?… Why not give yourself every advantage you can?" — why it works: it does not over-dramatize the problem ("you're doing fine"), then introduces missing out on opportunities you will never even know about, which sparks curiosity, and uses the "what if…" regret as the nudge while showing how easy it is to act.

- **Long close: value-lift and "you're not alone" (Mental Mastery):** "Some people choose to be stuck. Others choose Mental Mastery. We spend most of our lives going through our toughest challenges alone… if we JUST got help on the biggest 4-5 sticking points in our lives, we could soar… You don't have to do this alone… These aren't one-time techniques… these are the deep inner skills embodied by everyone who has 'won' the game… A year from now, you're going to be a year older. What are you going to do?" — why it works: a brilliant headline that frames being stuck as a choice, a close built on "you don't have to do this alone" (which the audience is guilty of), and a clear lift in perceived value (these are deep, lifelong skills, not one-time techniques), ending on future pacing.

## Scoring criteria

1. **Close present and appropriate:** Is there a distinct closing section, and does the product warrant one? MET when a close is present on a mid-ticket or flagship-type page. Treat as not-relevant for short, physical-product, or low-ticket pages (around $27) where a close can feel like overselling. (product-dependent)
2. **Speaks to the fence-sitter and the "is now the right time?" objection:** (the one job) Does it directly address the interested-but-hesitating reader AND the timing question (why now, why not wait)? This is the close's core job. MET when it clearly does both. PARTIAL when it speaks to the fence-sitter but never tackles timing, or the reverse. MISSING when it does neither.
3. **Re-addresses the audience's real objections:** Beyond timing, does it name and answer at least one genuine objection the audience holds (for example "I could do this myself," "too good to be true," "I've tried before," imposter syndrome)? MET when at least one real objection is named and actually answered. PARTIAL when objections are gestured at but not answered. MISSING when none are addressed.
4. **Reiterates your strongest copy elements:** Does it pull in at least one of your most powerful assets (the guarantee, your best proof or results or testimonials, your unique mechanism or framework)? MET when at least one strong element is clearly reiterated. PARTIAL when one is alluded to only vaguely. MISSING when none are. Set aside only if the product genuinely has no such elements to draw on. (product-dependent)
5. **Creates urgency or names the cost of waiting:** Does it give a real reason to act now, or make the cost of inaction felt? MET when it creates genuine urgency or concretely names the cost of not acting. PARTIAL when it only hints at it weakly. MISSING when there is no why-now and no cost of inaction.
6. **Takes an emotional journey and ends on an uplifting note:** Does it move the reader between their problems and their desired outcomes, and end on a good, uplifting note? (Ending uplifting is a firm rule; the full problem-to-desire swing is the ideal.) MET when it moves between problem and desire AND ends uplifting. PARTIAL when it ends uplifting but stays one-note, or swings but ends flat or low. MISSING when it is flat and ends on a down note.
7. **Clearly nudges a decision and action now:** (the one job) Does it end by clearly prompting the reader to decide and act now (an invitation, a clear choice, a button)? Core job. MET when there is a clear, unmistakable nudge to act now. PARTIAL when the nudge is soft or buried. MISSING when there is no call to decide or act.
8. **Uses future pacing:** (prose only) Does it paint where the reader will be in the future (for example "a year from now…")? (bonus — if met it can raise the score; being missing does NOT count against the score)
9. **Justifies the price where relevant:** Where price is a live objection, does it justify the cost (cost per day, money already wasted on alternatives, or a small-investment-big-impact framing)? MET when it includes a real price justification. PARTIAL when it gestures at value without making the cost feel small or worth it. Set aside when price is not a live objection for this product or offer. (product-dependent)
10. **Uses a proven close format:** (prose only) Does it commit to one of the recognizable, battle-tested close structures: a "still on the fence?" opener, a bullet close (painful problems or the dream), a from-the-heart personal note, or a testimonial-led close? MET when it clearly uses one of these. (Using more than one does not raise it further; the credit is for committing to a proven structure.) (bonus — if met it can raise the score; being missing does NOT count against the score)
