# Personal Transformation Story — Doctrine

**Scope:** Proof element, page-level and section-level. Run it on the whole page (does the page use the author's own story and results as proof) or on the story section itself. Placement judgments need page context.

**Overlap note:** This overlaps About You and Development Story, and often lives inside them. The distinction: About You builds relatability and authority (who you are); Development Story shows how the product was built and tested; this element is specifically the author's own before-and-after and results, used as PROOF that the author can get the reader the outcome. When one passage does two of these jobs, credit it under each and do not double-penalize.

## Principles (rules)

### Purpose
- Your own story, transformation, or results is one of the easiest proof elements to collect, because you already have access to it. Use it to prove you can get the reader the result.
- Show your own before-and-after wherever it fits the product: a fat-loss coach shows their own weight loss, an SEO course shows their own traffic spike, a guitar teacher demonstrates their own playing (especially valuable to a colder audience that does not know you yet).

### What makes it stronger
- Include the before, not just the after. Write about the challenges, struggles, and mistakes you faced, and show proof of the "before" state where you can. This makes you relatable ("you're just like me") and makes the after believable. Show the good AND the bad.
- Include specific results, and use specific numbers ("$19,070 in a month," not "about $20k"; "$344,523 in 5 days," not "$300k"). Specific numbers are more believable.
- Include your thoughts and emotions along the way (the specific things you thought and how they made you feel), which is what makes a story feel personal and lived.
- Use visuals and Show Don't Tell: relatable or funny photos, screenshots, results, growth graphs. These grab attention and prove the "before" and "after" without you having to claim them.
- Stack testimonials on top of your story. If you transition with "since I solved this, I've helped hundreds do the same," follow it immediately with strong testimonials.

### Placement and flow
- A common, natural flow places the personal transformation after the problem and the dream: once you have described the reader's problem and the better world they want, the transition "I understand where you're at, because not long ago I was in the exact same place" basically writes itself.
- You can also open the page with your own struggle and then flip to the reader ("if you've ever felt the same way..."), or, if you make a big bold claim at the top, drop in some of your personal results as instant proof and tell more of the story later.
- This is one of the more artistic parts of copywriting. Sprinkle different parts of your story where they serve the flow; test different placements to see which reads best.

## Examples

- **Struggle-first origin story, woven through the page (How to Talk to Anybody):** "I was socially awkward. I would say the wrong thing at the wrong time..." with specific relatable moments (checking email in the corner for 20 minutes at an event) and self-deprecating photos ("Yes, this guy has a show on Netflix"). — why it works: it leads with the before and the exact awkward thoughts, which makes the author relatable, and the photos prove the past awkwardness through Show Don't Tell.

- **Before-habits list with an after photo (Fit Moms 4 Life):** "I used to do hours upon hours of cardio... I used to try to eat less and less... I was frustrated that I saw NO changes," with a photo of the author fit and with her kids. — why it works: the after (fit, and a mom) is strong proof. Caution: it would be even stronger with a "less fit" before photo, which would prove the before and add relatability. Show the before as well as the after where you can.

- **Claim backed instantly by a personal result (Behind the Sales Page):** a top-of-page claim ("6- and 7-figure sales pages, for years"), immediately followed by a specific example ("this Finisher's Formula page earned $344,523 in less than a week"). — why it works: a big claim is backed at once with a specific, oddly-precise number, which is more believable than a round figure. (Note: this is lighter proof, no revenue screenshots, and it works here because the author is already widely known.)

- **Before-and-after with a growth graph and metrics (Part-Time YouTuber Academy, Ali):** "I started with 0 views, 0 subscribers, $0," then a growth graph climbing to 1M subscribers and $1M revenue, plus book, revenue, and lifestyle markers, and a line of emotion ("I look forward to Mondays"). — why it works: it is short, visual, specific, and stacks multiple proof elements (metrics, a graph, credibility markers) with a touch of emotion.

- **After-results with an objection handled and a revenue screenshot (Beloved Membership):** "my own membership earned $250K+ in the first 12 months... and I did that without a massive audience," backed by a Stripe dashboard screenshot. — why it works: it leads with specific results, pre-answers the "I don't have a big audience" objection, and proves the numbers with a raw screenshot.

- **Personal, emotional, with mistakes to avoid (A-Z Podcast Launch Plan):** a warm, specific voice ("corporate dropout, Shiraz lover..."), the specific fearful thoughts before launching, real results ("#1 in the Apple Podcasts business charts," "1.2 million downloads"), and the costly mistakes made along the way. — why it works: it uses real storytelling (specific thoughts and feelings), specific results, and honest mistakes, which makes it relatable and sets up the product as the shortcut past those mistakes.

- **Concise results with badges (Book Launch Hero):** a tight checklist of the author's own book results ("USA Today Bestseller," "sold over 4k copies in preorders and its first week"), a photo, and award badges. — why it works: it shares real personal results very concisely, with visual badges as proof.

## Scoring criteria

1. **Has a personal transformation story:** Does the page use the author's own story, transformation, or results as proof? MET when there is a clear personal transformation used as proof. PARTIAL when the author is mentioned but no real transformation or result is shown. MISSING when there is none. (product-dependent — set aside only where the author genuinely has no relevant personal transformation, for example a brand or team with no single founder-story; never invent one.)
2. **Includes both the before and the after:** Does the story cover both the before state (the struggle) and the after (the result), rather than only the end result? MET when both are clear. PARTIAL when the before is thin (mostly the after). MISSING when only the after is shown.
3. **Includes specific results:** Are there specific, concrete results, ideally with specific numbers rather than round or vague ones? MET when the results are specific and concrete. PARTIAL when present but vague or rounded. MISSING when there are no real results.
4. **Includes the author's thoughts and emotions:** Does the story include the author's real thoughts and emotions along the way, not just events? MET when the inner experience is present and felt. PARTIAL when named but flat. MISSING when it is a dry recounting of facts.
5. **Includes the challenges or obstacles faced:** Does it include the challenges, obstacles, or mistakes the author faced (not just a smooth rise)? MET when real struggle is shown. PARTIAL when hinted at. MISSING when it is all success with no struggle.
6. **Shows proof of the before state:** Is there visual proof of the "before" state (screenshots, photos)? (visual — audit must ask the user) (product-dependent — set aside where a before cannot be visually proven; note that showing it strengthens relatability and belief.)
7. **Shows proof of the results:** Is there visual proof of the results (screenshots, photos, a growth graph)? (visual — audit must ask the user)
