# Twisting the Knife — Doctrine

## Principles (rules)

### Purpose and placement
- This is the problem section: the problem(s) your audience faces that your product solves. Its job is to make the reader feel the pain of their problem so sharply that they need a solution.
- It flows differently depending on the lead. After a Problem-Solution lead, it is a natural extension of the lead (describe the problem, twist the knife, then transition to the solution). After a Promise lead, the framing flips: "this is the outcome you want (the promise), but there is a problem in the way."
- The core move: outline the audience's core problems and build them one on top of another, showing how one problem leads to the next and how they damage the reader's life, while carrying real emotion, until the reader feels a knife being twisted in their gut.

### What to call out
- Call out the biggest, most common problem(s) your product solves. Lead with the strongest, most relatable ones and move to less common ones after (most common to least common).
- Pull every problem from research, in the audience's exact words, rather than your own tidy summaries. The closer the language sits to what they actually say, the more they feel understood.
- Give each problem depth. A strong problem point combines three things: what is happening to them ("your Google Drive is a mess"), the feeling it creates ("you feel overwhelmed"), and the consequence ("you waste hours trying to remember where you saved a file").
- Describe the negative consequences: how the problem bleeds into the rest of their life, and what happens if they never solve it. This is where you raise the stakes.
- Pinpoint the core emotion and write to it. Move past intellectual problem-listing into what the reader actually feels.

### How to open it
- A proven default opener is "Do any of these sound familiar?" (or "Does this sound familiar?"). If you are unsure how to start the section, this is a safe, strong choice.
- Other strong openers: a direct emotional quote from your audience as the headline ("I'm smart and successful, but I don't feel confident charging a higher fee"), a research-credibility frame ("We asked thousands of language learners about their biggest barrier..."), or a bold claim ("All beginner writers face the same 10 problems"). Use a bold-claim opener only when your research confirms the audience will agree with the problems.

### Format and reassurance
- Bullets are common and effective, and they can carry depth. The strongest problem sections bold or headline the key problem, then expand it with the emotion behind it, the reader's own thoughts, or the consequence.
- Include a "you're not alone / you're in the right place" reassurance ("Sounds familiar? You're not alone," or "I get emails like this every day"). It tells the reader other people share this exact problem, and have come out the other side.

### Proof and visuals
- Real audience quotes shown as screenshots make the copy "write itself" and add believability. A resonant visual (the logos of the tools they juggle, a chart of the beginner's journey from excitement to a dusty guitar in the corner) can carry the same idea with more impact in the same space (Show, Don't Tell).

### Transition
- End with a smooth tease into the next section ("We promise there is a better way," "But before we fix this, there is something sinister at play"). This keeps the reader moving from the problem toward the solution.

## Examples

- **Proven default opener plus a visual and a transition (Notion Mastery):** "Any of these sound familiar?" over bullets ("you feel scattered across a ton of different tools," "things often feel like they're falling through the cracks," "you waste a ton of time trying to remember where you've saved your files"), then a row of app logos, then "We promise there is a better way." — why it works: it uses the safe, strong "sound familiar?" opener, gives depth-carrying bullets (what is happening plus the feeling plus the consequence), shows the chaos with a logo visual, and hands the reader to the solution.

- **Raw research quotes as bullets (Fat Loss Extreme):** "These frustrations sound familiar?" over verbatim customer quotes ("No matter what I try, I can't get this weight off," "I walk in the gym and I'm not even sure where to start," "If I had a road map I'd gladly put in the work"). — why it works: the bullets are lifted straight from customer research, so the section practically writes itself and every reader finds themselves in at least one line.

- **Ultra-short, as the whole lead (The Conqueror Virtual Challenge):** "Does this sound familiar?" over six tight bullets ("you start a fitness program full of motivation, then quit after 2 weeks," "you've tried everything, but nothing sticks"). — why it works: on a very short page with a strong offer to a warm audience, a tight problem section can carry the lead by itself. For most pages, go deeper.

- **Bold-claim opener with framing (Ship 30 For 30):** "All beginner writers face the same 10 problems," with the problems circled on the right (procrastination, perfectionism, impostor syndrome) and framing on the left ("chances are one of these is keeping you up at night... which is why you're on this page... writing is something that's been on your list for a while"). — why it works: a confident bold-claim opener works when you truly know the audience (they have 10,000+ students), and the left-side framing adds the reader's thoughts, the consequences, and reassurance that they are in the right place.

- **Headline as a direct audience quote plus "you're not alone" (Lean In Make Bank):** "I'm smart and successful, but I don't feel confident charging a higher fee." then "Sounds familiar? You're not alone," over belief-driven bullets ("you constantly got the message that making money is greedy"). — why it works: opening on a real, emotional quote states the core problem in the audience's own voice, and "you're not alone" reassures the reader others share it and have moved past it.

- **Emotion-led (Time Genius):** "Are you tired of feeling..." over feeling-first bullets ("constantly exhausted, overwhelmed, and stressed out?", "ashamed by how much time you waste on social media?", "like you're missing out on precious time with the people you love?"). — why it works: it leads with emotions in both the headline and the bullets, moving past intellectual problem-solving into what the reader actually feels.

- **Research-credibility opener (Fluent in 3 Months):** "We've asked thousands of language learners about their biggest barriers to speaking a new language. Here's what they've told us:" over real quotes ("I'm scared of making mistakes and looking stupid"). — why it works: framing the problems as things thousands of real learners said adds credibility and proof, and implies deep understanding, which can land harder than "does this sound familiar?"

- **Bullets with real depth (About Page and Bio Builder):** bolded problems, each expanded ("You hate talking about yourself. You can talk about what you do all day long, but when it comes to yourself, you get tongue-tied and overwhelmed," "You're confused by mixed messages. You heard 'your About page isn't about YOU,' but then how is it an About page?"). — why it works: instead of staying on the surface, each bolded problem expands into the emotion, the reader's thought, or the consequence, which is what a strong problem section does.

- **Short, escalating, names the enemy (Unignorable):** "Building an audience is f*cking hard. You've likely spent hours painstakingly crafting posts you hoped would go viral... Your post got 5 views. Being ignored is downright soul-crushing. It's not your fault though. You've been lied to by the audience growth experts... There is a better way." — why it works: it is short and concise, and it uses vivid language, creates a painful moment (5 views after all that work), escalates to the knife-twist ("soul-crushing"), names the enemy, and teases the fix. Every word earns its place.

- **Visual storytelling and the knife-twist (Find Love Offline):** "Is this you: 'Where are all the good men?'... It's Saturday night. You're home alone... Your ex-boyfriend is engaged... You finally admit it: being single feels lonely. Really lonely. And it's not fair... You've tried all the online dating sites. All the new apps. But still, nothing... You're missing opportunities to attract love, but it's not your fault. There is something sinister at play." — why it works: it uses visual storytelling and the reader's exact thoughts and emotions, twists the knife ("really lonely," "it's not fair"), acknowledges what they have already tried, and ends on a curiosity hook into the next section.

- **"You're not alone" via real emails, then a deeper beat (Strict Pull Up Strength):** "Every day I get emails from athletes who feel stuck... 'Just one strict pull-up seems like a million miles away.'... Not an inch of progress. And failing sucks." then a second headline "No Progress = No Fun" with deeper consequences ("muscle ups seem so far away"). — why it works: it establishes "you're not alone" with real emails and exact quotes, twists the knife ("not an inch of progress," "failing sucks"), then goes deeper into the consequences under a fresh headline.

- **Show, don't tell, with a visual (Learn, Practice, Play):** a "Typical Journey of a Beginner Guitarist" chart plotting progress over time through five stages, ending at "guitar sat in the corner, gathering dust." — why it works: it conveys the whole painful arc visually in the space of a few bullets, which grabs attention and lands the problem harder than text alone (great for a shorter page).

- **Strong body, weak headline (The Starrett System, a caution):** a too-clever "Let's keep it 100" headline, then a strong body ("you're driven, ambitious, but right now your body's getting the short end of the stick," problem bullets, ending on "but the results didn't last, and the time commitment? Unmanageable"). — why it works, and the caution: the body sets up the audience, names the desired outcome first, then the problems and consequences, and ends on a painful knife-twist. The caution: the headline is clever rather than clear, which is the one thing to fix. Choose clear over clever for the section headline.

## Scoring criteria

1. **Calls out the biggest, most common problems, strongest first:** Does the section name the biggest, most common problems the product solves, leading with the most relatable ones? MET when it clearly names the core problems and leads with the strongest. PARTIAL when it raises real but secondary problems, or buries the strongest. MISSING when it names no clear, relevant problem.
2. **Pulled from research, in the audience's exact language:** Do the problems read as the audience's actual words, rather than your tidy summaries? MET when the language is specific and recognizable (ideally verbatim). PARTIAL when accurate but generic. MISSING when it only summarizes in your own words.
3. **Opens with a proven hook:** Does it open with a strong, proven hook (a "does this sound familiar?" line, a direct audience quote, a "we asked thousands..." credibility frame, or a bold claim the audience will agree with)? MET when it uses a clear, strong opener. PARTIAL when the opener is flat or generic. MISSING when there is no real hook.
4. **Each problem has depth (what is happening, the feeling, the consequence):** Do the problems go beyond a surface list, combining what is happening to them, the feeling it creates, and the consequence? MET when the problems consistently carry this depth. PARTIAL when some are deep and others are flat labels. MISSING when it stays a shallow list.
5. **Describes the negative consequences and the cost of not solving it:** Does it show how the problem bleeds into the rest of their life and what happens if they never fix it? MET when the wider life-impact and the future cost are clear. PARTIAL when hinted at weakly. MISSING when there are no stakes.
6. **Escalates to a real knife-twist:** (the one job) Does it build problem on problem and rise to a genuinely painful peak, rather than staying flat? This is the section's signature move. MET when it clearly escalates to a felt low point. PARTIAL when it builds a little but stays mild. MISSING when it is a flat list with no build.
7. **Pinpoints the core emotion and writes emotionally:** Does the section carry real emotion tied to one core feeling, rather than just logical problem-listing? MET when the core emotion is clear and felt. PARTIAL when emotion is named but flat. MISSING when it is purely intellectual.
8. **Includes "you're not alone" reassurance:** Does it reassure the reader that others share this exact problem (and that they are in the right place)? MET when the reassurance is clearly present. PARTIAL when only faintly implied. MISSING when absent. (A near-universal element; include it unless the section format genuinely has no room.)
9. **Real audience quotes or a resonant visual:** Does the section use real audience quotes shown as screenshots, or a resonant visual (the tools they juggle, a journey chart)? (visual — audit must ask the user) (bonus — if met it can raise the score; being missing does NOT count against the score)
10. **Transitions smoothly into the next section:** Does it end by teasing a better way and handing the reader to the next section? MET when there is a clear, smooth forward tease. PARTIAL when the handoff is abrupt. MISSING when it ends flat with no bridge. (Set aside only if this section is genuinely the end of the page.)
