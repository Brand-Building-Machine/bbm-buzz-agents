# Bonuses — Doctrine

## Principles (rules)

### Purpose
- After the product, how it works, the features, and the curriculum, cover the bonuses. Bonuses raise the perceived value of the offer, which raises conversions. High-converting pages usually have 3 to 7 bonuses. This is a must-have section.
- Bonus creation never really ends. Watch for client feedback (sticking points you can solve with a bonus) and market trends (for example, AI tools), and keep adding or improving bonuses over time.

### What a bonus can be, and the angle it serves
- A bonus can be an extra video, mini-course, or masterclass; a PDF, guide, book, worksheet, template, script, or tool; transcripts, slides, or audio; a community; group coaching, expert interviews, teardowns, or Q&As (or recordings of them); or lifetime updates.
- Every bonus needs a benefit. The three most useful angles to message on are: addressing a common objection, easier implementation (removing a sticking point), and faster or better results.

### How to write the section
- Start with an intro: a headline (ideally naming the number of bonuses), and a line or two explaining how many bonuses there are and how they help (framing them around the reader's desires, like "unfair advantages," works well).
- Then cover each bonus: mark it clearly ("Bonus #3"), give it a compelling name, and write a benefit-driven headline that speaks to the objection, result, or problem it solves. The copy should explain what the bonus is, how it works, and its main benefit. Add a graphic or screenshot.

### Bonus values, done believably
- Including a monetary value for each bonus (and a total) can raise conversions, but only if the values are believable. Over-inflated values ("this $27 product comes with $1,497 of bonuses") make readers cringe and can lower conversions.
- The rule: ask what price a client would actually pay for this bonus if you sold it on its own. Would someone pay $500 for real legal advice? Yes. Would they pay $500 for a short PDF of headline tips? No. Use your honest judgment.

## Examples

- **Objection-addressing bonus (Part-Time YouTuber, "Video Editing with Final Cut Pro X"):** a mini-course that answers the objection "I don't know how to edit YouTube videos." — why it works: it turns a common objection into a bonus, so a fence-sitter with that exact worry feels covered.

- **Objection bonus with a believable value (Ship 30, "[BONUS #2] Become Known For A Niche You Own, $299 Value"):** answers "I don't know what to write about." — why it works: it addresses the objection and attaches a value the reader would plausibly pay.

- **Implementation and objection bonuses with values (Employee to Entrepreneur):** "Bonus #1: The Side Business Time Machine" (answers "I'm too busy"), "Bonus #6: Legal Made Simple" (removes the legal sticking point, value $500). — why it works: each bonus names what it is, how it works, and the benefit, with a believable value (real legal advice from an attorney is genuinely worth $500).

- **Better-results bonus (Weekend Woodworker, "How to sell woodworking projects online"):** helps you make money from your woodworking, not just get better at it. — why it works: it takes the client beyond the core promise to a bigger result.

- **Clear what/problem/benefit (iPhone Photo Academy, "Analysis of 459 iPhone photos"):** what it is (a video analysis of 459 photos), the problem (making the same mistakes), the benefit (learn from others' mistakes so you don't repeat them). — why it works: short copy that says exactly what the bonus is and how it helps.

- **Intro framing tied to desires (Employee to Entrepreneur):** "Your ETE Bonus Arsenal... 7 strategic 'unfair advantages,' each built to help you get clients faster, look like a pro sooner, and skip months of trial and error." — why it works: it frames the bonus stack around the reader's desires rather than listing them coldly.

## Scoring criteria

2. **Includes 3 to 7 bonuses:** Does it include a healthy number of bonuses (3 to 7 is the sweet spot)? MET when it lands in that range. PARTIAL when there are only one or two, or so many it feels padded. MISSING when there are no bonuses.
3. **Has an intro that frames the bonuses:** Is there an intro explaining how many bonuses there are and how they help, ideally tied to the reader's desires? MET when the intro frames them well. PARTIAL when it is a bare headline. MISSING when there is no intro at all.
4. **The bonuses are purposeful:** Do the bonuses serve real angles (addressing an objection, easing implementation, or driving faster/better results), rather than feeling random? MET when the bonuses are clearly purposeful. PARTIAL when some are and some feel like filler. MISSING when they feel random or irrelevant.
5. **Each bonus is named, explained, and carries its benefit:** For every bonus, is there a compelling name, a clear what-it-is, and the reason it matters to the reader? MET when all three hold for every bonus. PARTIAL when some bonuses are fully sold and others are bare labels. MISSING when the bonuses are a list of names with no explanation or benefit.
8. **Includes believable monetary values:** (violation check) Does it include a monetary value for each bonus and a total, kept believable rather than over-inflated? MET when values are present and believable. PARTIAL when values are present but one or two feel inflated, or when values are absent (a missed opportunity). MISSING when the values are clearly over-inflated in a way that erodes trust.
9. **Each bonus has a graphic or screenshot:** Does each bonus have a graphic or screenshot next to it? (visual — audit must ask the user)
