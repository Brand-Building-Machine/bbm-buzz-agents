# Offer Introduction — Doctrine

## Principles (rules)

### Purpose
- The Offer Introduction introduces the product on a medium-to-long page. It is a tease of the key information, not the full explanation. The deeper "How it Works" and "What You'll Learn" sections come after.
- The test for a good Offer Introduction: if someone reads only this section, do they know exactly what the product is and how it works? Could they explain it to a friend over coffee? If yes, it has done its job.

### Structure
- Start with an "Introducing..." line, the product name, a subheadline, and some intro copy.
- The subheadline usually carries the core promise, plus some of: additional benefits or positive consequences, the biggest objections ("even if..."), and the unique mechanism or system.
- The intro copy can describe what the product is (a 6-week course, a membership, an app), briefly cover what is included, reiterate the core promise, name the key benefits, address objections, call out the ideal audiences, and add hooks that keep the reader reading. Include the ones that fit your audience and where your product is strongest.
- You can add product visuals (screenshots) as an extra hook.

### Length and formula
- Keep it brief: usually a paragraph or two per element, and the whole section between half a page and one page. It is a tease.
- This is one of the few sections where it is fine to be more formulaic. If unsure, focus on three things: what the product is (and how it works), the core promise and benefits, and the biggest objections.

## Examples

- **Textbook, a bit of everything (Weekend Woodworker):** "Introducing The Weekend Woodworker. Build unique creations with your own hands even with limited tools, limited space, and limited time. The Weekend Woodworker is the only program of its kind that walks you step-by-step... a six-week online woodworking course..." — why it works: it hits everything in order (the Introducing line and name, a subheadline with the core promise and the objections, a description of what it is, what it covers, the ideal audiences, a hook like "beginner projects that don't look like beginner projects," and how the lessons work).

- **Short and tight (Adam Ondra Teaches Lead Climbing):** "Adam Ondra Teaches Lead Climbing. A course designed to help you climb harder routes without the need to get stronger. In over 50 detailed lessons across 7 modules, Adam shows you how..." — why it works: the subheadline says what it is, the core promise, and the objection (without getting stronger), and the copy covers the format (50 lessons, 7 modules) and the benefits.

- **A vivid analogy plus a strong promise (Master Market Sizing):** "Imagine if you could sit next to Julio and me for 20+ hours and learn everything we know about Estimations and Market Sizing... techniques that have taken candidates from the bottom 10% to the top 10% in just a few days." — why it works: it describes the experience with a vivid analogy (sitting next to us for 20 hours) and a strong, specific visual promise (bottom 10% to top 10%).

- **Short and objection-led (Employee to Entrepreneur):** "The Employee to Entrepreneur System. It's not just another online course. It's a battle-tested method for quitting your job and building a business... Even if you're not a natural at marketing, you don't have a business idea yet, or you've tried before and it didn't work." — why it works: short and sweet on a long page, it leads with the objection this burned audience holds (not just another course) and stacks the "even if" objections.

## Scoring criteria

*Numbering is stable across revisions: a missing number is a criterion deliberately removed, not an error. Judge only the criteria listed.*

2. **Compelling subheadline:** Is there a compelling subheadline that captures the core promise (plus benefits, the mechanism, and/or the biggest objections, as fits)? MET when the subheadline clearly carries the core promise and more. PARTIAL when it is present but flat. MISSING when there is no real subheadline.
3. **Names the product and clearly describes what it is:** Is the product clearly named, and does the copy say plainly what it is (a 6-week course, a membership, an app)? MET when the format is clear. PARTIAL when vague. MISSING when the reader cannot tell what it is.
4. **Describes what it includes:** Does it describe what the product includes (the system, modules, scripts, what the reader gets)? MET when what is included is clear. PARTIAL when thin. MISSING when it is not described.
5. **Includes additional promises or core benefits:** Does it include additional promises or the key benefits? MET when the benefits are clear. PARTIAL when thin. MISSING when there are none.
6. **Addresses the biggest objections:** Does it address the biggest objections (in the subheadline or the copy)? MET when the core objections are addressed. PARTIAL when only lightly. MISSING when none are. (product-dependent — set aside only for a product with no meaningful objections.)
7. **Calls out the ideal audiences:** Does it call out who the product is for? MET when the ideal audience is clearly named. PARTIAL when only implied. MISSING when there is no audience call-out.
8. **Passes the "explain it over coffee" test:** (the one job) After reading only this section, would the reader know exactly what the product is and how it works, well enough to explain it to a friend? MET when the answer is clearly yes. PARTIAL when they would get the gist but miss key parts. MISSING when they would still be confused about what it is.
9. **Stays a brief tease:** Is it kept brief (roughly half a page to a page), teasing the product rather than fully explaining how it works (which comes later)? MET when it teases without over-explaining. PARTIAL when it starts to over-dump. MISSING when it tries to explain everything here.
10. **Section design signals a new section and grabs attention:** Does the design make it clear this is a new section, and does it grab attention? (visual — audit must ask the user)
11. **Product visual or screenshots as a hook:** (prose only) Is there an attention-grabbing visual of the product (screenshots)? (visual — audit must ask the user) (bonus — if met it can raise the score; being missing does NOT count against the score)
12. **Additional hooks that keep the reader reading:** (prose only) Does it include extra hook points that encourage the reader to keep going? (bonus — if met it can raise the score; being missing does NOT count against the score)
