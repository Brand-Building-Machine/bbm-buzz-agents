# Subheadline — Doctrine

## Principles (rules)

### Purpose and its bond with the headline
- The subheadline's primary job is to get the reader to read the first line of the copy (the lead). That is the one thing it must do.
- Its secondary job is to make the headline itself stronger and raise the reader's interest, hooking them further.
- The usual method: say the things you could not fit in the headline. Whatever you left out of the headline, add it in the subheadline (proof, specifics, deeper curiosity, or an objection handled).
- The headline and subheadline are one unit. The subheadline exists only to strengthen the headline and carry the reader into the lead, so it cannot be understood or judged on its own.

- **Audit dependency (important): a subheadline cannot be judged alone.** Because the subheadline's whole job is defined by the headline, you need both to assess it. If the user gives you only the subheadline, do NOT score it yet. Gently ask them to share the headline too, and explain plainly why: the subheadline only works in relation to the headline, so you need both to give the best possible read. Wait for the headline before scoring. Never assume, guess, or invent the headline.

### The three moves (a MENU to choose from, NOT a checklist to run through)
Pick the ONE move that fits what the headline is doing; occasionally two. **This is a menu, not a sequence — do NOT force all three in** (same as `headline.md`'s "not all apply every time" and `close.md`'s "do not force all of them in"). The subheadline has ONE job: get the reader into the lead. Every move you add is more words the reader must cross before they arrive, so a move that does not clearly serve that one job is making the subheadline worse, not more complete. When in doubt: fewer moves, shorter.
- **Headline makes a promise, subheadline adds proof or specifics.** Back the promise with proof (numbers, track record, expert or social proof) and specific detail (exact outcomes, what the reader will do or get, positioning against weaker alternatives).
- **Headline creates curiosity, subheadline deepens it.** Give a small partial payoff while opening a fresh loop, so the reader is more hooked than before. Naming a unique mechanism is a strong way to do this: it pays off a little (the "secret" has a name) and opens a new question (what is it, how do I get it?).
- **Headline makes a bold claim, subheadline pre-addresses the objection.** A bold claim raises the reader's guard (is this true, will it work for me?). Answer the core objection head-on. Done well, defusing the objection can itself open a new loop (for example, "if you hate traditional networking, you're going to love this").
- Mixing is possible but is the exception, not the goal. A single subheadline CAN name a mechanism, add an outcome, and handle an objection at once — but only when the pair still reads as one idea and stays short enough that the reader actually reaches the lead. Running all three by default produces a ~50-word subheadline nobody finishes, which fails the one job. If you do combine, each added move must earn its words against the only question that matters: does this get them into the lead faster, or slower?

### Use specifics and your named mechanism
- Specific numbers make the pair stronger. "Trusted by 10,000+ students" beats "trusted by thousands." "You're wasting 64% of your ad spend" beats "you're losing money on ads." "Raise ROAS by 50% in just one week" beats "increase your ROAS."
- If you have impressive, real metrics about the product or the outcome, the subheadline is a great place to use them. Never invent a number; use only real ones.
- If the product has a uniquely named mechanism, method, or framework, the subheadline is a strong place to name it, for both curiosity and ownership.

### One core idea, and structure is flexible
- The headline and subheadline together must still read as one core idea. The subheadline expands that idea; it does not wander to a different one.
- The exact structure is flexible. A pair can look a little "messy" (you could argue there are two headlines), and that is fine as long as it flows well and keeps the reader reading. Communicating the idea clearly matters more than a fixed design.
- Objections can be handled in the subheadline, in the headline, or across the pair together. Address what the reader is already thinking, wherever it fits best.
- The eyebrow (the line above the headline) can do extra work too, such as carrying proof or calling out the audience, which frees the headline and subheadline to focus on the promise and the hook.

### Craft
- Test the pair: read the headline and subheadline together, out loud, and ask "do these two lines make me want to read the next line?" If yes, they are doing their job. If not, keep tweaking.
- While testing headlines with your audience, watch for follow-up questions and objections, then fold those objections into the copy.
- If a great subheadline will not come, brainstorm many more (aim for 100). Get the weak ones out, gather inspiration, move things around, and consult your copy and research until one clicks.

## Examples

Each example shows the headline and subheadline together, because the subheadline is judged as a pair with its headline.

**★ On length — read the SHORT ones as the norm, not the long ones.** Most of these subheadlines are one to two lines (roughly 15–25 words): Double Unders (~17), wetracked (~20), Peaceful Dreams (~14) are the default shape. Two run long — **Ship 30 For 30 (~48 words) and Beginner Bass (~38, and it deliberately mixes all three moves) are the EXCEPTIONS, not the target.** They work only because every added element still pulls toward the lead and the pair stays one idea; the length is a cost they just barely earn. Do not anchor on the longest example and treat it as the model — a longer subheadline is a bet you usually lose, and one that runs long because it ran every move has already failed its one job.

- **Promise, then proof and specifics (Ship 30 For 30):** Headline "Start writing online in 30 days." Subheadline "Ship 30 for 30 is a self-paced course that has taught over 10,000 students the fundamentals of digital writing. You won't learn passively, you'll create 30 pieces of published writing in public, build lifelong friendships, and leave with a new perspective for what is possible online." — why it works: the headline is a simple promise; the subheadline adds proof (10,000 students), answers the passive-learning objection, and adds specific outcomes (30 published pieces), all while staying one core idea.

- **Promise, then a guarantee and stacked proof (Double Unders PRO):** Headline "Learn double unders." Subheadline "100% guaranteed. Join the viral company and team of coaches and physiotherapists that's trusted by 1,000+ athletes." — why it works: a plain promise headline, then the subheadline leads with a strong guarantee and stacks two layers of proof (an expert team plus 1,000+ athletes). Without the subheadline, the opening would be much weaker.

- **Bold claim, then core promise and a metric (wetracked.io):** Eyebrow "#1 Highest Rated Ad Tracking Software For Shopify & WooCommerce." Headline "Broken tracking is wasting 64% of your ad spend." Subheadline "The only solution that pushes 100% accurate data into your ads manager. Raise ROAS by 50% in just 1 week." — why it works: the headline is a specific, urgency-driven claim; the subheadline states the core promise (100% accurate data) and a specific outcome (ROAS up 50% in a week), and claims category leadership ("the only"), which the rest of the page must prove. The eyebrow doubles as proof and an audience call-out.

- **Curiosity, then deeper curiosity and a named mechanism (Success Triggers):** Headline "Why are some people 10x, or even 100x, happier, more confident, and more successful than others? What do they know that you don't?" Subheadline "Master the psychological 'Success Triggers' that top performers use to shift their negative thinking into peak performance, natural confidence, and lasting happiness." — why it works: the subheadline gives a quick partial payoff (the secret has a name, "Success Triggers") while opening a fresh loop (what are they, how do I get them?), names the unique mechanism, and promises specific outcomes, so the reader is more hooked and cares more.

- **Curiosity, then a stronger reiteration and a new hook (About Page & Bio Builder):** Headline "The About page is the #2 most visited (and powerful) page on your site. Is yours leaving money on the table?" Subheadline "I'll bet it is. I'll bet it's leaving money on the table, on the floor, and every place but your bank account. Two possible reasons it's leaking out dollars by the day:" — why it works: it restates the core idea more powerfully and opens a new loop (the two reasons), so the reader keeps going. The pair is a little messy (arguably two headlines), and that is fine because it flows and holds the reader.

- **Problem curiosity, then expanded problem and a hint of the fix (Pillars of Productivity):** Headline "Why does your to-do list keep growing, no matter how hard you work?" Subheadline "Distractions, procrastination, FOMO, getting things done is hard these days. We have ALL the digital tools but NONE of the results they've promised us. That's changing now!" — why it works: it expands the problem (names the culprits), adds a sharp frustration (all the tools, none of the results), and hints at the solution ("that's changing now"), which builds curiosity.

- **Bold claim, then an objection turned into a loop (Rich Relationships Mastery):** Headline "The only in-person experience where you master how to build, nurture and activate your $100 million-dollar network, and take your life and business to the next level." Subheadline "This is the room where that happens. (Oh, and if you hate traditional networking, you're going to love this.)" — why it works: the headline makes a huge claim; the subheadline answers a specific objection (hating traditional networking) and turns it into a new open loop (why will I love this?), pulling the reader on.

- **Objection-stacking subheadline (Fit Moms For Life):** Headline "There's hope for moms." Subheadline "who've spent years dieting, cutting out carbs, eating bland, starving themselves, obsessing over the 'cheat day' and doing hours of sweaty and boring cardio without seeing results." — why it works: it stacks the audience's real failed attempts and objections in their own language, which lands hard in a crowded, objection-heavy market. The subheadline also completes the headline's sentence, so the pair is clearly one idea.

- **Promise, then mechanism, outcome, and objections at once (Beginner Bass Challenge):** Headline "This unique bass challenge will help you go from a beginner to competent bassist in 21 days." Subheadline "Using the T-G-E Framework, you'll be able to jam with your friends or family faster than 98% of other bassists, without spending hours trawling through confusing YouTube videos, doing boring exercises or getting 'stuck' and becoming an 'endless beginner'." — why it works: it mixes three moves, a named mechanism (T-G-E Framework) for curiosity, an extra specific outcome (jam faster than 98%), and several objections handled (endless YouTube, boring exercises, getting stuck).

- **Objections handled across the pair (Peaceful Dreams):** Headline "The easy-to-follow guide to better baby sleep with science-backed strategies, fewer tears, and less stress." Subheadline "Gentle, effective solutions for more restful nights and better naps in the first 6 months." — why it works: the pair pre-addresses several objections together (does it work? science-backed; will my baby cry? fewer tears; will it be stressful? less stress; will it work with a newborn? the first 6 months). It shows objections can be handled across the headline and subheadline, not only inside the subheadline.

## Scoring criteria

Score the subheadline as a pair with its headline. If the headline was not provided, do not score (see the audit dependency note in Principles): ask for the headline first.

**★ Criterion 1 (gets the reader into the lead) is the one that always governs; the rest SERVE it and are conditional, not additive.** Criteria 4–6 are headline-angle-dependent — judge only the one matching the headline's angle and set the others aside. Criteria 7–8 are product-dependent — set aside when the product has no real metric or named mechanism. A subheadline does not score better for satisfying MORE of them: a pair that runs every move and buries the lead fails criterion 1, which is the only one that is always the point.

1. **Gets the reader into the lead (the one job):** (the one job) Reading the headline and subheadline together, do they make you want to read the first line of the copy that follows? MET when the pair clearly pulls the reader onward. PARTIAL when it mostly holds interest but sags. MISSING when the pair gives no real reason to keep reading.
2. **Strengthens the headline and adds new material (no mere repetition):** Does the subheadline make the headline stronger by adding something new (proof, specifics, deeper curiosity, an objection handled, an extra outcome), rather than restating the headline in different words? MET when it clearly adds new, relevant substance. PARTIAL when it adds a little but mostly echoes the headline. MISSING when it repeats the headline or adds nothing.
3. **Headline and subheadline stay one core idea:** Do the two lines expand a single core idea together, rather than pulling toward two unrelated ideas? MET when they hold to one idea (a slightly "messy" pair still counts if it flows as one idea). PARTIAL when they mostly cohere but drift. MISSING when the subheadline wanders to a different idea and breaks the flow.
4. **Promise headline, so add proof or specifics:** If the headline makes a promise, does the subheadline back it with proof (numbers, track record, expert or social proof) or specific detail (exact outcomes, what the reader will do or get)? MET when it clearly adds proof or concrete specifics behind the promise. PARTIAL when it gestures at support but stays vague. Judge only when the headline makes a promise; set aside if the headline's angle is different. (headline-dependent)
5. **Curiosity headline, so deepen the curiosity:** If the headline opens a curiosity gap, does the subheadline deepen it (a small partial payoff plus a fresh open loop, a named mechanism, or intriguing specifics), rather than flatly answering it or dropping it? MET when curiosity clearly rises. PARTIAL when it keeps interest but does not build it. Judge only when the headline creates curiosity; set aside otherwise. (headline-dependent)
6. **Bold-claim headline, so pre-address the core objection:** If the headline makes a bold claim (or any claim that raises skepticism), does the subheadline, or the pair together, head off the main objection the reader now has (is it true, will it work for me, what they've already tried and failed)? MET when it names and defuses at least the core objection. PARTIAL when it hints at an objection without really answering it. Judge only when the headline invites skepticism; set aside otherwise. (headline-dependent)
7. **Uses specific numbers or metrics:** If the product has real metrics (counts, percentages, timeframes, results), does the subheadline use the concrete number rather than a vague version ("10,000+ students" over "thousands")? MET when it uses a real, concrete metric. PARTIAL when it references results but keeps them vague where a real number exists. Set aside if the product genuinely has no metric to cite. Never invent a number. (product-dependent)
8. **Names the unique mechanism or framework:** If the product has a uniquely named mechanism, method, or framework, does the subheadline mention it (for curiosity and ownership)? MET when the named mechanism appears. PARTIAL when a mechanism is implied but not named where a name exists. Set aside if the product has no named mechanism. Never invent one. (product-dependent)
