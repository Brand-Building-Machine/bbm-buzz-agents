# Case Studies — Doctrine

**Scope:** Proof element, page-level and section-level. Run it on the whole page (does the page use case studies, and well), or on a specific case-study block. Judging fit often needs whole-page context (which audiences and which claims the case studies need to serve).

## Principles (rules)

### Purpose and when they matter
- A case study is an extended testimonial that tells the full client transformation story. It can run 1 to 2 pages of the sales page.
- The higher the price, the more they matter. The bigger the investment you ask a buyer to make, the more important strong case studies become. Lean on them heavily for higher-ticket products.
- You do not need many. Three strong case studies (ideally one for each target audience you are trying to attract) can move conversions meaningfully. Do not feel you need tens of them.

### What a strong case study includes
- A strong headline with a hook, ideally carrying the transformation or a specific result ("How Brandon lost 40lb and overcame a rut," "5Xed lead acquisition in 3 months").
- Specific, spotlighted results (real numbers: revenue, subscribers, hours saved, pounds lost).
- The full arc where you have it: the before state and struggle, what changed, and the after.
- Stronger proof stacked in: video testimonials, before/after transformations, a growth graph or client journey, photos.
- A short "case study summary" (name, location, their situation, the problem, the success) works well for a scannable format.

### Formats (a menu)
- A full written story (long-form), which you can reuse in a dedicated "case studies" or "results" section elsewhere.
- A tighter combination: a written quote plus a video plus a few specific results.
- A growth graph with milestone callouts along the curve (great for a long-term transformation).
- A case study summary card (name, market, problem, success).
- Case studies combined with other proof, such as client logos plus specific results ("McDonald's, 615% ROI").
- A "Case Study Grid" of clickable cards that pop open the full case study, once you have five or six or more.

### How to collect and build them
- When a client has incredible results or is extremely happy, ask them to record an extended video testimonial on a live call or in person, then repurpose that conversation into a case study (with permission).
- Build order: collect them, start with simple written case studies, add photos, specific results, and videos, then design a growth graph for a strong long-term case study, then build a Case Study Grid once you have several.

## Examples

- **Full written transformation with a photo (MacroFactor, "How Brandon lost 40lb and overcame a rut"):** a headline carrying the transformation, then the before ("bulks led to excess fat gain, cuts lost more muscle than he wanted," a low point after injuries and surgeries) and the after ("the leanest he's ever been," a new discipline). — why it works: it tells the full arc with a real name, a real struggle, and a specific result, which is exactly what a case study does that a short testimonial cannot.

- **Results-led card with a video (Product Flywheel, Zack Arnold):** a video plus stacked results: "96 survey replies in 1 week," "$37,000 launch in a covid-decimated industry," "$200,000+ revenue while working under 20 hours a week." — why it works: it spotlights several concrete results and pairs them with a video, so the proof is both specific and hard to fake.

- **Growth graph with milestones (Part-Time YouTuber Academy, John Coogan):** "made ~35 videos, on 1,000 subscribers when he took PTYA," then a growth graph climbing to 450k+ subscribers with dated callouts, plus a video and result stats (300k+ subscribers in under 2 years, $5k+/month). — why it works: the growth graph makes a long transformation visual and engaging, and the milestone callouts tell the story along the curve.

- **Scannable summary card (Podcast Marketing Academy, Rob Orman):** a video plus a "Case Study Summary" (name, location, show, company, market), then Problem and Success ("5x'ed podcast-driven lead generation in 3 months"). — why it works: the summary format is fast to scan and still carries the problem, the specific result, and a face.

- **Long written case study in the client's voice (Launch Hero, Edward Bell):** "Launch Hero paid for itself immediately... 11 times over! (From a list of just 200 ppl!)," a detailed story of skipping most of the steps and still making bank, plus a later update. — why it works: a long, specific, in-their-own-words story reads as real and undeniable, and the specific numbers (11x, 200 people, $150 product) make it believable.

- **Concise case study (The Starrett System, Andrew):** a short before ("exhausted, stiff, constantly sore after 75 Hard"), a checklist of what the product helped him do (rebuild mobility after 6 knee surgeries, train without burning out), and a quote. — why it works: a case study does not have to be long, a tight before, a bulleted transformation, and a quote can carry it.

- **Case studies combined with client logos (monday.com):** "Leading brands drive efficient growth," then rows pairing a brand, a link to the case study, and a headline result: "McDonald's, 615% ROI," "Holt CAT, 105K hours saved annually," "Canva, 300% saved yearly." — why it works: it stacks recognizable client logos with specific results and links to the full case studies, combining several proof elements at once.

- **Case Study Grid (coaching example):** a grid of clickable cards, each a hook headline ("How Jen 10Xed coaching income in 6 months," "How Rob 5Xed lead acquisition in 3 months") with a "Watch Case Study" button. — why it works: once you have many case studies, a grid lets the reader self-select the story closest to their own situation.

## Scoring criteria

1. **Has enough case studies (aim for 3, or one per target audience):** Are there at least three case studies, or one for each core audience the page targets? MET when there are three or more (or clear coverage of each target audience). PARTIAL when there are one or two. MISSING when there are none. (product-dependent — case studies matter most for higher-ticket products; for a low-ticket product where they genuinely are not expected, note that adding them would still help rather than penalizing heavily.)
2. **Spotlights specific results:** Does each case study spotlight specific, concrete results (real numbers or outcomes), rather than vague praise? MET when the results are specific and stand out. PARTIAL when results are present but vague or buried. MISSING when there are no real results.
3. **Tells the transformation arc:** Does each case study tell the arc (the before or struggle, what changed, the after), rather than a bare quote? MET when the arc is clear. PARTIAL when it is thin (mostly the after). MISSING when it is just a one-line quote with no story.
4. **Each case study has a strong, hook-driven headline:** Does each case study carry a strong headline with a hook, ideally the transformation or the result? MET when each is headlined well. PARTIAL when only some are. MISSING when they are unheadlined blocks.
5. **Stacks stronger proof (video or before/after):** Do the case studies include video testimonials or before-and-after transformations where possible? (visual — audit must ask the user)
6. **Includes a growth graph or client journey:** (prose only) Does a strong long-term case study include a growth graph or a visual client journey? (visual — audit must ask the user) (bonus — if met it can raise the score; being missing does NOT count against the score)
7. **Uses a Case Study Grid where there are many:** (prose only) Where the page has many case studies, are they organized into a scannable, clickable grid? (visual — audit must ask the user) (bonus — if met it can raise the score; being missing does NOT count against the score)
