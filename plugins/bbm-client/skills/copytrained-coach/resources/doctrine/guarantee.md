# Guarantee — Doctrine

## Principles (rules)

### Purpose and when to use
- A guarantee reverses risk. It removes the buyer's fear that the product won't work for them, which is one of the easiest ways to raise conversions. Include a guarantee on the page unless a listed exception applies.
- Use guarantees for physical products, software, digital products, and in-person services.
- Consider leaving a guarantee off only when refunds would be very costly or unfair to the seller:
  - Events or conference tickets, or limited high-end mastermind slots (refunds are extremely costly).
  - 1-on-1 coaching, where the buyer pays for your time and results depend on their own implementation.
  - Done-for-you services with a large time investment (such as building software for someone).
- For most other products, run a guarantee, or at least test one for a few weeks.

### Choose a guarantee type
Each type trades conversion lift against refund risk and buyer quality.
- **No-questions-asked:** refund within a set window, no conditions. Gives the biggest conversion lift. Can be abused. Set a window long enough to fully test the product (commonly 30, 60, or 90 days).
- **Action-based:** refund only if the buyer did the required work and still got no result. Lowers refund rates, raises completion rates, and suits flagship and high-ticket courses. Smaller conversion lift than no-questions-asked. Must state plainly what "doing the work" means (for example, completing the core modules).
- **Personal assistance:** you promise to keep working with the buyer until they reach the result. Attracts serious buyers and reduces refunds while still reversing risk.
- **Result-based:** "get X result or get your money back." The strongest type, and stronger the more impressive and specific the result. Converts very well but needs a proven product to avoid refunds and bad experiences.

Selection guidance:
- Default to no-questions-asked when unsure.
- Move to action-based or personal-assistance if you draw unserious buyers or too many do-no-work refunds.
- Test result-based once the product reliably delivers for the large majority of buyers.
- On a high-ticket page (worth thousands), you may stack more than one guarantee to answer the objections of different buyers.

### What a strong guarantee includes
Write toward these:
- An attention-grabbing headline that does more than name a refund. Frame it around trying the product risk-free or loving the product, and match it to the brand's tone (for example, "100-day love it guarantee"). Keep it clear and easy to read in one pass. Do not stuff a long product name, a superlative, and "guarantee" into one clunky label; if a headline is awkward or hard to parse, simplify it even when the underlying idea is good.
- A personal tone, in the seller's own voice.
- Clear terms: how the guarantee works and what the buyer must do to qualify.
- Clear instructions for claiming a refund (the exact step, such as the email address to write to).
- Language that makes the buyer feel safe and protected, and that answers the fear of a painful past refund experience (for example, "no hassles, no hoops to jump through").
- A reason you offer the guarantee, which signals confidence in the product.
- The work and proof behind the product (development story, track record, a metric), so the buyer trusts it will work.
- A callback to the buyer's specific desire or the promised outcome.
- For physical products, offer free exchanges and use concrete, sensory language; state any limits (such as shipping) honestly.

### Visibility
- Make the guarantee easy to spot through its position, headline, and graphics. It should pass the "scroll test": when you scroll the page, it should stand out enough to make you stop.
- On longer pages that introduce the product late, place the guarantee near the end, just before the first buy button, or at the very bottom. A small reassurance badge can still sit near the top.
- When the guarantee is unusually strong, move it higher up the page, or lead with it.

### Craft
- Study examples for ideas. Do not copy them word for word. Use the concepts in the brand's own words and tone.
- Be honest about the terms. Do not label a guarantee "no questions asked" if it actually requires proof of work. The mismatch erodes trust.

## Examples

- **Good (digital, no-questions-asked):** "If Jumpspeak isn't for you, we can offer a full refund within 100 days of the date you purchased your subscription... [terms on where it applies]." — why it works: 100 days is an unusual number that gives plenty of time to test, and a long window signals the company trusts its own product. It also explains exactly how the guarantee works, which puts the buyer at ease.

- **Good (digital, no-questions-asked):** "You don't even have to say 'yes' today, just say maybe... then, you have 60 whole days to go through the program & if you don't believe it's one of the best breathwork online training programs you've gone through (& worth at least 10X its value), just email us & we will give you a full refund. That's absolutely no questions asked." — why it works: it sells trying the product risk-free instead of just naming a refund. "You don't have to say yes, just say maybe" speaks straight to fence-sitters, and "worth at least 10X its value" implies the program is excellent while easing the buyer in.

- **Good (digital, brand-matched headline):** "100-day love it guarantee. We designed the Carv membership to keep you improving every day you're on snow. That's why 95% of our members tell us they improve with Carv after just a few days. 100 days love it or return it." — why it works: the "love it" headline fits the brand tone and implies the buyer will love the product, and the subtle metric (95% improve in a few days) adds proof inside the guarantee.

- **Good (short and direct):** "30-day money-back guarantee. One email. All your money back. No questions asked." — why it works: it is very short, but it communicates every key idea clearly and fits a clean page design.

- **Good (physical, promise-focused):** "Fall In Love With Our Products Or Your Money Back... When you receive any of our products we have a simple promise: fall in love with it within 30 days or you can get a refund. No questions asked. (Minus shipping and handling, when applicable)." — why it works: short and concise, focused on the promise of loving the product rather than on heavy marketing copy, which fits the brand. It also states the shipping limit honestly.

- **Good (physical, taste-aware):** "The Love Your Coffee Guarantee... If you don't absolutely love your Lifeboost Coffee product, let us know within 30 days... we'll exchange it for free or give you a full refund. We encourage you to open a bag of our coffee, take in the aroma, make a cup, and try it for yourself... The fastest way to get help with an exchange is to call us at 1-800-479-1596..." — why it works: it acknowledges that tastes differ and offers a free exchange so a poor-fit buyer is not lost, uses concrete sensory language ("take in the aroma, make a cup"), reiterates the product benefits, and tells the buyer the fastest way to get help.

- **Good (traditional, safety-focused):** "When you enroll... you're fully protected by our 100% Satisfaction Guarantee. If you don't feel like you've received massive value and you decide you want to cancel any time within the next 30 days, just let us know and we'll refund you promptly. No hassles, no questions, no hoops to jump through." — why it works: "fully protected" makes the buyer feel safe, and "no hassles, no questions, no hoops" answers the objection of anyone burned by a hard refund process before.

- **Good (personal tone, audience-specific):** "60-Day Money-Back Guarantee. You've had enough disappointments in your love life – this course won't be one of them... I'm so committed to helping you find a relationship... that I want to remove every reason you might not join this course... So if you don't see results in 60 days, I don't want you to pay – I'll give you a full refund." — why it works: the opening line speaks directly to a buyer disappointed in their dating life, the tone is personal and shares the seller's values, and "remove every reason you might not join" frames the course as risk-free.

- **Good (objection-answering):** "OUR 60-DAY MONEY-BACK GUARANTEE... You have 60 days to go through all the lessons and see if the program works for you. So you can be sure that it's up to your level, that you like my style of teaching, that your French is really improving. If the program doesn't suit you, email me or my team, ask for a refund and you'll get 100% of your money back..." — why it works: it directly answers the buyer's real objections (is this the right level, will I like the teaching style, will my French actually improve), and the optional-feedback line is a nice touch that also helps the seller improve.

- **Good (longer, proof-rich):** "If you don't LOVE this training program, I insist that you get 100% of your money back... I built this course over YEARS... I've thrown out all the things that didn't work, which leaves everything that actually works... If you don't LOVE this training program, I insist that you get 100% of your money back. It's simple: Join the program and see for yourself." — why it works: a longer guarantee is fine, especially on longer pages. It spotlights the work behind the product (which reassures the buyer it works), names the audience's specific desires, and uses a stronger tone by having the seller "insist" on the refund.

- **Good (action-based):** "Our 100% Satisfaction Guarantee & Money-Back Promise. We want your investment in this course to be an absolute no-brainer for you – if you're actually going to do the work. 'Do the work' technically means completing the core modules. If you've done that and... aren't 100% happy... we'll refund your entire payment..." — why it works: "an absolute no-brainer" is a strong opener, and it states plainly that the buyer must do the work and defines exactly what that means (completing the core modules), which an action-based guarantee should do. It feels less strong than a no-questions-asked guarantee, but it lowers the refund rate by filtering out buyers who never intend to do the work.

- **Good, with one caution (action-based):** "No-Questions-Asked Refund Policy... If you complete the entire 30-day writing program, complete all the modules, and implement all of our frameworks... and STILL don't feel like you extracted the value you hoped, send us your 30 pieces for the month and we will give you a full refund. No questions asked." — why it works: it is transparent, explains why the policy exists, and fairly puts responsibility for doing the work on the buyer. The caution: calling it "No-Questions-Asked" while still requiring proof of completion is misleading, and that mismatch can erode trust. Match the label to the real terms.

- **Good (action-based, see-for-yourself):** "Try Analytics Academy for 30 days, 100% risk-free!... We've tested every system, every technique inside the course with real candidates... We know it works... which is why we offer you to try the whole course risk-free for 30 days... Just send us an e-mail, show us you did the work and we will return you 100% of the investment... We'll even eat the credit card fees." — why it works: it tells the product's development story, uses a "don't take our word for it, see for yourself" nudge, and closes by reiterating what the buyer will learn.

- **Good (stacked guarantees, high-ticket):** "Guarantee #1. 60-Day Risk-Minimizing Policy... If you've done the work and you're not happy, send us your completed work within 60 days and I'll give you every penny back." plus "Guarantee #2. First 3 Clients Support Guarantee... We stick with you... until you land your first 3 paying clients (or for up to a full year)... Either you give it your best shot and get your money back... or you get your first 3 clients. Either way, you win." — why it works: stacking is fine on a high-ticket page because the two guarantees answer different buyers. Calling it a "risk-minimizing policy" with a "do the work, get the results" badge reframes it cleanly, and the second guarantee mixes personal assistance with a result promise for buyers who care most about the outcome.

- **Good (result-based, used as headline):** "I'll Beat Your Best Ad, Or You Pay Nothing. Full Refund If My Ad Loses. Backed By A 94.4% Win Rate... If my version doesn't deliver better results on YOUR chosen KPI? You get EVERY penny back. Keep the research. Keep the ad. Keep everything." — why it works: the guarantee is so strong it becomes the page headline. In a crowded market, and for a cold audience, "keep everything" plus a 94.4% win rate makes the buyer feel they have nothing to lose.

- **Good (result-based, graphic-led):** "Improve your grade or get your money back. Your Current Math Grade → Your Grade with Astra AI. 2 Grade increase guaranteed." — why it works: a result-based promise is strong on its own, promising a specific 2-grade increase is stronger than a vague one, and delivering it through an attention-grabbing graphic gives the page an extra hook.

## Scoring criteria

*Numbering is stable across revisions: a missing number is a criterion deliberately removed, not an error. Judge only the criteria listed.*

2. **Guarantee badge:** Is there an attention-grabbing guarantee badge or stamp? (visual — audit must ask the user)
3. **Headline grabs attention and reads clearly:** Does the guarantee headline grab attention and frame trying the product risk-free or loving it (rather than only naming a refund), AND read clearly and smoothly? It must not be clunky, awkward, or overstuffed: avoid cramming a long product name, a superlative, and the word "guarantee" into one label, or packing several ideas into a single line so it's hard to parse. A buyer should understand it in one quick read. Mark it down if the wording is confusing or hard to say out loud, even when the idea behind it is good.
4. **How it works is clear:** Is it clear how the guarantee works, including the time window and what the buyer must do to qualify?
5. **How to claim is clear:** Is the exact way to claim a refund spelled out, including the specific step or contact point (the actual email address, link, or precise action to take)? MET only when that specific claim step is present. If the copy only gestures at it ("contact me," "email me," "send a message") without the actual address or exact step, mark PARTIAL. MISSING if there is no claim instruction at all.
6. **Exceptionally strong guarantee:** (prose only) Does the page use an especially strong guarantee (result-based, long-duration, or personal-assistance)? (bonus — if met it can raise the score; being missing does NOT count against the score)
7. **The guarantee sells safety, not just terms:** Does it make the reader FEEL safe, rather than only stating the policy? Three ways in, judged once: it reads as reassurance rather than legal wording, it says why the guarantee is offered and what that says about the seller, and it ties back to the outcome the reader wants. MET when at least two land and the section reads as safety rather than admin. PARTIAL when only one lands. MISSING when the terms sit there with no reassurance around them.
9. **Proof and work behind the product:** Does it explain the work that went into the product and why you know it works (development story, track record, or a metric)? (product-dependent)
11. **Visible and well-placed:** Is the guarantee easy to spot and well-placed (passes the scroll test; near the first buy button or bottom on long pages; moved up or led with when unusually strong)? (visual — audit must ask the user)
