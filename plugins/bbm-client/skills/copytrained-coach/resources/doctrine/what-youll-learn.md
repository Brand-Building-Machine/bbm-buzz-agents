# What You'll Learn — Doctrine

## Principles (rules)

### Purpose (educational products)
- This section is for educational products (a course, membership, ebook, or coaching program). It presents the content of the program and ties it to the reader's challenges, problems, and desires. For physical or software products, this section does not apply, so set it aside.
- The star of this section is the fascination bullets (fascinations). Their job is to spark curiosity and take the reader from "I'm interested" to "I need this."

### Fascination ingredients
- Write fascinations by taking the most interesting and valuable parts of your content and making them spark curiosity. Use a mix of: answers to burning questions, costly mistakes to avoid, secrets most people do not know, must-have (ideally named) strategies and techniques, solutions to specific problems, and desired results. You do not need every type in every section; mix the ones that fit.

### Structure and craft
- Use a clear section headline ("Here's what you'll learn in [product]," "Inside the [product] curriculum").
- For a multi-module product, give each module a compelling headline, number the modules, and introduce the core promise of each module at the start.
- Use future pacing where it fits ("by week 1, you'll be able to...").
- Write it in a personal, friendly tone, like you are talking to a friend.
- Do not reinvent the wheel here. Focus your energy on writing the best possible bullets, and make them the star.

## Examples

- **A simple list of fascinations (Book Launch Hero):** curiosity-driven bullets for a small-scope product ("the Title Test for knowing if you've got a potential bestseller," "13 rookie mistakes, one of which might have cost me the NYT bestseller list," "whether it's worth it to hire PR"). — why it works: for a small-scope product, a strong list of fascinations is enough, mixing burning questions, costly mistakes, and secrets.

- **Bullets with expanded copy (Unignorable):** a bolded fascination plus a short expansion ("Write scroll-stopping hooks," then why hooks matter and what you'll learn). — why it works: expanding each bullet adds hooks while keeping the fascination as the headline.

- **A section per module (Climbing Technique):** "Module 1: Make hard moves feel easy," a core promise, then fascination bullets (footwork, flagging, using your arms, compression). — why it works: for an in-depth product, each module gets its own headline, a core promise, and its own fascinations.

- **Flagship, with education and voice woven in (Employee to Entrepreneur):** "Missions" instead of "Modules," each carrying education, a personal narrative, and future pacing inside the fascinations ("your first paying client, in weeks not years"). — why it works: on a long, high-ticket page, the fascinations can carry education, a personal voice, and future pacing while still sparking curiosity.

## Scoring criteria

*Numbering is stable across revisions: a missing number is a criterion deliberately removed, not an error. Judge only the criteria listed.*

2. **Fascination bullets are the star and spark curiosity:** Are fascination bullets present, front and center, and do they genuinely spark curiosity? MET when strong fascinations carry the section. PARTIAL when the bullets are present but flat or generic. MISSING when there are no real fascinations.
3. **Fascinations use a strong mix of ingredients:** Do the fascinations mix the right ingredients (burning questions, costly mistakes, secrets, named techniques, solutions to specific problems, desired results)? MET when there is a rich, varied mix. PARTIAL when they lean on one or two types. MISSING when they are a plain content list with no curiosity.
4. **Each module has a compelling headline, and modules are numbered:** Does each module or part have a compelling headline, and are the modules clearly numbered? MET when each is headlined and numbered. PARTIAL when only some are. Set aside for single-unit products (like a short ebook) with no modules.
5. **Introduces the core promise of each module:** Does it introduce the core promise of each module at the beginning of it? MET when each module opens on its promise. PARTIAL when only some do. Set aside for single-unit products with no modules.
6. **Uses future pacing where it fits:** Does it use future pacing ("by week 1, you'll be able to...")? MET when future pacing is used well. PARTIAL when only lightly. MISSING when the content is listed with no forward pull. (A soft, upside-leaning criterion.)
7. **Written in a personal, friendly tone:** Is it written in a personal, friendly tone, like talking to a friend? MET when the tone is warm and personal. PARTIAL when it is mostly flat. MISSING when it is dry and impersonal.
8. **Includes visuals where they help:** Does it include helpful visuals (module graphics, mockups)? (visual — audit must ask the user) (bonus — if met it can raise the score; being missing does NOT count against the score)
