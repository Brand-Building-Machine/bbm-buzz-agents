# Flow — Doctrine

**Scope:** Editing/craft, primarily page-level (it is about how the whole page holds together), and it also applies within a long section to check local flow. Run it across the whole page. A deep section audit can check a section's internal flow and its transitions into and out of neighboring sections, but the full judgment needs page context.

## Principles (rules)

### Why flow matters
- When copy flows well, every argument leads naturally to the next, every idea follows the previous one logically, and the reader moves through the page without stopping or rereading, fully immersed. When flow breaks, readers get confused, lose interest, start skimming, or close the page.

### One core idea
- The strongest pages solve one problem really well. One problem per product, one result, one core idea. Every part of the page should build toward that one problem.
- Talking about different aspects of the same problem (the negative consequences, the emotions, the obstacles) is still one idea, because it all serves the same core problem. What breaks flow is introducing a second, unrelated idea.
- Example of a break: starting with "I'll help you lose weight" and, pages later, talking about building muscle. That is a different problem. So is promising "financial independence" and then also promising to help avoid burnout.

### Tangents break flow
- An unintentionally introduced new idea becomes a tangent. Tangents are a main cause of broken flow, because they pull the reader off the core argument, and when you return to the original idea they have to work to re-engage.
- You want the reader to read when they read, not think. Once you spot a tangent, either remove it, or relocate it to a place where it becomes the logical next idea rather than a detour.

### Smooth transitions
- Even with ideas in the right order, abrupt shifts hurt flow. Fix them with transitions, which can be a single sentence or a few paragraphs. Good transitions are short, clear, and curiosity-inducing, and they set up the idea the next section expands.
- Ideally the flow of the page mirrors the thinking process of the reader (which you can map from research).

### Finding flow problems (editing method, not scored)
- Reverse-engineer the outline. After writing, list every key idea and point in order, then ask: do they follow a logical sequence, does each argument build on the previous, are you evolving one idea over time rather than introducing new ones? This makes tangents and breaks easy to spot.
- Experiment with order. No two pages should look the same; the section order is tailored to the product and the story, not a template. Try reordering, moving the personal story earlier or later, or switching between a problem-solution lead and a promise lead.
- Getting flow right is hard, which is where most rewriting happens. Aim for a 7 or 8 out of 10 flow on a first launch and refine later from market feedback (spend longer only on very high-value pages). If stuck, study similar swipe pages, step away and return with fresh eyes, or ask a reader "where does it not flow, and where do I lose you?"

## Examples

- **Transition sentences that carry the reader (various pages):** "The good news: it doesn't have to be this way..." (from the problem into the solution); "And this didn't just happen to me..." (from a personal failure story into the universal problem, turning "my story" into "your story"); "But it wasn't always that way..." (from a success into the backstory); "there's something sinister at play..." (from the emotional pain into the named enemy). — why they work: each is short, clear, and curiosity-inducing, and it sets up the next section instead of shifting abruptly.

- **A standard long-form flow that works (How to Talk to Anybody):** a story lead, then twisting the knife, then education, painting the dream, product introduction, qualifying, proof, what's included, more proof, guarantee, FAQ, buy buttons, bonuses, close. — why it works: it does not use every possible section, and it keeps one core idea and moves logically from problem to solution to offer, so it flows.

- **A different order for a different product (Level Up Your Ear):** a story lead and twist, then the dream, the reframe, education, the dream again, the unique mechanism, alternative options, About You, development story, then the offer and proof. — why it works: the section order is tailored to the product and story rather than a template, the dream repeats to build an emotional roller coaster, and it still flows.

## Scoring criteria

1. **One core idea throughout:** Does the whole page hold to one core idea (one problem, one result), rather than drifting into a second, unrelated idea? MET when it stays on one core idea. PARTIAL when it mostly holds but wobbles. MISSING when a second, unrelated problem or promise is introduced.
2. **No tangents pulling the reader off the argument:** Is the page free of tangents that take the reader away from the core argument? MET when there are none. PARTIAL when there is an occasional detour. MISSING when tangents recur and break the thread.
3. **Ideas follow a logical sequence, each building on the last:** Do the ideas follow a logical order, each argument building on the previous one, evolving one idea over time? MET when the sequence is clearly logical. PARTIAL when mostly logical with a few out-of-place jumps. MISSING when the order feels arbitrary.
4. **Smooth transitions between sections:** Are the shifts between ideas and sections smoothed by clear, curiosity-inducing transitions, rather than abrupt jumps? MET when transitions are smooth throughout. PARTIAL when some shifts are abrupt. MISSING when sections lurch from one to the next.
5. **The whole page reads smoothly end to end:** Reading the page start to finish, does it flow smoothly from section to section without points where you stop, get confused, or want to skim? MET when the overall read is smooth (a 7 to 8 out of 10 or better). PARTIAL when it mostly flows but sags in places. MISSING when the flow is choppy throughout.

## Reporting a flow audit (attach this on top of the report)

Before the What's-working / improvements prose, attach a reverse-engineered, section-by-section outline of the page. This is the tool that makes flow problems visible, so do it on every flow audit (and reuse the same outline for a full editing audit).

1. **List every section in order,** top to bottom, one plain line each, in the reader's real reading order. This is the page's skeleton.
2. **Color-code each section** for how well it earns its place and holds attention: 🟢 pulls its weight, 🟡 works but drags or repeats, 🔴 weak, out of place, or redundant.
3. **Scan the seam BETWEEN every pair of sections for the transition,** and mark each seam: 🟢 a real, smooth, curiosity-inducing bridge; 🟡 a weak or mechanical transition; 🔴 an abrupt jump with no bridge (the reader has to re-orient). Missing transitions are the most commonly missed flow defect, so check every seam on purpose. Do not assume a transition exists just because the two sections are in a sensible order. Name each missing or weak seam explicitly (for example "pain quotes → author intro: no bridge, the page jumps from the reader's pain straight into 'Hi, I'm...'").
4. From that map, explain WHY the flow scores where it does, pointing to the specific red and yellow sections and seams. A page can hold one clear idea and a logical order and still flow only moderately, because of missing transitions, a section placed where it interrupts the emotional line, or the same idea restated several times instead of evolving. Judge accordingly: a page with several 🔴/🟡 seams cannot sit under a near-perfect transitions score.

Then give the normal report and the band and Copy Score (out of 100). The map is the evidence, and the score must match it.
