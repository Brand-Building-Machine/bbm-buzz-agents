# Price Justification — Doctrine

## Principles (rules)

### Purpose and placement
- Price Justification justifies the price and demonstrates how valuable the product is. Placement is flexible (after the bonuses, after the guarantee, or even before you talk about the product). If unsure, right after the Qualifying section is a good default.

### When to use it (the gate)
- Include it for products at roughly $97 and up (it matters more the higher the price, up to a point).
- Skip it for a very low-ticket impulse product (a $7 ebook), where it can read as overselling.
- It is not crucial for extremely high-ticket products ($25,000+ masterminds or coaching) that are sold on a call rather than the page.
- If you cannot yet write a compelling, authentic value narrative, leave it as a "someday" and add it once you can.

### Three approaches (pick what fits, or combine)
- A written price justification (best for most courses and coaching).
- A visual cost comparison (best when you are a cheaper alternative to an expensive method).
- A savings or revenue calculator (best for software, or products with large savings over time or for companies).

### How to justify the price
- Do the math for the reader. Turn a vague "I might be missing out" into a specific number ("you're losing over $300 a day by not negotiating," "Time Genius saves 10 hours a week, that's $26,000 a year").
- Address the hidden costs of not buying (health, relationships, missed opportunities, money left on the table).
- Anchor the price to something bigger: a larger investment they already made (your $1,000 iPhone versus a $59 course), or the cost of the common alternative (a single $75 lesson versus a $27 challenge).
- Include the reason the price is "so low" where relevant (it also answers an objection), and invite the reader to try it and decide for themselves.
- Keep the value believable. A big number ("$39,000 value") only works if the copy has genuinely built that value from the features and bonuses. Never invent numbers or savings.

## Examples

- **Doing the math (Time Genius):** "If Time Genius saves you just 10 hours a week, that's 520 hours a year. At $50/hour, that's $26,000 back in your pocket. Every single year," plus the hidden costs in health and relationships. — why it works: it does the math for the reader and adds the hidden costs of staying overwhelmed.

- **A vague worry made specific (No Stress Negotiation):** "a single $5,000 negotiation, invested and compounded, is worth over $1.3 million after 40 years... you're losing over $300 per day by not negotiating," with a graph. — why it works: it turns a fuzzy "I should negotiate" into a concrete daily cost, backed by a visual.

- **Anchoring to a bigger investment (iPhone Photo Academy):** "Your iPhone is worth €1,000. But are you making the most of it?... for a tiny fraction of the cost of your phone." — why it works: anchoring a €59 course to a €1,000 phone the reader already owns makes it feel like a no-brainer.

- **Comparing to the common alternative (21-Day Bass Challenge):** "A single lesson with a good teacher is $75. 21 lessons would be $1,575. The challenge is $27, one-twentieth the cost of an entry-level bass." — why it works: it does the math against the traditional path (a teacher), anchors to gear the reader already bought, and invites them to try it.

- **Visual cost comparison (AI Dream Team):** a table of role costs ($23,000-$41,000 per month to hire) versus $997 for the program. — why it works: it positions a one-time price against the ongoing cost of the alternative, visually.

- **Savings calculator (WeTracked, Notion, AppointWise):** an interactive tool where the reader plugs in their own numbers and sees their savings or added revenue. — why it works: instead of doing the math for them, it lets them prove the value with their own numbers.

## Scoring criteria

1. **Has a price justification appropriate to the product:** Is there a price justification, and does it fit the product's price point? MET when there is a real justification appropriate to the price. Set aside for a very low-ticket impulse product (where it reads as overselling) or a $25,000+ product sold on a call rather than the page; if there is no authentic value narrative yet, note it is better left as a "someday." (product-dependent)
2. **Uses a strong approach for the product:** Does it use a strong approach (a written justification, a visual cost comparison, or a savings/revenue calculator), fit to the product? MET when it uses a fitting approach well. PARTIAL when the approach is weak or half-formed. MISSING when there is no real justification. (Judge whichever approach it uses; combining is fine.)
3. **Does the math for the reader:** Does it make the value concrete, doing the math (how much they save or earn, or a vague worry turned specific)? MET when the value is made concrete with real numbers. PARTIAL when it gestures at value without numbers. Set aside only when the value is genuinely non-numeric (then anchoring and comparison carry it). Never invent numbers.
4. **Anchors the price to something bigger:** Does it anchor the price to a larger existing investment or the cost of the common alternative? MET when there is a strong anchor. PARTIAL when the anchor is weak. MISSING when there is none.
5. **Addresses the hidden costs of not buying:** Does it surface the hidden costs of inaction (health, relationships, missed opportunities, money left on the table)? MET when the cost of inaction is clear. PARTIAL when hinted. MISSING when absent.
6. **Keeps the value believable:** (violation check) Is the claimed value believable and genuinely built, rather than over-inflated? MET when the value is believable and supported. PARTIAL when a figure feels a little high. MISSING when the value is clearly over-inflated in a way that erodes trust.
7. **Includes a supporting visual:** Does it include a supporting visual (a cost-comparison graphic or a calculator)? (visual — audit must ask the user) (bonus — a written-only justification can work without one, so absence does NOT count against it)
