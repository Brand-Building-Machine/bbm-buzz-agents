# Expertise Proof — Doctrine

**Scope:** Proof element, page-level and section-level (typically inside About You). Run it on the whole page or on the About You block. Product-dependent: absence of a given credential is not a flaw where the author does not have it, so treat gaps as upside opportunities to suggest, not failures to penalize.

**Overlap note:** This overlaps About You, where it usually lives. About You establishes who you are and why you can help; expertise proof is the concrete evidence that you are an expert (certifications, education, books, podcast, speaking, a research team). Credit a shared passage under each and do not double-penalize.

## Principles (rules)

### Purpose
- Expertise proof is concrete evidence that you know what you are talking about. It builds trust and credibility, especially with a colder audience or for a topic where credentials matter (health, science, specialized skills).

### What counts as expertise proof
- Certifications or relevant education (for example a PhD, or a specialist certification).
- A published book (especially a bestseller), or a popular podcast.
- Public speaking at conferences, masterminds, or workshops.
- A research team with listed credentials, for a science-based product.
- Reach and scale markers tied to teaching (for example "taught 250,000+ people in 67 countries").

### How to present it
- Use Show Don't Tell. Photos of you speaking or teaching, a book-tour photo, the book in your hands, or a research team shown with their credentials all prove the claim rather than just stating it.
- Place it where it builds authority, typically in the About You section.
- Match the proof to the topic. For a sleep-training product, a "Certified Pediatric Sleep Specialist" credential is exactly what the buyer wants to see; for a science-based supplement, a research team is.

## Examples

- **Scale plus teaching photos (Mastering The Breath):** "having taught 250,000+ people in 67 countries," shown with a collage of talks and workshops. — why it works: the specific reach number plus real teaching photos prove the expertise through Show Don't Tell.

- **A bestselling book, shown on tour (Mastering The Breath):** "I have written multiple bestselling books... translated into 15 languages," with a photo of the author speaking at a book event. — why it works: the book is named and the tour photo makes it tangible.

- **The book worked into the story (Selena Soo, Rich Relationships Mastery):** a mention of publishing her book "Rich Relationships," with a photo of her holding it alongside others. — why it works: the book is used as concrete proof of expertise and shown visually.

- **A matched specialist credential (Moving On From Cosleeping):** "Meet your baby sleep expert... I'm a mom of three, Infant-Parent Mental Health Specialist and Certified Pediatric Sleep Specialist." — why it works: the credential is exactly the one a buyer wants from someone teaching them to sleep-train their child.

- **A research team with credentials (AG1):** "Our research team," a grid of named scientists with their credentials and fields (ND, PhD, nutritional science, immunology). — why it works: for a science-messaged product, a credentialed research team is powerful proof that the claims are grounded.

## Scoring criteria

1. **Mentions relevant certifications or education:** Where the author has them, does the page mention certifications or relevant education (a PhD, a specialist certification) that fit the topic? MET when relevant credentials are present. PARTIAL when mentioned vaguely. Set aside when the author genuinely has none (not a flaw; suggest which credential would matter most if pursued). (product-dependent)
2. **Mentions a book or podcast:** Where the author has one, does the page mention a popular book (or bestseller) or a popular podcast? MET when present. Set aside when the author has neither (suggest as a longer-term credibility play). (product-dependent)
3. **Mentions public speaking:** Where relevant, does the page mention public speaking at conferences, masterminds, or workshops? MET when present. Set aside when the author has none. (product-dependent)
4. **Presents expertise proof with Show Don't Tell visuals:** Is the expertise proven visually (photos of speaking or teaching, book-tour photos, the book itself, a credentialed research team) rather than only stated? (visual — audit must ask the user)
5. **The expertise proof matches the topic:** Does the expertise shown actually fit what the product teaches (the right credential for the right buyer)? MET when the proof is well-matched to the topic. PARTIAL when it is real but only loosely relevant. MISSING when the credentials shown do not speak to this product's promise.
