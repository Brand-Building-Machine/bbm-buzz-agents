# Counter-Mechanism — Doctrine

## Principles (rules)

### Purpose
- The Counter-Mechanism is the named reason why your audience has not solved their problem yet. It is the polar opposite of the Unique Mechanism (the named reason your product works).
- It gives the reader hope, helps them avoid mistakes and pitfalls, shifts responsibility off them, and educates. You can reach these goals other ways (myths, mistakes, limiting beliefs), and a named Counter-Mechanism is one of the strongest, because naming it makes it memorable, and a curiosity-inducing name can be reused up in the lead to open a loop early.
- This is a must-have section. If a page does not have one, it is one of the most under-rated additions it can make.

### How to build it
- Give it a name that is simple, clear, and curiosity-inducing (and that does not already exist), so it sticks and can double as an early hook.
- Explain what it is, why it happens, and the negative consequences of staying stuck in it.
- Pull it from the journey the audience is already going through, in their real language, so it resonates.
- Visualize it with a graphic where you can (it grabs attention and makes the idea clearer).

### Placement
- Position the Counter-Mechanism just before the Unique Mechanism. It is a natural progression: this is why what you have been doing does not work, and here is what does. You can also present the two side-by-side, with the Unique Mechanism shown as the superior option, and a "shortcut" framing can set it up.

## Examples

- **A named trap with causes and consequences (Earnable, "The Great Squeeze"):** "I call this The Great Squeeze. It's the slow-burning trap that so many people are stuck in today... This is why today's work style is so broken... It's no wonder there's such an epidemic of anxiety, overwhelm, and unrelenting burnout." — why it works: a memorable name for the trap the reader is in, explained with its causes (broken work rules, following average advice) and its painful consequences, which sets up the solution.

- **A named pattern with a visual (Level Up Your Technique, "The Tragedy of Tip Soup"):** "For most beginners, the first place they go is YouTube. But random videos and articles don't work. Why not? Because while the information might be great, there's no structure... What you end up with is 'tip soup'," with a graphic of a chaotic tangle of play buttons. — why it works: it names the reader's real pattern, explains why it fails (no structure), shows the negative consequence (rabbit holes, the bass sits unplayed), and visualizes the chaos.

- **Side-by-side with the Unique Mechanism, plus shortcut framing (Beginner Bass Challenge):** the "YouTube Hamster Wheel" (Counter-Mechanism) on the left versus the "T-G-E Framework" (Unique Mechanism) on the right, preceded by shortcut framing ("The 21-Day Challenge is a SHORTCUT," a YouTube search screenshot showing 2.3M results, and real quotes like "I feel like it's just one step forward, two steps back"). — why it works: the side-by-side visually presents the new mechanism as the superior escape from the named old one, and the shortcut framing gives it context with a screenshot and real quotes.

## Scoring criteria

1. **Has a named Counter-Mechanism:** Is there a named reason why the audience has not solved the problem yet? MET when there is a clear, named counter-mechanism. PARTIAL when the reason is described but not actually named. MISSING when there is no counter-mechanism at all.
2. **The name is curiosity-inducing and memorable:** Is the name simple, clear, and intriguing enough to stick (and to reuse as an early hook in the lead)? MET when the name is memorable and curiosity-inducing. PARTIAL when it is named but flat or clunky. MISSING when there is no real name.
3. **Explains what it is, why it happens, and the consequences:** Does it explain what the counter-mechanism is, why it happens, and the negative consequences of staying stuck in it? MET when all three are clear. PARTIAL when one or two are thin. MISSING when it just names it with no explanation.
4. **Pulled from the audience's real journey and language:** Is it built from the journey the audience is already going through, in their real words, so it resonates? MET when it clearly reflects their real experience. PARTIAL when accurate but generic. MISSING when it does not reflect what they actually go through.
5. **Transitions toward the Unique Mechanism:** Does it tease that a better way is coming and set up the Unique Mechanism? MET when it clearly bridges toward the solution. PARTIAL when the handoff is faint. MISSING when it dead-ends. (Set aside only if this section is genuinely the end of the page.)
6. **Visualized with a graphic:** Is the counter-mechanism visualized with a graphic? (visual — audit must ask the user) (bonus — if met it can raise the score; being missing does NOT count against the score)
7. **Presented side-by-side with the Unique Mechanism:** Is it presented in a side-by-side comparison with the Unique Mechanism, with the Unique Mechanism shown as the superior option? (visual — audit must ask the user) (bonus — if met it can raise the score; being missing does NOT count against the score)
