# Tightening Up — Doctrine

**Scope:** Editing/craft, section and page. Run it on a single section (is this section tight) or across the whole page (is the page as a whole tight, is any section dragging). It is one of the editing elements a deep section audit should apply, and it also runs at the page level.

## Principles (rules)

### Length is not the target
- Do not aim for a specific length. A page is too short only if it is missing key elements (it does not cover the objections that need covering, trigger the emotions that should be triggered, or speak to the core problem and desires). Following the section guide covers those by default, so "too short" is rarely the real problem.
- "Too long" is a more legitimate concern, but the real issue is almost never length itself. It is that the copy is not interesting enough. A long movie holds you if it holds your attention; a boring one loses you in fifteen minutes whatever its length. The goal is not to hit or stay under a length, it is to cut the parts that are not interesting and lose the reader.

### Length follows the audience and offer
- Match length to how warm and how expensive the offer is. A low-ticket product sold to warm existing clients needs little convincing, and overselling reads as cringy. The same product sold cold through ads needs longer copy with more sections, because those readers do not know or trust you yet. This is mostly a planning decision, and in editing you can still add a missing section or cut a redundant one.
- When drafting, do not worry about length at all. Write as much as you can, tangents and all. Only after the core elements are written, the flow is fixed, the tangents are removed, and the copy is clear do you tighten. Between two pages that make the same arguments with the same strength, the shorter one often converts better.

### The three ways to cut
- **Every word earns its place.** Go over every sentence, paragraph, and word and ask: does this add anything new (does it build trust, make them believe, answer an objection, or shift their perspective)? If removing it would not cost a conversion, it is fluff. Cut it.
- **Cut accidental repetition.** Deliberate reinforcement that brings a fresh argument is fine. What to cut is two or three paragraphs that say the same thing in slightly different words without adding anything new, which happens a lot in drafting.
- **Halve the sections that drag.** If you or a test reader start skimming or get bored at a certain point, that section needs tightening. Force it to say the same things in half the space. If it is two pages, make it one; if it still drags, halve it again. Usually once or twice is enough.

### The result
- The end result is the same structure and the same arguments, told tighter, which is more powerful and captivating. It is like trimming the fat off a steak, keeping only the best parts, and it is often what turns a good page into a great one.

## Scoring criteria

1. **Every line earns its place:** Does every sentence and paragraph add something new (build trust, make the reader believe, answer an objection, or shift the reader), with no fluff? MET when nothing could be cut without loss. PARTIAL when it is mostly tight but carries some fluff. MISSING when it is padded with lines that add nothing.
2. **No accidental repetition:** Is the copy free of accidental repetition (two or three passages that restate the same point in different words with nothing new added)? MET when there is none, or repetition is deliberate reinforcement that brings a fresh argument. PARTIAL when a point is restated once without adding anything. MISSING when the copy repeats itself often.
3. **No sections that drag:** Are there no sections where the reader would start skimming or get bored, that should be cut down? MET when the copy holds attention throughout. PARTIAL when one section drags and should be halved. MISSING when several sections drag.
4. **Length fits the audience and offer:** (prose only) Is the length appropriate for how warm the audience is and how expensive the offer is (tight and not oversold for a warm, low-ticket offer; full enough with the needed sections for a cold, higher-ticket one)? MET when the length fits the context. PARTIAL when it is somewhat over- or under-built. MISSING when it badly overshoots (overselling a warm audience) or falls short (thin copy for a cold, high-ticket offer).
