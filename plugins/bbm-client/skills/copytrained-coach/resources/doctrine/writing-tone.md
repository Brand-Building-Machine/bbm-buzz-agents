# Writing Tone — Doctrine

**Scope:** Editing/craft, section and page. Run it on a single section or across the whole page. It is one of the editing elements a deep section audit should apply, and it also runs at the page level (tone should be consistent everywhere).

## Principles (rules)

### 1. Write to one person
- Always write to one specific person, not a group. The exception is a genuine group offer (a cohort, mastermind, or membership where you really are addressing several people).
- Writing to one person makes the copy feel personal. The reader feels more seen and more understood, ideally as if it was written just for them.
- This matters even more outside English, where "you" has separate singular and plural forms, so a plural "you" reads as impersonal.
- It helps to have one specific person in mind while writing: an ideal client profile, a potential client, a past client, or (when rewriting an existing page) an existing client who was a great fit. Ask what you would say to that person, what language would resonate, and what you would not say.

### 2. Write to a friend
- Write as if you are talking to a potential client over a cup of coffee: casual, personal, and warm. Do not write in a tone that feels like selling, pushy, or salesy.
- The test: read the copy out loud at least once. If a line feels pushy, unnatural, salesy, or like something you would never actually say to someone in person, reword it into something you would say to a friend or your best client.
- Think of a calm, high-end store (buying an expensive watch, a luxury massage): they ask what you are looking for, answer your objections, and give context, rather than hard-selling. That calm, confident tone earns more trust than pressure.
- Keep the tone true to your own personality. It can be gentle and encouraging, or blunt and challenging; what matters is that it is personal and one to one. Never write anything you would not actually say, and lean into your own personality, which makes you more memorable in a crowded market.

### 3. Write in first person, consistently
- Solopreneurs and small teams should write in first person, "I." Do not inflate to "we" to seem like a bigger company. Embrace the smallness: "I built this. I did the work" is honest and personal.
- Use "we" only when there is a real team to credit (a product, design, or research team), which is maybe 10 to 20% of cases. Then you can spotlight those people and how they contributed, which adds credibility.
- Whichever you choose, keep it consistent across the whole page. Switching between "I" and "we" pulls the reader out of the flow to process the inconsistency.
- Never refer to yourself in the third person. A common slip is a page written in first person that suddenly shifts to third in the About section ("Primoz is an entrepreneur with ten years of experience..."). That breaks the flow, cools the tone, loses the personal touch, and reads as robotic. Stay in first person all the way through.

### The end state
- The final copy should feel like you are always speaking to one person, speaking to a friend, and speaking as one person in first person. That makes the copy flow better, feel relatable and casual, and read as more immersive, so the reader feels seen and understood.

## Examples

- **Warm, understanding tone (Find Love Offline):** "It's Saturday night. You're home alone... You finally admit it: being single feels lonely. Really lonely." — why it works: it is written one to one in a warm, understanding, encouraging (but honest) voice, so the reader feels seen.

- **Edgier, no-BS tone, still one to one (Earnable):** "I'm not buying it. Here are hundreds of thousands of people, with 90% telling me they want to start a business. But what's stopping them?... I choose to take control of my destiny." — why it works: it is still one person talking to one person, just blunt and challenging instead of soft. The tone fits the writer's real personality, which is the point: match your own voice.

- **Honest-talk close that feels like a real conversation (Structuring Drills):** "Straight talk with you: I've given all the arguments I have for you to join... I want you to take a hard look at yourself, introspect a little bit... But if it did help you, why would you not do it?" — why it works: reading it feels like sitting across from the writer in conversation, which is exactly how copy should feel.

- **Weak (pushy and salesy):** "You should join right now. And if you don't join today, in the next 30 minutes, your life will become a living hell... by the way, you'll get all these amazing bonuses. This is the last time to join." — why it falls short: put yourself on the other end and you would back away fast. Threats, fake urgency, and piled-on bonuses read as desperate. A calm, confident tone earns trust instead.

## Scoring criteria

1. **Written to one person throughout:** Is the copy addressed to one specific reader, not a group, all the way through? MET when it holds a singular, one-to-one address throughout. PARTIAL when it mostly does but slips into addressing a crowd in places. MISSING when it reads as a broadcast to a group. (Set aside the singular requirement for a genuine group offer like a cohort or mastermind.)
2. **Personal, friend-over-coffee tone:** Does it read like a warm, casual, personal conversation with a friend, rather than pushy, salesy, or unnatural selling? MET when it reads natural and personal. PARTIAL when mostly genuine with a few salesy or stiff lines. MISSING when it leans pushy, hypey, or corporate.
3. **Consistent first-person voice:** Does the copy stay in one voice ("I" or "we") across the whole page, without switching? MET when it is consistent. PARTIAL when it mostly holds but switches once or twice. MISSING when it switches noticeably. (A small operation writing "I" is preferred over an inflated "we"; use "we" only with a real team.)
4. **No third-person self-reference:** (violation check) Is the author kept in first person throughout, with no shift into third person (for example an About section that suddenly describes the author by name)? MET when there is no third-person self-reference. PARTIAL when it slips once. MISSING when the author is described in the third person.
5. **Tone fits the writer's own personality:** Does the tone read as authentically the writer's own (gentle or blunt, but real), rather than a forced or generic register? MET when it feels true to a real person's voice. PARTIAL when it is mostly authentic but generic in places. MISSING when it reads as a generic, personality-free sales voice.
