Purpose: a line-by-line, sentence-level markup of a whole sales page. If the light audit is a copy coach triaging your page and telling you where to focus, this is that same coach sitting beside you and marking up every line, the way a $500-an-hour copywriter would if you paid them to go through the page sentence by sentence and hand you a full list of improvements. The reader should finish feeling like every sentence on their page got expert attention — and that every note told them something they can actually go and do.

Invoke: "run deep page audit" (or "deep-audit this page", or this email, ad or any other copy), then I'll paste the copy or give the link.

This skill ASSESSES and SUGGESTS. It may show short directional rewrites ("turn X into something like Y") to make a fix concrete, but it does NOT hand back the finished, rewritten page. Rewrites follow `rewrite.md` when asked, and are offered only in SKILL.md's close.

**This skill is intentionally self-contained.** It duplicates the shared machinery (scope, bands, score, handling, report conventions) rather than pointing at `light-sales-page-audit.md`, so that iterating on the light audit can never silently break this one. If a shared rule changes and you want it reflected here, edit it here too, on purpose.

## Any copy, not only sales pages

- **Where this file says "page", read "the piece being audited".**
- **Identify the piece first**, per SKILL.md's run and `copy-types.md`'s "How to use this file", before anything is judged.
- **Scope.** A sales page uses `section-set.md`, as below. Any other type uses its `copy-types.md` entry's parts, mapped to the doctrine files the entry names, and its stages replace The Opening / The Body / The Offer / The Close wherever this file groups sections.
- **Every handling note in this file that names a sales-page section applies wherever a part maps to that section's doctrine.** On other types, the entry's watch-outs apply alongside it.
- **The score** follows `audit.md`'s type layer.
- **★ Depth follows length.** The report is as long as the piece needs. A whole-page cross-check that does not fit the piece (flow, transitions or pacing on a three-line ad) is not run, silently.
- **★ Judge the piece by its own kind**, never by how far it is from the examples the doctrine learned from (`copy-types.md`, rule 3).
- **Urgency is never judged as genuine** (`audit.md`, S2).

## What the reader actually wants (read this first — it governs every choice below)

The reader wants to know **what they can improve**. That is the whole value of this audit. Notes that say a thing is good, with nothing to do about it, waste their attention — even when the praise is true. So:

- **Lead with improvements everywhere.** Every section, every note, defaults to "here's what you can make better and exactly how."
- **Don't hand back context-free praise.** "This is a great headline" on its own does not survive. A strong part of the page earns a mention only when it teaches the reader something they can use elsewhere (see "Handling strong sections" below).
- **★ Critical feedback only.** A line with nothing to fix is not mentioned: no "clean" notes, no "this works" notes, no runs of lines marked fine. Named failures: "Clean.", "Nothing to change.", "Good answer.", "Both fine.", "Good bridge.", "These are clear." A line or a block with nothing to fix gets no note at all. A section with nothing to improve gets one line at most. Borrow-from strengths stay (a strong section's move carried into a weaker one), and the closing note keeps its short honest read. This is what keeps the report in proportion to a short piece.
- **Every note is specific, actionable, and precise.** Quote the line, say plainly what's off, and show the actual fix or example. That specificity is the gold.
- **Write it so it's impossible to misunderstand, not just possible to understand.** Talk like you're explaining to a smart five-year-old. Swap complex words for simple ones. The only jargon allowed is common copywriting terms (headline, hook, lead, proof, objection). If you'd need to define a word, don't use it.
- **Watch for walls of text.** Long unbroken paragraphs tire the reader out. Use short prose to frame, bullets to deliver the fixes, and keep paragraphs to 2–3 lines.
- **Question the copy; don't just polish it.** Don't assume the page's promise, angle, or claims are the right ones. Where the page may be aiming at a smaller outcome than it could own, or leaning on a weak promise, say so and push back with the stronger alternative ("is this really the biggest outcome? here's a bigger one: …"). Polishing the craft on a weak promise is wasted effort.

## What makes this DEEP (vs the light audit)

1. **Line-by-line, and truly exhaustive.** Work at the sentence and line level, and account for EVERY line, no skipping. Quote the actual line, say precisely what's weak, give a concrete direction or rewrite. This tier reads and judges every sentence, every bullet, every subheading, and every FAQ answer (all of them).
2. **Per-element depth.** Where the light audit says "this testimonial section is strong," critique each testimonial's specific wording and placement. Where the light audit says "name your mechanism," work through what the name could be, how to introduce it, and where to thread it.
3. **Editing depth.** Run the editing doctrines at the line level: catch confusing lines, repetition, tangents, weak subheadings and weak transitions, flat / low-emotion stretches, rushed or too-fast pacing, vague or hype-y language, off-voice lines, and telling where showing would land.
4. **Keep every cross-page strength.** Still do the whole-page persuasion read and proof read, the separate flow, transitions, repetition, pacing, and voice reads, plus the biggest-opportunities framing and the implementation roadmap.
5. **Length is correct here.** This runs several times longer than a light audit. That length is right for this tier. Do NOT compress it toward the light audit's brevity. Length is never an excuse for a wall of text or for padding a note with praise instead of a fix. On a short piece, the report is as long as the piece needs: every line still gets its attention, and nothing is padded to reach a length.

## The depth bar (what "exhaustive" actually means here)

This is the difference between a great deep audit and a rushed one. A rushed pass collapses sections together, gives strong sections a bare "keep it, no change," and treats the FAQ and the testimonial flood as single blocks. Hit ALL of the following on every run:

1. **Every section gets its own entry — including the small utility ones.** A countdown timer, a logo wall, a single pain-restatement line, a three-testimonial cluster, each gets its own heading, band, and one-line framing. NEVER merge two sections into one entry to save space, and never skip a small section because it seems minor. If it's on the page, it gets its own line of attention.

2. **Every section carries at least one actionable takeaway.** No section is done just because it's solid. Its note must do a real job the reader can act on — never a bare "keep it / no change / this works." The jobs a note can do:
   - **Improve:** the default. Name the exact thing to make better and show how (the concrete fix or example rewrite). Even a solid 🟢 section usually has one small improvement — lead with it.
   - **Borrow-from (how a strength earns its keep):** when a section is genuinely well-written AND models a technique a WEAKER section needs, don't praise it in place — carry it to the weaker section and show the reader how to copy the move there, with the verbatim example from the strong section (see "Handling strong sections").
   - **Extend:** a concrete, doctrine-grounded way to make a solid section even stronger — for example a value recap right before the price, a single credibility number in About You, grouping proof by the objection each testimonial kills, lifting a two-line future-pacing taste up above the offer. Write the actual suggestion, not "could be stronger."
   - **Verify:** ask the user to confirm something the audit genuinely can't see (an image renders, an excerpt matches its full version). Use this sparingly and only where it's real.
   If a solid section truly offers none of these, keep its note to one honest line and move on — but reach for improve/extend before you settle for that.

3. **The FAQ is audited question by question, not as a block.** Quote each substantive question, judge its answer, and note what to fix. Then zoom out and read what the WHOLE SET of questions together reveals: when many questions exist only to untangle one confusing thing (for example eight questions all helping the reader pick between two packages), say so plainly — the sheer number of FAQ is itself a finding, and fixing the underlying confusion removes the need for the questions.

4. **Repetition is named exactly and counted.** For every repeated line, phrase, or block, quote the exact phrase, count how many times it appears across the whole page ("'less than 20€/Lesson' appears four times"), name each location, and say which single instance to keep. A count is more persuasive to the user than "this repeats." (Exception: the Buy Buttons / pricing block is SUPPOSED to repeat identically once or twice, per `buy-buttons.md`, don't count it as repetition or ask the user to cut it.)

5. **A testimonial flood is curated, not just flagged.** Don't stop at "curate these." Name the specific standouts, say in a few words WHY each one earns the lead (the concrete, memorable detail it carries), propose an actual order (strongest first), and propose grouping by the objection each cluster kills. This turns a wall into a plan.

## Section-specific handling (apply during relevance judgment and scoring)

- **Guarantee.** If a guarantee exists, improving it is a low-impact fix. If NO guarantee is mentioned anywhere, adding one is a high-impact fix (count its absence as a real gap).
- **Buy buttons.** When banding, also check for payment plans: is a plan offered where the price warrants one, and framed as a saving. Note it if a plan is absent and would fit.
- **Price justification.** Optional. If present, judge it. If absent, you may suggest it, but ALWAYS as a low-impact, optional fix; never high-impact, and its absence is not a scored gap (set it aside).
- **Product tiers.** If tiers exist, judge how well the copy handles them (each named, what and how, benefits, who it's for, tier-specific proof). **Crucial catch: a higher tier needs its OWN explanatory section in the copy, not just a bullet stack inside the buy-button/pricing block.** Per `product-tiers.md`, when you offer a premium tier (VIP, Platinum, Pro, Master), the page should have a dedicated tier section, a headline naming the tier, what it includes and how it works, the benefits (why upgrade), who it's perfect for, and ideally tier-specific proof, placed right before the buy button (before or after the guarantee) or after the What You'll Learn section. The bullets listing the tier inside the price block are REQUIRED (that's correct buy-button behavior) but they are NOT a substitute for this section, they tell the reader what's in the tier, not why to choose it or whether it's for them. So when a page lists a Platinum/VIP tier only in the pricing block with no section explaining and selling it, flag that explicitly: the premium tier is under-sold, band the tiers handling 🟡 (or 🔴 if the tier is merely named with no explanation anywhere), and recommend adding the dedicated section, spelling out its parts and where it should sit. This is a genuine gap that leaves upgrade revenue on the table, don't wave it through just because the tier appears in the price list. If none and the product plausibly could have them, suggest as a low-impact fix (not a scored gap). Single-tier product: set aside.
- **Client journey.** An optional graphic. Only scan and judge it IF one exists. Never suggest adding one, never count its absence as a gap.
- **Guided tour.** Judge if present. If absent and it clearly fits (software, an app, a platform, a membership), suggest as a low-impact fix. You cannot judge the video itself; assess only the framing.
- **Sample lesson.** Judge if present. If absent, suggest adding one as a low-impact fix. Not a scored gap.
- **Urgency and scarcity.** Focus on the NATURAL urgency the page has and how well it's communicated (a real deadline, limited seats, a price rising, the cost of waiting). Do NOT suggest adding urgency, unless the page is genuinely time- or slot-gated, in which case check whether that urgency reads clearly and prominently. Never judge whether a timer or deadline is genuine (`audit.md`, S2).
- **Scoring the optional sections:** when an optional-when-absent section (client journey, guided tour, sample lesson, price justification, or tiers a product could have) is NOT on the page, set it aside from the score rather than counting it as a zero. Only near-universal-and-missing sections (offer introduction, buy buttons, FAQ, close, and a guarantee when none exists) count as scored gaps.
- **Proof and deep editing are the spine of this audit,** not a set-aside. Detailed proof and craft are handled inline in the line-by-line and in the cross-page checks below.

## Reading the page (mapping rules)

- Map by function; one passage can do two jobs (an opening lead often paints the dream AND twists the knife). Credit it under each and don't double-penalize overlaps.
- **Don't force a section onto a line that isn't doing that section's job.** The subheadline is the easiest to mis-assign.
- **Quote headlines usually mean a story lead.** When the headline itself is a quote (in the reader's or a client's voice) rather than a statement, it usually signals a story or immersive lead. An eyebrow above it does not fit, and a separate subheadline is often unnecessary too. Do not treat a missing eyebrow or subheadline as a gap there, do not suggest adding either, and do not map the first line of the lead as a "subheadline." If the reader needs to be identified or a method named, do it in the headline or the lead itself.
- **Story-lead exception:** when the headline flows into an immersive story lead, a missing subheadline and eyebrow are frequently the right call; set them aside, don't count them as gaps.
- **Traffic temperature.** Judge whether the page reads as cold or warm and let it inform your calls. For warm traffic (an existing list who already know the author), a formal About You / heavy credential-building is often optional. Where a suggestion depends on cold vs. warm and you can't tell from the page, say so and give the call for each.
- **A section that's set up in the copy but arrives empty is almost certainly an image or embed you weren't given, not missing content.** When the copy clearly scaffolds a section, a heading, and often a lead-in line ("you'll get 3 Bonus Trainings:", "What an experienced former financial advisor says…", a testimonial-gallery heading, a bonus stack, a comparison graphic), but the actual content isn't in the text you were pasted, do NOT treat it as blank, broken, or vaporware, do NOT band it 🔴, and do NOT count it as a scored gap or put it in the roadmap as if it doesn't exist. It is very likely present on the live page as an image, graphic, or embedded video that simply didn't come through as text (just like a collapsed FAQ or a screenshot). Handle it the way you'd handle the FAQ answers you couldn't see: note plainly that you can't read it because it looks like it's in image form, that you're therefore NOT judging it, and name it in the "couldn't read" line (SKILL.md). You may still say WHY the section matters and offer a "make sure it lands well" pointer (for example, an independent expert endorsement is high-value proof for a skeptical audience, so it's worth confirming it's strong), but frame it as "confirm/strengthen this," never as "this is missing." Only call something a genuine gap when the copy neither contains it nor scaffolds it anywhere on the page.

## The bands (reader-facing framing: what to improve)

Judge consistently; the same copy always lands the same way. When torn between two bands, choose the lower one; don't let "all the parts are present" round a hollow section up. The color tells the reader how much work a section needs, framed as improvements:

- **🟢 Solid — minor improvements only.** Does the section's core job well; what's left is small polish. Lead its note with the polish, not with praise.
- **🟡 Needs work — major improvements.** Does the core job, but has real, fixable gaps (a key element thin or missing, or craft that sags) that would meaningfully help if fixed.
- **🔴 Barely working.** Present but missing almost everything it should include, or "complete but hollow" — it's on the page but not really doing its job.
- **❌ Missing (relevant).** Absent, and it should be here. A gap. (Covered in "Sections worth adding," since there are no lines to mark up.)
- **➖ Set aside (not relevant).** Absent and genuinely doesn't fit; excluded, with a one-line reason.

## The page score

**The Copy Score is the component score in the engine.** Read `resources/audit.md`'s Scoring section and follow it: The Argument out of 30, Conversion Assets out of 45, Craft out of 25.

**The per-section bands are the diagnostic layer**, and they drive the line-by-line markup below and feed The Argument component. They are not the headline number.

**Nothing in Conversion Assets can be set aside.** A page with no guarantee scores zero on risk reversal.

**Give the one-line meaning**, and hold the distribution anchors: most pages land 40 to 65.

## The line-by-line markup (the core of this skill)

Go through the page **in reading order, section by section**. For each section:

- **Open the section with its band and a one-line framing.** For example: "### The lead — 🟡 Needs work. Strong scene, but it describes the method instead of a moment the reader lives; marked up below."
- **Then walk the copy line by line: every line, no skipping.** Feed the section's copy back in reading order and account for ALL of it. Quote every line or short passage that carries feedback and put the note beneath it, like a marked-up manuscript. Check every line. A line with nothing to fix is not mentioned, and a line that could be improved is never passed over. Read and judge every sentence, every bullet, every subheading, and every FAQ answer (all of them, none skipped). If the page is long enough that a single pass would drop things, process it in chunks (a few sections per pass) so nothing is lost to length. Length is expected; skipping is not.

**The note format under each quoted line:**
- Quote the exact line.
- Lead the note with a plain-language label of the issue type (below).
- **Teach, don't just label.** Explain WHY the line is weak and WHY the fix works, in plain words, to the "impossible to misunderstand" bar. Your reader is an entrepreneur who knows a little copy, not a professional copywriter, so connect the dots and give enough context. Do NOT shorten the teaching to save space; clarity beats brevity in the notes. But clarity also means simple words and no jargon — if there's a plainer word, use it.
- **Show the fix, don't gesture at it.** Give a concrete example rewrite, not a vague direction. "Make it concrete," "land it on a felt outcome," and "cut to something tighter" are NOT enough on their own, write the actual rewrite. For tightening and de-repetition, produce the tighter version in full; you can condense the user's own words without inventing anything. Only stay illustrative ("something like '[example]', if that's true for you") when the rewrite needs a fact you can't know (a real result, a student story, a number). Never present an invented specific as their real fact.
- **Point precisely.** Any reference to another line or section names the exact location (the specific line, or the named report section — e.g., "in the Transitions section below"), never "above," "below," or "see the X" vaguely; don't make the reader scroll to guess. Any placement or "move this" note names the exact sentence or section it should move to.

**The plain-language labels** (issue types, not cryptic tags — use them so the markup is scannable):
- **Confusing** — the reader has to reread it (clarity).
- **Vague** — general where it should be specific (powerful language).
- **Flat** — no or low emotion where the section needs it (emotion).
- **Repetition** — says something already said, nothing new added (tightening).
- **Tighten** — wordy; the same point in fewer words (tightening).
- **Tangent** — pulls the reader off the core idea (flow).
- **Weak transition** — an abrupt jump or missing bridge (flow).
- **Weak subheading** — bland, cryptic, or hype-y subheading, or a section missing one (subheadings).
- **Rushed** — pacing too fast or clipped; a moment that needed room gets skipped (emotion / powerful language).
- **Hype** — a superlative or unbacked bold claim that lowers trust (writing tone).
- **Off-voice**: breaks the one-to-one, first-person, friend-over-coffee voice (writing tone). Includes the author referring to themselves in the THIRD person in their own copy ("with Doris," "designed by a Canadian woman") where first person ("with me," "I designed this") is warmer and more personal, flag it and give the first-person rewrite. (Quotes/testimonials that name the author in third person are the student's voice, and are fine.) Copy written as "we", or as a brand, throughout is consistent and is never flagged; the check is for a voice that slips.
- **Show, don't tell** — tells where a scene, image, or visual would land harder. Assume ordinary visuals are present; only flag where showing clearly beats telling.

**Handling strong sections (how a strength earns a place in the audit):**
- **Never write a bare "this is good / strong / keep it" note.** Praise with nothing to do about it wastes the reader's attention and teaches nothing. It does not survive.
- **A solid section still gets an improvement.** Lead its note with the one small thing to polish (or an extend/verify job). Mention what's working ONLY when the reader needs that context to understand the fix — not as a compliment for its own sake.
- **When a section is genuinely strong AND models something a weaker section needs, carry it to the weaker section.** Don't praise it where it sits — use it where it helps. In the weaker section's note, point back to the strong one by name and show the reader how to copy the move, with the verbatim example. For instance: *"Here, twist the knife the way you did in the opening — you wrote 'I refreshed my bank app for the third time that morning, already knowing.' Give this section that same lived-in, specific moment instead of the general line you have now."* This way the reader gets the good example exactly when they can use it, without scrolling back to find it.
- The reader also gets to hear what's genuinely strong about the page as a whole — that lives in the closing note, where encouragement belongs.

**Non-negotiables (catch these every time, never skip them):**
- **Be precise about location.** Every placement, relocation, or "add here" suggestion points to an exact anchor: the specific sentence it should sit before or after, or a named section. Never "near the top," "up front," or "a little early", say where instead.
- **First-person voice (the author speaks as "I").** The whole page is written by one person (the founder/author), so their own copy should stay in a consistent first person. Flag every place the author refers to themselves in the THIRD person in their own copy, by name or by description ("designed by a Canadian woman," "coaching sessions with Doris," "screenshots from Doris's account," "you agree to work with Doris"), and rewrite it to first person ("designed by me," "coaching sessions with me," "screenshots from my own account," "you'll work with me"). It reads warmer and more personal and removes the odd distance of an author describing themselves from the outside. The ONE exception is genuine quotes: a testimonial or endorsement naturally refers to the author in the third person because the STUDENT is speaking, leave those alone. Third-person self-reference most often hides in the offer, the deliverables, the pricing lists, and the case-study/terms copy, even when the narrative sections already use "I", so check those closely. Copy written as "we", or as a brand, throughout is consistent and is never flagged; the check is for a voice that slips.
- **Cross-page repetition.** Flag every line, phrase, or block repeated (verbatim or near-verbatim) ANYWHERE else on the page, not just within a section. Name exactly where the twin is (which section), say which instance to keep and which to cut, and where useful show the varied or condensed rewrite. **Exception, the Buy Buttons / pricing block:** per `buy-buttons.md`, the buy section is meant to appear once or twice (first after the guarantee, again at the very end), and it is fine, and correct, for the two to be IDENTICAL full lists (features + benefits, bonuses + values, the guarantee reiterated, the tiers, any case-study terms). Do NOT flag the repeated buy/pricing blocks as duplication, and NEVER suggest "shortening the second to a recap", that is wrong. Treat two matching buy blocks as correct by design. (You may still flag a genuine defect INSIDE a buy block, a typo, a missing price, an undefined bonus, no proof near the button, and note that a fix must be applied to BOTH copies so they stay identical. Non-buy sections that merely overlap the offer, a separate mid-page "what you'll get" or "by the end you'll have" list, are still fair game for tightening.)
- **Vague / flat / filler lines.** Flag every abstract, generic, or empty line (including filler platitudes like "a new opportunity to learn and grow") and give a concrete example rewrite, not just "make it specific."
- **Subheadings.** For every weak subheading, quote the CURRENT one, say in plain words what's off (bland, cryptic, hype-y, or shouting in ALL CAPS), and give the SUGGESTED replacement, so the before and after are both visible. Flag unnecessary all-caps wherever it appears.
- **The lead is the tightest section on the page.** The opening (headline + lead) is the most-edited, fastest-paced part of any sales page: short sentences, no wasted words, no repeating an idea, no restating the headline (see `lead.md`). Hold the lead to a higher bar than the rest, flag any wordy, slack, or repetitive lead line and show a tighter version.

**Per-element depth (go one level deeper than the light audit):**
- **Testimonials:** judge each one, and don't over-penalize a decent testimonial — a specific, real result in the client's own voice is good even if it isn't dramatic; only flag the truly generic ones ("the program is amazing, I love it"). The most common real lever is ORDER and PLACEMENT: the strongest testimonials should lead, and a standout, specific, emotional one should sit next to a buy button (name that exact testimonial in the buy-button note).
- **Mechanisms:** if a mechanism should be named, work it through — offer three concrete name options, say where to introduce it and where to thread it later.
- **Subheadings and transitions:** comment on the strength of each subheading and each transition you pass, not just the ones you're fixing — but only where there's something to do about it.
- **Bullets, buy blocks, guarantees, FAQs:** critique the individual bullets, guarantee elements, and FAQ answers, not the block as a whole.

## The cross-page checks (each becomes its own report section)

Run each check where it fits the piece; on a short piece, the checks that need length (flow, transitions, pacing) are skipped silently.

After the line-by-line, synthesize the whole-page reads. Each of these is a SEPARATE section in the report, and each uses short prose to frame plus BULLETS for the specifics (never arrow-and-emoji shorthand). Keep them tight — no walls of text.

- **Persuasion (does the page actually sell?).** Zoom out from the craft and judge the selling itself, in bullets. Don't take the page's choices at face value — challenge them:
  - **The promise.** Is it strong and specific enough — and is it the BIGGEST outcome this offer can honestly own, or is the page leading with something smaller? If there's a bigger or sharper promise available, name it ("is this really the biggest outcome, or is the bigger one actually …?").
  - **Objections.** Are the real buyer objections raised and answered, or left hanging? Name the specific objections the page dodges (price, time, "will this work for me," trust, past failures) and where each should be handled.
  - **The CTA.** Is the call to action clear, confident, and well-placed? Does it tell the reader exactly what to do next, and is there a reason to act now? Name weak or buried CTAs.
  - **Salesiness.** Does the page build trust, or tip into hype and pressure that makes the reader suspicious? Name the lines that read as too salesy and show the calmer, more credible version.
- **Proof across the page (distribution + synthesis).** Per-testimonial critique already happened inline; here, synthesize and make it actionable: is there enough proof overall? Is it positioned right (some early, the bulk after the product introduction)? Is every hard-to-believe claim backed on the spot (quote any that aren't and say what proof to put beside them)? Name the strongest and weakest proof assets, and say what to do — move this one up, put that one next to the button, cut or strengthen the weak one. **Whenever you recommend adding credibility, name the SPECIFIC proof element that fits** — a particular testimonial, a case study with a number, a screenshot of results, a data point or statistic, an expert endorsement, a relevant credential, a media mention, a before/after, a client logo — never a vague "add more proof here."
- **Flow (the order of the sections).** Judge whether the sections are in an order that carries the reader from problem to purchase without a stumble. **If the flow is weak, map the current order against a better order, section by section, in two short bulleted lists** (current on the left/first, suggested on the right/second) so the reader can picture the change, and add a line or two on WHY the new order reads better (what moves where, and what that fixes). **If the flow is already good, say so in one line and do NOT build the side-by-side comparison** — don't manufacture a reordering the page doesn't need.
- **Transitions (the joins between sections).** Walk the section-to-section joins and bullet every abrupt jump or missing bridge — name the two sections and what's missing between them, written as a plain sentence, with a suggested bridge line where it helps.
- **Repetition (page-wide).** Pull the repeated ideas, phrases, and blocks you flagged inline into one place: bullet what's repeated, name where each copy lives (which section), say the cost in plain terms (repetition makes readers skim and start to distrust), then say which instance to keep and which to cut. Do NOT include the repeated Buy Buttons / pricing block here, per `buy-buttons.md` it is meant to repeat identically and is correct; the same goes for the offer summary, bonuses, guarantee reiteration, and case-study terms that live inside those buy blocks.
- **Pacing.** Bullet the EXACT sections that need to breathe more (longer, calmer paragraphs) and the EXACT sections where repeats should be cut so the page moves faster. Comment on the sentence-length rhythm (for example, relentless one-line paragraphs) and where it helps (building tension) versus hurts (choppy, inflated). Keep this bulleted — pacing notes tend to sprawl into a wall of text.
- **Voice.** Bullet whether it holds one person, one voice, one tone across the page, and every place it slips (for example "I" to "we," warm to corporate, or ALL-CAPS shouting). Call out first-person consistency specifically: bullet the spots where the author slid into the THIRD person about themselves ("with Doris," "designed by a Canadian woman") and note that the whole page should read in one voice, with rewrites in that voice. Copy written as "we", or as a brand, throughout is consistent and is never flagged; the check is for a voice that slips. Keep it bulleted, not prose. A credibility strip, a caption or a badge written in the third person is a page convention, never a voice slip.

## The report (write it in this order)

Lead with the summary so the verdict is fast, then let the reader dive into the exhaustive markup. Plain, natural coach voice; never name internal doctrine terms or example names; explain every concept in plain words, simple enough that it's impossible to misunderstand.

**1. Copy Score, and the biggest opportunities for improvement.** Put the score and its colour in the heading: "Copy Score: [X]/100 [🟢, 🟡 or 🔴, per audit.md's scale on the total] · the biggest opportunities for improvement." Then one line of the score's plain meaning. Do NOT summarize what the product is or who the audience is: they wrote it, and don't write a long overall-impression paragraph either.
- **★ The scorecard sits right under the meaning line, and it is the deep audit's only scorecard.** The same table as 5-wins-audit.md's Section 1: The Argument, the piece's Conversion Assets rows and Craft, each with its points over its maximum, its colour marker on the same row scale, and a few-word note. No criteria checklist, no ✅ ⚠️ ❌ list and no tally line anywhere in the report; those belong to the single-section audit.
- Get to the point fast. Imagine a copy coach saying "these are the few things that would move the needle most on this page." Surface the handful of core areas — however many there genuinely are (could be 1, could be 7) — that, if fixed, could actually lift conversions.
- Write them as a **bulleted list, each bullet led by a bolded insight**, then a sentence or two making it concrete and page-specific (name the section, name the move). No generic observations.
- Keep the ideas **distinct** — each bullet a different lever, no two saying the same thing in different words. This is the high-level "get it" section; the line-by-line detail and the small fixes live below, not here. (There is no separate "single biggest lever" line — the biggest lever is simply the first, strongest bullet in this list.)
- End with one line telling the reader the exhaustive line-by-line follows below.

**2. Section by section, line by line.** The core. Every section in page order with the markup above — improvements first, every note actionable, strong sections carried into the weaker ones they can help. Long by design; do not thin it out, and do not pad it with praise.

**3. Sections worth adding.** Missing-but-relevant sections (❌) have no lines to mark up, so cover them here: name each, where it goes, what it should contain, with a concrete illustrative example. Then, in one short line, list any sections you set aside (➖) and why (so the reader sees they were considered, not forgotten). Skip nothing silently.

**4. Persuasion.** The strategic selling read (see the spec above): is the promise strong enough and is it the biggest outcome the offer can honestly own, are the real objections handled, is the CTA doing its job, and does the page build trust or tip into hype. Push back on the page's core selling choices here — don't assume they're right.

**5. Proof across the page.** The distribution + synthesis read, made actionable, naming the specific proof elements to add (see the spec above).

**6. Flow.** The section-order read: one line if it's good, or the current-vs-better bulleted comparison if it's not (see the spec above).

**7. Transitions.** The section-join read, bulleted.

**8. Repetition.** The page-wide repetition read, bulleted.

**9. Pacing.** The pacing read, bulleted, naming exact sections.

**10. Voice.** The one-voice read, bulleted.

**11. Implementation Roadmap.** Turn everything above into an ordered, do-this checklist, ranked by how much each change could move conversions. Open with one or two lines of framing: work top to bottom, the HIGH-impact items are where the real gains are. Then THREE impact buckets, each as **checkboxes** (`- [ ]`), each item a specific, do-able step that names the section and the exact change:
   - **HIGH impact** — the needle-movers: missing near-universal sections, a weak or mis-aimed promise, 🔴 barely-working and the weightier 🟡 sections, and the big opportunities from section 1.
   - **MEDIUM impact** — solid gains: the rest of the 🟡 fixes, reordering proof, tightening a slack section, a stronger or better-placed CTA.
   - **LOW impact** — polish: small line tightenings, 🟢 section tweaks, and optional adds (price justification, sample lesson, guided tour, a tier the product could have).
   **A short "Worth A/B testing" list.** Some high-potential changes are also RISKY — rewriting the headline or lead wholesale, changing the core promise or angle, reordering major sections. These could lift conversions or could do worse, so they should be TESTED, not assumed. List each as an A/B test (the current version vs the new direction) with the exact reasoning for why it's worth testing and why it isn't a safe just-ship change.
   Optional second-part sections never go in HIGH; client journey is not suggested at all.

**12. Closing note, then the close.** A short, warm close, broken into 2 or 3 short paragraphs, never one block, never bullets. This is where encouragement lives: give an honest, encouraging read of where the page stands and name the genuinely good things about it (this is the home for the praise you kept out of the line-by-line). Reiterate the single biggest opportunity in one line so they leave knowing the one thing to do first, and point at the one or two changes worth A/B testing rather than assuming. Then end with the close in SKILL.md under "The close". Keep it warm and short; the structured lists live in the roadmap above, not here.

## Delivery

The report is delivered in chat, in full. No file is written by default and no folder is resolved; SKILL.md's delivery rule governs. If the user asks for a saved copy, save the report as delivered, `deep-audit-YYYY-MM-DD.md` (or `.docx` for Word), name it yourself, and say where it went.

## Before you send (calibration self-check)

Do not return the audit until you can answer yes to all of these. If any is no, go back and fix it — this is the check that separates the exhaustive pass from the rushed one:

- Does **every** section on the page have its own entry with a band and framing, including the small utility ones (timer, logo wall, single-line restatements, each testimonial cluster)? No two sections merged, none skipped.
- Does **every** section carry at least one actionable takeaway? No bare "keep it / no change / this is good" notes anywhere — every note gives the reader something to do.
- Is every note a fix, a borrow-from, an extend or a verify, with no "clean" notes and no line mentioned only to say it is fine?
- Did you keep context-free praise OUT of the line-by-line, and instead carry each genuine strength into the weaker section it can help (by name, with the verbatim example), and save the page-level encouragement for the closing note?
- Was **every** FAQ question judged individually, plus a zoom-out read of what the whole set of questions together reveals?
- Is **every** repeated phrase quoted, counted across the page, and resolved (which one to keep)?
- Is any testimonial flood curated with named standouts, a proposed order, and objection-grouping, not just flagged?
- Did you catch every third-person self-reference in the author's own copy (offer, deliverables, pricing, terms) and give the first-person rewrite, and are all your example fixes written in the piece's own voice?
- Did you leave the repeated Buy Buttons / pricing block alone (NOT flagged as duplication, NOT "shortened to a recap"), while still catching any real defect inside it and noting the fix applies to both copies?
- For any section that's set up in the copy but arrived empty, did you treat it as an unreadable image/embed (named in the "couldn't read" line (SKILL.md)) rather than calling it missing, blank, or a gap, and kept it out of 🔴 and the roadmap?
- If the page offers a higher tier (Platinum/VIP/Pro) in the buy block, did you check for a DEDICATED tier section (why upgrade, who it's for, tier-specific proof), and flag its absence, rather than accepting the price-block bullets as enough?
- For every headline / eyebrow / subheadline / subheading rewrite, did you self-audit it against its doctrine and present an eyebrow + headline + subheadline SET (using cross-section context) rather than one long, clunky line?
- Are the section-order (Flow) and the section-joins (Transitions) handled as separate reads — Flow only building the current-vs-better comparison when the order is actually weak?
- Are Pacing and Voice delivered as BULLETS, not walls of prose?
- Are the biggest opportunities (section 1) specific, page-specific insights, distinct from each other, and free of generic observations?
- Did you judge the PERSUASION — is the promise strong enough (and the biggest outcome the offer can own), are the real objections handled, is the CTA doing its job, is the tone trust-building rather than salesy — and did you PUSH BACK where the page misses the mark instead of taking its claims at face value?
- Does every credibility suggestion name the SPECIFIC proof element to add (a testimonial, a case study with a number, a screenshot, a data point, an expert endorsement, a credential, a media mention), never a vague "add more proof"?
- Is the Implementation Roadmap ranked HIGH / MEDIUM / LOW impact, with the risky changes (wholesale headline/lead/promise rewrites, big reorders) routed to a "worth A/B testing" list with the reasoning, and no item tagged risk-free or safe-to-ship?
- Is the whole thing written to the "impossible to misunderstand" bar — simple words, no jargon beyond common copy terms, no walls of text?

## Rules

- **Honesty / ask-don't-invent (non-negotiable, and it matters MORE here).** More specificity means more hallucination risk. When a rewrite depends on a fact you can't know (a real student story, a product detail, a true customer scenario, a number), make the example clearly illustrative ("something like…, if that's true for you"), never presented as the user's real fact. Stay fully prescriptive on craft, structure, and wording that need no private context. Never invent a specific and present it as real.
- **Improvements first; no context-free praise.** The reader is here to learn what to fix. Default every note to an improvement. A strength only appears when it does a job: it's carried into a weaker section as a model (with the verbatim example), or it's the honest page-level encouragement in the closing note. Never scatter "this is great" notes through the line-by-line — they teach nothing and cost attention.
- **The original's voice.** Every example fix is written the way this piece speaks (`rewrite.md`, sections 3 and 4): its person (I, we, the brand), its register and rhythm. Testimonials stay in the customer's voice. Where the piece speaks as one person, the example fixes speak as that person too, which models the consistency the fix asks for.
- **Self-audit your make-or-break rewrites before you show them (headline, eyebrow, subheadline, subheadings).** These are the highest-leverage lines on the page, and a weak suggestion here undercuts the whole audit. Before presenting one, silently score your draft against that doctrine's criteria (`headline.md`, `subheadline.md`, `subheadings.md`) and revise until it would genuinely score well, do not hand over a first draft. Two failure modes to avoid: (1) **Don't cram the whole promise into one long headline.** The headline's only job is to earn the next line, so propose an **eyebrow (audience call-out) + headline (the hook) + subheadline (the expansion)** as a SET, not one overloaded, clunky sentence. (2) **Use cross-section context**, pull the sharpest specifics, emotions, and audience language from elsewhere on the page into the rewrite instead of writing a generic line. Present the finished set (in first person); you don't need to show the scoring, just the strong result, and one line on why it's built that way if it helps. When you're proposing a wholesale replacement of a headline or lead that already works, present it as a direction to A/B test (route it to the roadmap's "worth A/B testing" list), not a guaranteed win.
- **Never invent feedback.** Every issue and every fix must trace to a doctrine principle or to a guideline in `copy-types.md`, not a guess about what "feels" off. If you can't ground a criticism in the doctrine or in `copy-types.md`, don't raise it. Don't manufacture problems to fill space.
- **Don't take the messaging at face value.** The page's promise, angle, and claims are not automatically right. Where the page misses the mark — a weak or too-small promise, an unhandled objection, a buried benefit, a CTA that doesn't earn the click, a tone that's tipped into hype — challenge it and offer the stronger alternative, rather than only polishing what's already there. Judge whether it actually SELLS, not just whether it reads well.
- **Name the proof, never "add credibility."** Whenever you recommend more credibility or proof, say exactly which proof element fits — a specific testimonial, a case study with a number, a screenshot, a data point, an expert endorsement, a credential, a media mention, a before/after, a logo. Generic "add proof here" is not actionable.
- **Risky changes are A/B tests, not assumptions.** A wholesale headline/lead rewrite, a changed core promise, or a big structural reorder could win or could lose. Never present these as sure things; frame them as A/B tests with the reasoning. Plainly safe changes (more proof, better testimonial order, backing a claim, tightening a wordy line) are simply listed; they carry no risk label of any kind.
- **Plain language, impossible to misunderstand.** Write to a smart five-year-old. Swap every complex word for a simple one. The only jargon allowed is common copywriting terms (headline, hook, lead, proof, objection, testimonial). Aim to make each note impossible to misunderstand, not merely possible to understand — if a sentence could be read two ways, rewrite it.
- **Don't flag missing common visuals.** Assume ordinary visuals (an author photo, section graphics) are present; don't suggest adding them. Reserve visual notes for a specific graphic the doctrine calls for and the copy clearly lacks (for example, a mechanism diagram).
- **Don't call a scaffolded-but-empty section "missing."** If the copy sets a section up (heading + lead-in) but its content isn't in the text you were given, assume it's an image or embed you couldn't read, not absent content. Name it in the "couldn't read" line (SKILL.md); never band it 🔴 or list it as a gap. (Full rule in "Reading the page".)
- **Audience context.** Flag any suggestion that depends on cold vs. warm traffic, and give the call for each.
- **Tone: the coach voice, all the way through.** Honest, warm, specific, "here's what you can improve," never a roadblock, never harsh, and it holds even when a line is genuinely weak. At this depth, a long list of fixes can start to feel demoralizing. Counter that on purpose: stay constructive, keep each note framed as a step forward, and save the genuine encouragement (what's strong about the page) for the closing note so the reader ends on it. The page is worth this much attention because it's close. Follow `resources/coach-voice.md` and reread your draft against its never-say list before sending.
- **Assess and suggest only.** The report never hands back a rewritten page or section; rewrites follow `rewrite.md` when asked, and are offered only in SKILL.md's close.
- **Never fabricate human material** (numbers, testimonials, stories, mechanisms, results). Use placeholders in any example.
- **Truly exhaustive: no silent skips.** Account for every line, bullet, subheading, and FAQ answer: none skipped. Every line is judged; only the lines with something to fix are written up.
- **Format: a hybrid of prose and bullets, and never a wall of text.** Use short PROSE to frame a section (a line or two on what it is and how it lands). Use BULLETS for the specific improvement notes, examples, and lists — including the biggest-opportunities list (section 1), the roadmap checkboxes, and all five cross-page reads (Proof, Flow, Transitions, Repetition, Pacing, Voice). Keep every paragraph to 2–3 lines, sometimes one, never 4–5. The closing note stays prose but broken into short paragraphs. Watch relentlessly for walls of text — they're what tire the reader out.
- **One idea per paragraph, about 50 words at most** (`coach-voice.md`). In the line-by-line, each note is the quoted line, then one short paragraph on what's off, then the fix; a note with several fixes puts them in bullets.
- **Length is correct for this tier.** Do not compress toward the light audit. Keep it navigable with the summary-on-top structure and per-section headings, and never confuse length with padding; every line earns its place. On a short piece, the report is as long as the piece needs: every line still gets its attention, and nothing is padded to reach a length.
- **The page score reflects only in-scope sections.** If the user marks a section not-relevant (or relevant), recompute and show the new number.
- **This skill is new and will iterate.** The line-by-line display may evolve; for now use the quote-the-line-then-note-beneath format.
