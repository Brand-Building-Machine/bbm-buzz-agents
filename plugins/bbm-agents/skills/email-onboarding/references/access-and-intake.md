# Asking for access and documents safely

Onboarding is often the first time a client hands the owner something sensitive: logins, bank statements, employee data, a gate code. How the owner asks sets the tone for the whole relationship. A careful ask reads as professional; "just email me your password" reads as a risk.

## The rules

1. **Never ask for a password by email, text or chat, and never put one in a draft.** Email is stored in many places, forwarded, and searched by anyone who gets into either mailbox. A password that has been emailed should be treated as exposed.
2. **Use the tool's own invite or delegate access first.** Almost every business tool lets the account owner invite another person with their own login: an accountant user, a partner or agency access, a delegate, a collaborator. The client stays the owner, sees who has access, and can remove it in one click. Say so in the email; it reassures them.
3. **No invite option? Use a password manager share.** If the tool truly has one shared login, ask the client to share it through a password manager's secure share feature (most business password managers have one, and many can send a one-time link to someone who doesn't use the same manager). Then ask them to change that password when the work ends.
4. **Documents go through a secure upload, not attachments.** A client portal, a shared folder with access limited to named people, or the owner's secure file request link. Attachments sit in inboxes forever.
5. **Ask for the least access that does the job.** Read-only when the work is reading. Admin only when the work needs it, and say why. Named accounts, never one shared login for the whole team.
6. **Some things never travel by email at all:** full card numbers, full bank account and routing numbers, government ID or tax ID numbers, health information, and physical access codes. Those go through a secure portal, the provider's own system, or a phone call.
7. **Say what happens at the end.** "When we finish, we remove our access and you can check that we did." One line, and it builds trust.

## If a client sends a password anyway

It happens, usually to be helpful. The draft reply should:

- Thank them, then say plainly you'd rather not keep it in email.
- Ask them to change that password once the proper invite is set up, and give them the invite steps.
- Never repeat the password in the reply, and never quote the message that contains it.

Tell the owner too, so they can delete the message from their own mailbox.

## Writing the ask

In the email itself, for each item:

- **What** it is, in the client's words ("your QuickBooks", not "the GL system").
- **How** to share it, with the steps or the menu names, as briefly as possible. Check the steps with the owner; don't guess menu paths for a tool you haven't confirmed.
- **Why** it's needed, in one line, only when it isn't obvious.
- **By when**, as a real date or "before the kickoff".

Number the items. People tick off numbered lists; they skim paragraphs. Keep it to what's needed to start; everything else can wait for the kickoff.

## Intake checklists by service type

Starting points for the owner interview. Offer the relevant list as your recommended answer, then keep only what the owner actually needs to start. Items marked **sensitive** follow rule 6 above.

### Marketing, advertising or web agency

- Goals, target customers, offers, and what worked or didn't before (intake form)
- Ad accounts: partner or agency access through the ad platform's business manager, never a login
- Analytics and tag manager: user invite at the lowest role that works
- Website or online store: a named user account in the platform (editor, not owner, unless the build needs it)
- Domain and DNS: only if the work needs it; ask the client to add records themselves or grant delegate access at the registrar
- Email platform and CRM: user invite
- Brand assets: logo files, fonts, colours, photos, brand guide (shared folder)
- Past reports and current spend
- Who approves work, and how fast they usually reply

### Accountant or bookkeeper

- Accounting software: accountant or advisor invite from inside the software
- Bank and card statements for the agreed period (secure upload; **sensitive**)
- Read-only bank feed connection, set up by the client in the software
- Payroll provider: accountant access from the payroll tool
- Last year's tax return and any open letters from tax authorities (secure upload; **sensitive**)
- Open invoices and bills, and how receipts are collected today
- Entity type, fiscal year end, and the client's outside accountant or tax preparer, if any

### Insurance or employee benefits broker

- Current policies, declaration pages or plan documents, and renewal dates
- Loss runs or claims history from the current carrier (the client usually requests these; give them the exact wording to ask)
- For group benefits: an employee census (**sensitive**; dates of birth and dependants go through the broker's or carrier's secure portal only, never email or spreadsheets attached to email)
- Any health or claims details (**sensitive**; the carrier's or broker's secure system only; the owner confirms their own privacy rules)
- A signed broker of record letter or authorisation, if the owner's process needs one
- The client's HR or finance contact, and who signs off on decisions
- What they like and dislike about their current coverage, in their words

### Consultant or coach

- Goals for the engagement and how the client will judge success (intake form)
- Pre-work: a short questionnaire or assessment, sent with a clear due date
- Relevant documents: current plan, org chart, recent numbers, past reports (shared folder)
- Tools the consultant needs to see: user or guest invites, read-only where possible
- Scheduling: session cadence, time zone, who attends, and how to reschedule
- Anything off-limits or confidential inside the client's business

### Trades and home services

- Site address, and the best times to be on site
- Access: who will be home, parking, pets, and any gate or lock-box codes (**sensitive**; by phone or text to the crew lead on the day, never in a group email)
- Photos of the area and any existing problems (reply with photos or a shared folder)
- Measurements, plans or previous work records, if the client has them
- Permits, landlord or HOA approvals, and who is getting them
- Colour, material or fixture choices and the deadline for each decision
- How the client wants updates while the job is running (text, call, email)
