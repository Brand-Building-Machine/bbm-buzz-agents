# Cold email copy

How to write the brief, the first line, the sequence and the subject lines, and how to score a draft before the owner sees it. The shared rules in `../email-sequences/references/writing-rules.md` still apply; this file adds what is specific to cold email.

## Principles

- **Peer, not vendor.** Write like someone in their world who noticed something, not like a company selling to them. Contractions, short sentences, no company-speak.
- **About them, three to one.** For every sentence about the owner or their business, three about the reader, their situation or their problem.
- **The research is the personalization.** A first name and a company name merged into a template is not personal. A true, specific reason for writing to this person is.
- **One email, one job, one ask.** The reader should be able to reply in five words or fewer ("yes", "not now", "talk to Jo").
- **50-90 words.** 125 is the hard limit the checker enforces; going over 90 needs a reason.
- **Honest beats clever.** If there is no real hook, the email says plainly why the owner is writing. A fake-warm opener reads as a mail merge in the first line and burns trust.
- **Rotate the value.** Every offer is one of: save time, make money, save money, reduce risk. If email 1 led with one and got no reply, the next email leads with another.
- **Earn the meeting.** Early emails ask for interest or a confirmation, not a 30-minute call.

## The first line

The first line decides whether the rest gets read. It is about them, it is true, and it connects to the reason for the email.

**The personalization test:** delete the first line. If the email still makes exactly the same sense, the first line was decoration. A good opener leads straight into why you are writing.

Hook strength, strongest first (use the strongest one you can source):

1. **Buying signal.** They are hiring for the job the owner does, opening a location, growing, changing systems, or facing a new rule or deadline. "Saw you're hiring a billing coordinator who also keeps the books."
2. **Shared connection or client.** A real person who knows both sides. Name them in the first line, and only with that person's OK.
3. **Specific knowledge.** Something the owner genuinely knows about their business or their type of business, from experience or a public page they pointed to. "Your booking page only takes new patients by phone."
4. **Comparable result.** A real result for a similar client. "We just finished the same cleanup for another three-chair practice."
5. **Honest cold.** No hook. Say who the owner helps and ask a sharp question. "I help small dental practices get their books off the dentist's desk. Is that still yours?"

First-line strategies to offer the owner (pick 2-3 that fit, recommend one):

| Strategy | When it fits | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| **Signal** | A dated, public trigger exists | "Saw <what they did>." then why that matters |
| **Problem spotted** | A public page shows a gap the owner fixes | "Noticed <specific thing on their site/listing>." |
| **Whole offer** | No personal hook, but the offer is clear and self-selecting | The offer as a question: "Who does the books at your practice right now?" |
| **Situation question** | The role almost always has this problem | "When a claim gets denied, who chases it?" |
| **Right person** | Unsure who decides | "Are you the right person for <area>, or is someone else?" |

Never: open with a compliment about their business, open with who the owner is, claim to have read something you have not, or reference "my last email".

## Structures

Pick the one that fits the hook. All of them end in one low-friction question.

**Observation, problem, proof, ask.** You noticed something; it usually means a problem; the owner has fixed it for someone similar; interested?
> Saw you're hiring a billing coordinator who also keeps the books. That's a hard mix to find. We do the books for 14 dental practices, so your hire only has to be good at claims. Worth a look?

**Trigger, insight, ask.** Something changed for them; here is what that usually causes; want help with it?
> Congrats on the second location. The part most owners don't see coming is two sets of payroll and supplier bills in one set of books. Is that sorted already?

**Question, value, ask.** A pointed question about their situation, one line on what the owner does, a soft ask.
> Who reconciles the card processor at your practice each month? We take that off the dentist's evenings. Worth a conversation?

**Referral or right person.** Used when you do not know who decides, or as the last email.
> If someone else looks after the books at your practice, who should I talk to?

**Useful thing, no pitch.** One genuinely useful tip or resource, offered with nothing asked except "want it?". Often the email that gets the reply.

**Ideas inside a box.** When suggesting ideas for their business, only suggest things the owner actually offers. List the owner's 3-5 real services first, then write 2-3 ideas that each use one of them. An idea the owner cannot deliver is a false promise.

## The sequence

| # | Day | Thread | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0 | New | The hook, one value, one question. No link, no attachment, no image. |
| 2 | 3 | Same thread as 1 | A different value (rotate), a new first line. Never "following up". |
| 3 | 7 | New thread, new subject | Something useful, or a new angle with real proof. |
| 4 | 11 | Either | Clean close. Say it is the last note; ask for the right person or leave the door open. |

- Three or four emails is the norm; five is the hard cap. After the last one, stop for good.
- Any reply stops the sequence for that person. So does a bounce or an opt-out.
- In the draft file, a same-thread email says `Subject: same thread as Email 1`. The owner sends it as a reply to their own email 1. Never put `Re:` on an email that is not a real reply.
- Each email must make sense to someone who never read the others.
- Send on weekdays, in the recipient's morning. Skip Monday mornings and Friday afternoons.

## Calls to action: the ladder

Start at the bottom. Move up only after they reply.

1. **Confirm:** "Is that still the case?" / "Is month-end still on your plate?"
2. **Interest:** "Worth a look?" / "Worth a conversation?"
3. **Resource:** "Want the one-page version?" / "Should I send it over?"
4. **Right person:** "Who handles this at <company>?"
5. **Time:** "Worth 15 minutes next week?" Only in a later email, or once they have replied.

A booking link belongs in a reply to someone who said yes, not in email 1.

## Subject lines

- 2-5 words, plain, lowercase is fine. It should look like something a colleague could send, and it must be honest about what is inside.
- Tie it to the email's one idea: "your billing coordinator role", "three numbers for a dental practice", "month-end at <company>".
- A new subject for each new thread. Vary them; four copies of one subject read as nagging.
- Never: `Re:` or `Fwd:` on a first email, "quick question", "curious", "following up", the prospect's first name as bait, urgency, or anything the email does not deliver.

## Score before showing (0-100)

Gates first. Any of these is an automatic fail, whatever the score: an invented fact, result, client or contact; fake familiarity; a fake `Re:`/`Fwd:`; a checker error.

| Dimension | Points | Full marks when |
|---|---|---|
| Why them | 25 | The first line is specific, true, sourced and connected to the reason for writing |
| Value and proof | 20 | One clear value in their terms, backed by a real result or honestly without one |
| About them | 15 | At least three sentences about them for each one about the owner |
| Ask | 15 | One question they can answer in five words or fewer |
| Length and plainness | 15 | 50-90 words, no filler, reads aloud in under 20 seconds |
| Subject | 10 | Short, honest, specific to this email |

85 or more: show the owner. 70-84: another pass. Under 70: start again from the hook.

## The three-pass cut

1. **Cut the throat-clearing.** Greeting filler, "I wanted to", "I was wondering", hedges like "perhaps" and "I think". Start with the point.
2. **Compress.** Turn clauses into sentences. "We built a service that can take care of X" becomes "We do X." Delete most "that" and "which".
3. **Cut the adjectives.** Keep numbers and specifics; delete "great", "powerful", "seamless". Then read it aloud once more.

## Example sequence

A fictional business: Northwind Bookkeeping does monthly books for dental practices. The owner, Sam, said: "we do the monthly books for 14 dental practices" and "clients get a closed month and a one-page summary by the 10th". The prospect's job ad came from the practice's careers page, which Sam sent over. Everything in the emails traces to those.

```markdown
---
kind: cold
business: Northwind Bookkeeping
audience: Owners of independent dental practices with 1-3 dentists, US
trigger: Practice posts a job ad for a billing or front-desk bookkeeping role
goal: A reply saying a short call is worth it
exit: Any reply, bounce or opt-out
format: plain
status: draft
---
# Dental practices hiring for billing

## Email 1: Name the hire and offer another way
Send: Day 0, Tuesday morning
Subject: your billing coordinator role
Alt subject: billing hire at Harbor Family
Preview: A thought before you make the hire
CTA: Reply if it's worth a look

Hi Dr. Lee,

Saw Harbor Family Dentistry is hiring a billing coordinator who also keeps the books.

That's a hard mix to find. Most people who are great at insurance claims aren't bookkeepers, and the other way round.

We do the monthly books for 14 dental practices, so the person you hire only has to be good at claims.

Worth a look before you fill the role?

Sam
Northwind Bookkeeping, 200 Example Street, Springfield
Not relevant? Say so and I won't email again.

## Email 2: The owner's evenings
Send: Day 3, morning
Subject: same thread as Email 1
Preview: Month-end shouldn't be your job
CTA: Reply yes or no

Hi Dr. Lee,

In most small practices I talk to, month-end lands on the dentist. Reconciling the card processor, payroll and supplier bills after the last patient leaves.

Our clients get a closed month and a one-page summary by the 10th. They review it, they don't build it.

Is month-end still on your plate?

Sam
Northwind Bookkeeping, 200 Example Street, Springfield
Not relevant? Say so and I won't email again.

## Email 3: Something useful, no pitch
Send: Day 7, morning
Subject: three numbers for a dental practice
Preview: Worth checking every month, whoever does your books
CTA: Reply if you want the one-page version

Hi Dr. Lee,

Three numbers worth a look every month, whoever does your books:

Collections against production. A widening gap usually means claims are stuck.
Insurance receivables older than 90 days. Those get harder to collect every week.
Supplies as a share of production. It creeps up quietly.

I have a one-page sheet that tracks all three. Want it?

Sam
Northwind Bookkeeping, 200 Example Street, Springfield
Not relevant? Say so and I won't email again.

## Email 4: Close out and ask for the right person
Send: Day 11, morning
Subject: who handles the books at Harbor Family
Preview: Last note from me
CTA: Reply with a name, or no

Hi Dr. Lee,

I'll leave it here after this one.

If someone else looks after the books at Harbor Family, who should I talk to? If the timing's wrong, no problem at all.

Sam
Northwind Bookkeeping, 200 Example Street, Springfield
Not relevant? Say so and I won't email again.
```
