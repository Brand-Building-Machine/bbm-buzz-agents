# Post formulas

Pick **one** formula per post. Blending two weakens both. Pick by what the post should earn first, then by the material the owner actually has. Every formula needs a real specific from the owner or the story bank; if the specific doesn't exist, pick another formula or ask for it.

Method adapted from sergebulaev/linkedin-skills (MIT), rewritten for small-business owners.

## Pick by goal

| The post should earn | Use |
|---|---|
| **Comments** (conversation, reach) | Dated fact, Year-over-year, Contrarian with receipts, One-variable test, Both answers fail, Permission slip, Open loop |
| **Reposts** (quotable, shareable) | Named thanks, Diverging paths, Era ending, Risk list |
| **Likes** (warmth) | Cold open, Good news in disguise, Status strip |
| **Saves** (reference value) | The ledger, Free playbook, Plain-English explainer, Story meets evidence |

## Pick by material

| The owner has... | Use |
|---|---|
| Real numbers: costs, results, time | The ledger |
| A change in how they work vs a year ago | Year-over-year |
| A mistake or loss with a date on it | Dated fact |
| An opinion most of their industry disagrees with, plus evidence | Contrarian with receipts |
| A method they could charge for | Free playbook |
| A story with stakes | Cold open |
| Jargon their customers don't understand | Plain-English explainer |
| Two jobs, clients or approaches that differed in one way | One-variable test |
| A thank-you owed to a named person (with permission) | Named thanks |

## The formulas

**1. The ledger** (saves). Open with an odd, exact number and what it covers. Then the line items, real and unrounded. Then what the total replaced or what surprised them. Close with a plain takeaway.
> "$11,240. That's what one misquoted policy cost a client of ours last spring." Then the breakdown.
Never with made-up or rounded numbers. This is the strongest opener when the numbers are real.

**2. Year-over-year** (comments). "A year ago I [old belief or habit]. Today I [new one]." Then the moment that changed it, with a date, and what it costs or saves now.

**3. Dated fact** (comments). Open with a specific, uncomfortable fact stated flat: "We lost our biggest account on 14 February." No "let me be honest", no "confession:". The fact carries it. Then what happened next and what they'd tell someone in the same spot.

**4. Contrarian with receipts** (comments, reposts). State what their industry says, then why it's wrong, with evidence: a number, a case, a date. One contrast only. Close with where they draw the line, so it reads as conviction, not a rant.

**5. Era ending** (reposts). "[Old way] is over for [who]." Then what replaced it and the proof. Use only with real evidence; never about LinkedIn itself.

**6. Risk list** (reposts). Four or five short parallel lines, each a real risk with a specific ("Your carrier can change the plan in 30 days."). Then the reframe in a plain sentence, then what they do about it. The repetition is the post's one device; no other contrast.

**7. Free playbook** (saves). "People charge [real price range, if the owner knows it] for this. Here it is free." Then the steps, numbered, each concrete enough to use today.

**8. Open loop** (comments). A specific surprising result in line 1, paid off within two lines. Never the teaser phrases ("what nobody tells you"). If it can't pay off fast, use another formula.

**9. Cold open** (likes). Start mid-scene: "The phone rang at 6:40 on a Sunday." No warm-up. Then what was at stake, what they did, and one line on what it taught them.

**10. Permission slip** (comments). For an audience that's discouraged: "If you [struggle], you're not behind." Only with a real, dated fact behind the reassurance, or it's filler.

**11. Good news in disguise** (likes). Open like bad news ("We're changing how we quote."), reveal it's an upgrade for the reader. Use for real policy or process changes.

**12. Named thanks** (reposts). Thank a named person for something specific they did, with the detail only an insider would know. Get their okay before naming them.

**13. Plain-English explainer** (saves). Take one term customers nod along to but don't understand ("deductible vs out-of-pocket max") and explain it like to a smart friend, with one real example.

**14. Status strip** (likes). A senior or successful owner shares an ordinary, human moment plainly. Warmth, not humility theatre.

**15. One-variable test** (comments). Two cases that were the same except for one thing, and the outcome split. "Same size company, same carrier. One renewed at +4%, one at +19%. The difference was..."

**16. Both answers fail** (comments). Name the two obvious answers to a problem, show why each fails with a specific, then give the third option. This is the post's one contrast.

**17. Story meets evidence** (saves). Something they noticed in their own work, then the numbers that show it's not just them (from a named, checked source or their own records).

**18. Diverging paths** (reposts). Two approaches that look the same today and end up far apart in a year. Usually the closing move of a longer post, one quotable line.

**19. Self-proving** (comments). The post is the test: "I'll answer every question in the comments today" or a public commitment. Only if the owner will actually do it.

## Rules that apply to every formula

- **Line 1 is the hook.** A specific number or fact beats a question. Never open with a question; move it to the end.
- **"How I" beats "How to".** First-person experience over generic advice.
- **The first line must make sense alone** and fit before "...see more" on a phone (about 140 characters).
- **One contrast and one list of three per post, at most.** Zero "The result?", "Plot twist:", "Here's the thing".
- **Every abstract line earns its place with a specific** within two lines: a date, a dollar figure, a name, a percentage.
- **Close** with a specific question drawn from the post, or a clean last line, or a P.S. with a real follow-up. Never "What do you think?", "Agree?", "Tag someone", or "comment X and I'll send it".
- **Layout:** one or two sentences per paragraph, blank line between. That's normal LinkedIn formatting.
- **Links go in the first comment,** not the post. Hashtags: none to three, specific ones.
